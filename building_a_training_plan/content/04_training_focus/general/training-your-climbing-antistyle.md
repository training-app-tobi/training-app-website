# Training Your Climbing Antistyle

**Type:** Planning, Goals & mindset · **Trains:** Endurance · **For:** Advanced

**TL;DR:** Confronting your weaknesses head-on is one of the most effective ways to fuel long-term growth; as you become more advanced, the return on investing time into your strengths diminishes. Keep the same training framework and change project selection: about 70% of your projects in your antistyle, 30% in your own style to keep morale high. Getting better at your antistyle expands the range of routes and crags where you can perform at a high level.

## Why confront your weaknesses
- Confronting your weaknesses head-on is one of the most effective ways to fuel long-term growth in any sport.
- As you become more advanced, the return on investing time into your strengths diminishes.
- Your "antistyle" is your specific weakness: the types of climbs that feel most unnatural or difficult for you.

## Example antistyle
- Example antistyle: long, resistance-based routes that demand sustained aerobic output, with minimal or no true rests, where the pump builds and stays for 10 or 20 minutes or more.
- The opposite natural style: shorter, more anaerobic, power-endurance-based lines.
- Antistyle routes can take twice the effort and time to send compared with routes of the same grade in the natural style.
- Avoiding your antistyle (gravitating to "your style" routes you can send more quickly and confidently) leaves a glaring hole in your skill set: you may look successful on paper while your overall capability is imbalanced.

## Changing the approach
- The training plan doesn't need an overhaul: keep the same framework, always pushing intensity as you get stronger.
- The key change is project selection.

| Share of outdoor sport projects | Route type | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| About 70% | Your antistyle (e.g. long, sustained routes with few rests), chosen deliberately | Close the gap in your skill set |
| The remaining 30% | "Your style" | Keep morale high with more regular successes |

- Keep the split up over a long period (e.g. 18 months).
- Expect it to test patience, discipline and ego: projecting routes several grades below your peak because they aren't your style is frustrating.
- Example: a 9a (5.14d) resistance testpiece can take weeks, while other 9a routes in your own style go in a few sessions.

## The payoff
- After such a period you can be nearly as capable on endurance-heavy climbs as on power-endurance routes, with a large gain in confidence.
- Getting better at your antistyle expands the range of routes and crags where you can perform at a high level.
- A more solid aerobic base gives a foundation for future strength and performance gains: you can train harder, recover better and take on more demanding climbs across a broader range of styles.
- Identify your own antistyle and start addressing it with the 70/30 split: most of your projecting effort on your weaknesses, with routes that suit your strengths sprinkled in to stay motivated.
