# Setting Training Goals

**Type:** Goals & mindset, Planning

**TL;DR:** Training goals keep you motivated through a season of training. Set the goal in a measurable exercise or movement that contributes to your climbing goal, lay out progressions on a timescale working backwards from the goal, and move to a harder progression only after two consecutive sessions at the previous one. Track climbing, training and outside stresses to stay objective, and don't set too many conflicting goals.

## Why set training goals
- Maintaining motivation during a season of training is one of the most important factors in progressing. Setting training goals is a great way to stay focused, and may be especially useful when access to facilities is restricted.

## Make it specific
- The aim of a training goal is to keep you motivated for your training as a whole, so choose a training goal that is in line with your overall climbing aim or goal.
- Match the goal type to the climbing:

| Climbing aim | Training goal type |
|---|---|
| Become stronger at bouldering | An endurance-based training goal probably isn't for you |
| A 50 m sport route or a big wall | A power-based goal may not be the most applicable |

- Set the goal in a training exercise or movement that will contribute to your performance in the climbing you are working towards.
- With a very specific climbing goal (a specific boulder or sport climb), set a training goal specific to a crux movement or to the style of the climb.

## Make it measurable
- Part of what makes training goals motivating is that progress is measurable. Climbing is a complex mixture of strength, movement, coordination and focus, so climbing performance is hard to measure over a shorter period of time, which is partly why motivation can start to wane.
- Measurable climbing performance also fluctuates with the style or the setting at the gym. A fixed board (e.g. a home board) avoids this and is great for setting goals, e.g. choose a project boulder and aim to be doing triples on it by the end of the winter.
- Choose a more basic, but climbing- and goal-specific exercise so you can easily measure progress. Fingerboard, weighted or bodyweight exercises are easy go-tos, where time, reps or load can be measured.
- For exercises like front levers, use video to track form as well as time held.

## Set out progressions and work to a timescale
- Having something to aim for is motivating. Choose a goal with easily accessible progressions and set short-term goals to hit over the training season.
- Work backwards from the actual training goal; this also helps you see how realistic the goal is.
- Units of progression vary with the goal, e.g. 1 second a week or 0.5 kg a week.
- Progress isn't always linear: plan to move on to a harder progression only after completing two consecutive sessions at the previous progression, e.g. 1 more rep every 2 weeks. This lets you consolidate and reduces the risk of injury.

## Remain objective
- You won't always hit the mini goals on your timescale. Training goals are set to be challenging and motivating, not a source of performance anxiety or frustration: keep it light-hearted and remember that the "real" goal is the climbing.
- To stay objective, monitor progress across both climbing and training exercises, plus anything else that could have an impact, such as busy weeks at work or other stresses.
- Your training goal is a personal challenge: stay focused on it rather than comparing yourself to others.

## Don't have too many conflicting goals
- **Warning:** More training goals does not equal better: numerous training goals will be overwhelming and may become hard to schedule.
- **Warning:** If you set several training goals, check whether they conflict in terms of adaptation, as this could hamper progress and make them harder to achieve, e.g. trying to do 1-5-9 (campus) and run a 4-minute mile.
- For variety, a couple of training goals can be a good way to go, especially if they contribute to the same climbing goal: you train a greater variety of exercises, the week is more interesting, and you avoid too many repetitions of the same exercise.

## Example set of training goals
- Overall goal: get better at roof cracks for a specific climb and improve finger strength (a relative weakness). So: training goals specific to the climb plus one for general progression.

| Goal | Target | Progression and tracking |
|---|---|---|
| Crack | Climb 4 laps of an indoor crack training project (parallel strips of wood attached to a roof) | 1 lap, then 1 lap 4 times in a session, then 2 laps, and so on |
| Front lever | Hold an assisted front lever for 10 seconds with good form (an important position for roof climbing) | Use a heavy resistance band to work the position while far from a full front lever. Film each session to check form while increasing the time held. Currently at 5 seconds: add 1 second every 2 weeks, and improve form for the time held in the other weeks |
| Finger strength | A 10-second two-arm hang with +20 kg in half crimp | Gradual loading approach |
