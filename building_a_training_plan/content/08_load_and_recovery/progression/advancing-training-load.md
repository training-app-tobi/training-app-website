# Advancing Training Load

**Type:** Planning, Principles · **Trains:** General strength, Finger strength, Pulling strength, Power endurance · **For:** stalled progress, Older, injured or returning

**TL;DR:** You get better at doing harder things by doing harder things, and which factors you increase, when and by how much determines your training outcomes. Training can be advanced by progressing the load, the volume or the density, decreasing rest between like sessions, adding assistance exercises or stacking sessions. Advancing load is a pivotal choice and should be the last intervention considered: ask whether you really need more training, and when you add it, do it carefully and incrementally and keep good notes on what you do and the true results.

## Who should do what

| Situation / level | Do this |
|---|---|
| Happy where you are | No need to try harder |
| Older athletes; people coming back from injury | 2-4-6 ladders at 75% of max: a very safe rep range |
| Ramping up to bouldering comps or a destination bouldering trip | Density bouldering |
| Can't manage more than a few hard moves in a row | Density bouldering |
| Tracking performance in like sessions, with the discipline to back off when you go flat | Decreasing rest between like sessions can be a useful way to jumpstart progress |
| Going flat on a workout (e.g. 3 × 5 pull-ups), or a hangboard session no longer gives the results it did earlier in the cycle | Consider adding assistance exercises rather than swapping the whole workout |

## Why progress, and what to change

- The way to get better at doing harder things is to do harder things. Constantly playing it safe, building volume and avoiding discomfort means doing the same routes over and over.
- Structured training (hangboard, weights, campus board, etc.) is easier to quantify, and so to progress, than climbing at the crag or bouldering in the gym, where so many factors are involved that it can be hard to tell whether you are improving at all.

| You can | Next target |
|---|---|
| Do 1 pull-up | 2 pull-ups |
| Hang a 25 mm edge | 20 mm: an excellent new target |
| Bench press 61 kg | Get to the gym more often; aiming to add load is a good starting point |

- At some point progress just stops, and that is the best case: often it goes backwards for no known reason. No reason to despair: training regularly, you should be able to keep getting better and stronger for a long time.
- Which factors you increase, when, and by how much determines your training outcomes.

## Progress the load

- Asking the muscles to do more work is the fundamental principle behind getting stronger.
- Progressing load (weight): keep the sets and reps the same and increase the weight you move.

Example, deadlift at 5 sets of 2 reps:

| Stage | Sets and loads |
|---|---|
| New lifter, first few weeks | Same load on all 5 sets, adding 2.3 kg to the bar each workout: 102 kg on all sets, then 104.5 kg, then 106.5 kg, and so on |
| After several cycles the work adds up and gains level off | One workout: 156.5 kg on the first 4 sets and only 1 rep on the last set. Next workout's goal: 156.5 kg on all sets |
| From this stopping point on, progress won't be easy anymore | Stick to 10 total reps (the original 5 × 2), even if that takes a couple of singles. Progress the load one set per workout |

Set-by-set progression (kg, sets of 2):

| Workout | Set 1 | Set 2 | Set 3 | Set 4 | Set 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Next session | 156.5 | 156.5 | 159 | 156.5 | 156.5 |
| Then | 156.5 | 156.5 | 159 | 159 | 156.5 |
| Then | 156.5 | 159 | 159 | 159 | 156.5 |
| Then | 156.5 | 159 | 159 | 159 | 159 |
| Then | 159 | 159 | 159 | 159 | 159 |

- Next session: a couple of sets to get "lit" before trying the new max (159 kg), then a conservative last two sets at 156.5 kg.
- After 159 kg on all five sets, start on 161 kg with the same progression.
- It's OK not to get all the reps in the planned sets: a few reduced sets are normal when pushing strength and nothing to worry about. If you are not injured, always have the discipline to get all the reps, however many sets it takes.
- This holds for almost all strength training, whether you do 2 reps per set or 6.
- If you are aiming for higher reps, add weight to the bar and force yourself back into a respectable rep range.

## Progress the volume

- Even the strongest stop advancing the load they can push. Almost all athletes, almost all of the time, want to generate more force or generate it more quickly, and in any program progress in that ability inevitably stalls; the plateau can last months or years.
- The body also responds well to more volume at slightly submaximal loads: more work per session, per week or per any other period can increase work capacity and also maximal strength.
- **Warning:** in finger strength training it is not uncommon for the muscles' force to outpace the strength gains of the connective tissue: grip strength rises, the fingers don't keep pace, and the result is a tendon strain or pulley injury. Volume at slightly submaximal loads is especially useful in this situation.
- Loads as low as 60% of max can really increase strength if training is regular and of high enough volume. So even a bodyweight-only hangboard program, or exercises done for more than the standard 3–5 reps, can really work, with much lower injury risk.

### Ladders

- Ladders are the preferred way to add strength through volume. A ladder is a repeated cycle of reps (or static hold times) at the same load across all sets.

| Scheme | Reps per set | Load |
|---|---|---|
| Instead of | 3 sets of 3 | About 85% of max |
| Ladder | 1-2-3 | About 70–75% of max |
| Next session: add reps, not load | 1-2-3-1 (one more rep) or even 1-2-3-1-2 (three more reps) | Same |
| 2-4-6 ladders: big strength gains | 2-4-6 | 75% of max |

- In one 6-week period, a woman stuck at a 90.5 kg bench press max reached 101 kg while training at only 70.5 kg.
- Ladders are also the basis of a hangboard ladder program that seems "too easy and boring" to possibly work. Think of ladders as a savings account: easy, slow progress and, if you stick with them, great long-term rewards.

### Informal volume progression

To improve your pull-up, simply do more of them (the basis of "grease the groove" programs):

| Element | Rule |
|---|---|
| Exercise | A hard one: no more than 4 reps per set |
| Start | About 6–8 total reps |
| Progression | Add just 1 rep per session |
| Length | A 4–6 week series |

Example: 3 good pull-ups, trained twice a week (reps per set):

| Week | Session 1 | Session 2 |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 3-3-2 | 3-3-2-1 |
| 2 | 3-3-3-1 | 3-3-3-2 |
| 3 | 3-3-3-2-1 | 3-3-3-3-1 |
| 4 | 4-4-3-3 | 4-4-4-3 |

- Once volume has progressed to about double the starting volume, cycle back and try to increase load again to keep progressing.

## Progress the density

- There is more to training than maximal strength: sometimes you need strength endurance (exerting high loads for longer) or capacity (doing work over several intervals or even days). One of the best ways to build these on a limited schedule is to increase session density: how much work you do in a given timeframe.

### Circuits

| Element | Rule |
|---|---|
| Exercises | 1–3 that you could do for about 10 reps |
| Format | 5 reps of each in a circuit, for a fixed amount of time |
| Progression | Over the training cycle, keep the load and the time the same and try to do more sets |

Weight-room example: 5 rack squats, 5 pull-ups and 5 levers in sequence for 15 minutes.

| When | Sets |
|---|---|
| Session 1 | Three sets of each |
| Session 2 | Goal: one extra set of squats |
| Session 3 | Goal: one more set of pull-ups |
| By session 4 | 4 full circuits |

### Density bouldering

A preferred climbing-specific drill:

| Element | Rule |
|---|---|
| Time | 30 minutes on a timer |
| Problems | As many mid-level problems (around your onsight grade) as you can |
| Score | Add up the V-grades of all problems completed and divide by the session time in minutes |

- Keeping the same session duration for a whole training cycle is recommended, but density can still be improved with slightly varied times:

| Session | V-sum | Time | Density |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 35 | 30 min | 1.17 |
| 2 | 38 | 30 min | 1.27 |
| 3 | 34 | 26 min | 1.31 |
| 4 | 46 | 35 min | 1.31 |

- After as few as 5 sessions, most climbers show a noticeable improvement in strength endurance.
- Most importantly, the session structure makes performance easy to track, so it's clear when things are getting better.

## Decrease rest between like sessions

- **Warning:** this is almost universally the most-abused and least useful way of progressing training, suggested only with hesitation.

Example: bouldering twice a week for 90 minutes, flat for 6 months.

| Step | Sessions per week | Session length |
|---|---|---|
| Now | 2 | 90 min (180 min a week) |
| Add a third weekly session to force adaptation, starting at the same total volume | 3 | 1 hour |
| If your elbows don't hurt and your fatigue doesn't go up | 3 | 70 min, then 80 min, eventually 90 min |

- Weekly volume does rise, but what really changes is less rest between similar sessions. Weekly volume can be raised by any means, such as adding a kettlebell session; decreasing rest between like sessions is different because it tries to fast-forward specific adaptations.
- **Warning:** use this method sparingly and conservatively. After a couple of months on it, stop and try something new.

## Add assistance exercises

- Sometimes a little more non-specific work added to the primary exercises restarts progress. This can simply come from a slight change of angle, a change in the sequence in which the muscles fire, or more training volume.
- Integrative exercises can help too: usually multi-joint compound movements that include, to some degree, the primary pattern you are trying to improve.

| Primary workout | Add |
|---|---|
| Pull-up workout | A couple of sets of dumbbell rows or TRX rows |
| Hangboard session | Some heavy finger rolls |
| Overhead press | Turkish get-up (integrative): an excellent assistance exercise |

- Start by adding just a couple of sets per session and look for progress in the primary exercise. If there is none after 2–3 weeks, try a different exercise or add a bit more volume.

## Stack sessions

- **Warning:** session stacking is an advanced programming decision that can lead to overtraining or injury if abused.
- Do a session, recover incompletely, then do a second session (in some cases even a third).
- The incomplete recoveries take you deeper into fatigue: the idea is to get much more fatigued than any single session allows.

| Session | Readiness at the start | Intensity and movement |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Full | The most intense |
| 2 | About 90% | Less intense, with simpler movement, to reduce injury risk |
| 3 (in some cases) | 70–80% | |

- Many climbers already stack without meaning to:

| First session | Followed by |
|---|---|
| Tuesday bouldering | Wednesday route laps |
| Saturday redpointing | Sunday mileage |

- The result of stacking, intended or not, is an increase in work capacity.
- Stacking is a simple intervention that needs much less focus and motivation than going harder or longer in a single session.

## Before adding more training

- Advancing load is a pivotal choice: don't add even more hard training without thinking carefully about what you need as an athlete. Do you really need more training? Is there a better way to get the same result?
- Advancing load is too often seen as the only way to improve, when it should really be the last intervention considered.
- When it is time to add more training, do it carefully and incrementally, and keep good notes on what you do and what the true results are.
