# Four Ways to Progress Endurance Training

**Type:** Principles, Planning · **Trains:** Endurance, Power endurance, Power

**TL;DR:** There are four ways to increase the load or difficulty of an endurance (interval) session: the difficulty of the climbing, the duration or distance of the intervals, the duration and type of rest, and the number of repetitions. Each way has a profoundly different outcome, so progressing the wrong facet gives results you don't want. Progress only one of the four at a time, and add rest as the load grows.

## The four ways and what they train
There are several ways to increase the load or difficulty of an endurance (interval) session, and this applies to progressing any kind of training. Each way has a profoundly different outcome; progressing the wrong facet gives results you don't want.

| Way to progress | What it trains |
|---|---|
| 1. Difficulty of the climbing | Strength or power |
| 2. Duration or distance of the intervals | Aerobic power: the ability to keep up a power output at a given level. Usually builds muscular endurance or local anaerobic endurance |
| 3. Duration and type of rest | Passive rest: a longer or harder bouldering session. Active rest: may develop capacity a little more, in shorter sessions |
| 4. Number of repetitions | Capacity and long endurance |

## 1. Increase the difficulty of the climbing
- Increasing the difficulty of the climbing builds strength or power.
- It is the number one way for young people to advance, and the number one way for anyone to advance who wants to climb harder grades or send longer routes: the number one way to move forward in any and all climbing is to do more hard climbing.
- **Warning:** You can't just keep adding harder and harder climbing. When you add harder climbs to a session, keep the rests really long, and increase the rest as much as you increase the difficulty. If you pursue harder climbing together with more climbing or less rest, you hit the wall quickly and plateau.

## 2. Increase the duration or distance of the intervals
- Distance = more moves; duration = more time (depends on how you track it).
- Longer work intervals build aerobic power: the ability to keep up a power output at a given level.
- Longer work intervals usually build muscular endurance or local anaerobic endurance.
- Important for route climbing; less important for bouldering, unless you fatigue toward the end of a particular boulder problem.
- Increase intervals slowly, and lengthen the rest along with them; the rest has to grow faster than the interval. Example: a circuit that takes 60 s with 60 s rest may recover fine; increase the circuit to 90 s and just match it with 90 s rest, and you get really tired very quickly.

Work-to-rest ratio for aerobic work, when you want to do a lot of intervals:

| Level | Work:rest |
|---|---|
| Minimum | 1:1 |
| Preferred | 1:2 |
| Upper limit | About 1:4, before it becomes too much rest |

- Example of too much rest: 30 s work, 2 min rest, repeated, could be kept up for a very long time, so it might not be a very effective workout.

## 3. Change the duration and type of rest

| Rest type | Example | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Passive | Sitting on the floor | Lets you do a longer or harder bouldering session |
| Active | Easy climbing between hard climbs | May develop your capacity a little more, but sessions are by necessity shorter |

- When you get really fatigued toward the end of a session, consider adding more recovery rather than decreasing the difficulty or the number of problems.
- Rest-period manipulation (active rest, longer or shorter rests) is usually the last of the four things you want to change.
- **Warning:** Rest in indoor route climbing is often too short. At the crag you might climb 10 min and then rest 30–40 min between climbs; in the gym you might climb 5 min, your partner climbs a few minutes, and you are back on with less rest than normal. You get super fatigued quickly and may think you are getting fit when you are only getting tired in that session, with no long-term fitness gains. Pay attention to both the duration of the activity and the duration of the rest.

## 4. Increase the number of repetitions
- The number of repetitions is directly linked to capacity. Example: if you can do 8 routes in a gym session, increase capacity and long endurance by working up to 9, then 10, then maybe 12 routes.
- Instead of doing longer circuits, do more sets or more total circuits per session: a great way to increase capacity and to get lots of quality climbing per session.
- When adding more to the session, rest plenty between climbs, and rest more and more as you start to get fatigued.
- A common problem in interval training: timing the climbing and timing the rests at fixed values even though physiologically you are not getting back to the same starting line. As the session goes on, add more rest.
- More rest doesn't make you weak, doesn't decrease fitness gains and doesn't make you a lesser climber; it keeps more high-quality movement per unit time.

## Progress one thing at a time
- **Warning:** Don't try to advance more than one of the four at a time. Doing harder problems with shorter rests gets you super fatigued, but the training effect is negligible. Be careful and intentional about the outcome you train for.
