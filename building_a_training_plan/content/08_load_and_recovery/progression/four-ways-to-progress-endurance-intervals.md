# Four Ways to Progress Endurance Intervals

**Type:** Planning, Principles · **Trains:** Endurance, Power endurance, Power · **For:** stalled progress

**TL;DR:** Treat endurance training as energy system development done in intervals, and keep progressing the sessions once your body has adapted. There are four ways to progress any interval: harder climbing, longer intervals, shorter or more active rests, and more repetitions. Each builds a different kind of fitness and has its own cost. Progress slowly: add about one repetition, or no more than about 10% of interval duration, per session.

## Who should do what
| Situation / level | Do this |
|---|---|
| Goal: steady, low-power endurance | Lengthen the intervals: you get better at recovering on the fly and at doing multiple semi-hard moves in a row. This is clearly the way to get better at sustained redpoints or traversing boulders, but it doesn't translate well to doing hard cruxes while redlining |
| New to endurance training | Progressing interval duration is appropriate |
| Boulderer switching to route climbing | Progressing interval duration is appropriate |

## Why sessions must progress
- If you've done ARC training and 4x4s but still fall off just before the anchors, the cause may be that you aren't progressing your sessions, or that you are increasing the difficulty in the wrong way.
- Treat all "endurance" training as energy system development. There is no real "endurance" in single-pitch climbing (unless you are doing something like the Fitz Traverse): every pitch has a finish line and there is only so much time you can spend on it, so all training should focus on performing for limited periods of progressively harder efforts.
- Most energy system work should be interval training. Intervals let you amass more quality hard work than a long, steady-state effort, and climbing itself is broken into periods of hard effort separated by longer rests. Boulder problems, 4x4s, ARC and most other formats are some kind of interval.
- Adaptation: the first time you do a set of 4x4s you'll probably be sore and fatigued for a few days; repeat it and soreness and fatigue are less; after a few more sessions you've fully adapted and your body can do that work. There are many ways to make it hard again, and the fitness you gain differs greatly depending on the method you choose.

## The four ways to progress intervals
Every kind of interval advances the same way.

| Method | What it builds | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| Increase the difficulty of the climbing | Power output; the ability to do very hard moves under varying states of fatigue; the most sustainable long-term fitness | Doesn't directly lead to higher work capacity or more low-end endurance |
| Increase the distance or duration of the intervals | Aerobic power; steady, low-power endurance; recovering on the fly | Power drops as intervals get longer; doesn't translate well to doing hard cruxes while redlining |
| Change the duration and type of the rest period | Ability to recover on-route; aerobic power | Can detrain top-end strength endurance and power |
| Increase the number of repetitions | Energy system capacity: the number of hard tries you can put in per session or climbing day | Can slow the sending rate; trades away the drive to go hard on every try |

## 1. Increase the difficulty of the climbing
- Increasing the difficulty of the climbing in each set over a series of sessions slowly increases power output. This is most obvious in bouldering: doing harder boulder problems than before means you are a better boulderer. Keeping this mindset on longer problems or links develops the ability to do very hard moves under varying states of fatigue.
- Harder sets don't directly lead to higher work capacity or more low-end endurance. To improve those, manipulate the other variables while holding problem difficulty at about the same level.
- Example session format to progress this way, a linked problem: climb one problem, downclimb on open holds, then climb a second problem.
- For long-term performance, difficulty is the most important factor to manipulate: strength and power trickle down eventually, and an interval program built around intensity creates more sustainable fitness.

## 2. Increase the distance or duration of the intervals
- Longer intervals develop more aerobic power.
- Short intervals lean toward power development, long ones toward single-effort endurance. As intervals get longer the body switches primary energy systems, so power drops as you move to the lower-power aerobic system. The drop is not linear: somewhere around the 90-second to 2-minute mark there is a massive decline in "snap".
- **Warning:** Don't increase interval duration by more than about 10% per workout. Round, easy-to-count jumps such as 30 to 45 seconds tend to fatigue you too quickly, and the too-steep improvement curve can shorten the whole training cycle.
- **Warning:** Increase interval duration very little; whole cycles focused on one duration are preferred. Moving from a 60-second interval early in a cycle to a 90-second interval later might completely switch energy systems, which could mean undertraining both.
- Total session load must be considered: as you add time per set, you may have to drop some sets to avoid overreaching.

## 3. Change the duration and type of the rest period
- Decreasing rest, or making it "active", improves your ability to recover on-route. On a route even the rests aren't very restful: short of a sit-down ledge, just hanging out on the jugs is hard work. The skill is raising your threshold levels so that even an engaged position lets you recover: you recruit less muscle and have a higher anaerobic threshold.

Example progression for training route redpoints on boulders:

| Step | What to do | Move on when |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Pick a traverse you have fairly wired and climb it using the natural rests | You can do it several times |
| 2 | Rest on a poor hold instead | You have mastered it |
| 3 | Make the rest harder again | After a few weeks you feel ready for anything |

- **Warning:** Changing rest has a positive effect on aerobic power but can hurt top-end strength endurance and power: maxing out your ability to handle the highly acidic game of resting on-route naturally detrains your top-end values. It is a compromise there is no way around.

## 4. Increase the number of repetitions
- Increasing the number of repetitions of a given interval increases energy system capacity: the number of hard tries you can put in per session or climbing day.
- Add one repetition per session. Example for 4x4s: go to 3x4 + 1x5 rather than straight to 4x5. That is 16 → 17 problems (about a 6% increase) instead of 16 → 20 (a 25% increase).

Progression over a whole training phase:

| Step | Sets |
|---|---|
| 1 | 4x4 |
| 2 | 3x4 + 1x5 |
| 3 | 2x4 + 2x5 |
| 4 | 1x4 + 3x5 |
| 5 | 4x5 |

- Why progress slowly: you train for next year, not next week. It is better to undertrain than to overtrain: undertrained, you still supercompensate, just not as much; overtrained, the body plummets and fights to retain balance. Smaller CNS demands over a longer period bring more acceptance and greater improvement; rushing to get more done leads to uncertainty later.
- **Warning:** More work capacity has a cost: adding more tries to each day has slowed top climbers' sending rate. If you focus too much on sustaining work for long days, you trade away your drive to go hard on every try.

## Long-term
- At the most basic level, any of these increases will raise strength-endurance or muscular endurance. After a few seasons the gains come less easily, and you have to get very smart to push to higher levels.
