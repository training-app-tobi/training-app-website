# Building Work Capacity

**Type:** Planning, Exercise/protocol · **Trains:** Endurance · **For:** limited by work capacity

**TL;DR:** Work capacity is the ability to do a lot of work, day after day, and to repeatedly come back for any level of intensive effort. To build it, add more total time: first more general activity (walking at the crag, easy cardio before and after a gym session), then make that extra time climbing-specific, then intensify it. If it hurts, back off: connective tissue lags behind muscular development.

## Who should do what
| Situation / level | Do this |
|---|---|
| Limited by work capacity | Build capacity with the steps below: probably the fastest way to get more total skill time and climbing time, without having to build a lot of strength, power or endurance |

## What work capacity is
- Work capacity is simply the ability to do a lot of work: to go day after day after day (e.g. climbers who climb 5 days on, or haven't taken a day off in 30 days). People with physical jobs and people who do a lot of endurance training tend to have it.
- Capacity is the ability to repeatedly come back and do any level of intensive effort, in climbing, in the weight room or in general activity.
- Signs that capacity is likely what's holding you back:
  - you feel tapped out by the approach to the crag
  - you only have one good burn on a project, or only one go on a route
  - you get really tired toward the end of a comp
  - you get tired and unpsyched at the end of a planned training session
  - you are wiped out at the end of a day, or can't climb two days in a row
- If you are not running up against capacity, there's no real reason to build more of it (e.g. happy climbing two hours a week, a couple of days).

## Where endurance gets limited
| Limiter | What it is |
|---|---|
| Uptake | Bringing oxygen into the lungs and delivering it to the heart |
| Delivery | The heart pushing that blood to the specific muscles |
| Utilization | The muscles using that blood |

- You can have high uptake and delivery and still be unable to utilize it. Example: a marathon runner probably has great general endurance but will likely fail on a really pumpy route, especially without climbing experience, because the specific ability in the arms isn't developed. Be aware of where the athlete is limited.
- General capacity work addresses uptake and delivery before getting to specific utilization.

## Adding time
- **Warning:** The obvious idea, just doing more climbing, can be too much: you get to the end of a climbing day and can't even do an easy pitch.
- The first step in building capacity is adding more time: more general activity.

| Where / starting point | Add this |
|---|---|
| At the crag | Add 5–10 more minutes of hiking or walking during the climbing day, e.g. walk 5 minutes down the wall and 5 minutes back a couple of times during the day. Climbing 3 days a week and adding 20 minutes of walking on each of those days (carrying the backpack, a slightly longer approach) slowly builds the body's ability to do more work. It is just hiking and focuses on the legs, but that is where to start. |
| In the gym | Do your general warm-up, then 5 minutes of easy activity on cardio equipment (rowing machine, air bike or ski erg), then your normal climbing session; when you finish (skin trashed, feet hurting), go back on the machine for another 5 minutes. That is 10 extra minutes of general activity per session, before and after climbing; training 3 days a week, another 30 minutes of activity per week. |
| Can't exercise at all yet (5 minutes of air bike before and after a bouldering session is unimaginable) | Start with the very simplest level: go for a walk, walk up and down the stairs, walk around the block, but do more. |

- Over time this feels very normal and easy; then take away some of the general work and move it into specific work: weight training (a little more intensive), or a few more boulders or routes. The aim is adding climbing volume, not one more limit boulder: add 1, 2 or 3 boulders at the end of the session.
- Track it: over time you'll see yourself able to do more and more.

## Three-month sequence
| Month | Do this | Until |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Add 30 minutes of general activity per week | About a month, until you are used to it |
| 2 | Make it more specific: from air bike or rowing machine to more boulder problems | About a month, until it is just more climbing |
| 3 | Intensify that climbing, bringing it up to your normal level | — |
| Still need more capacity | Add 30 more minutes of general activity, make it climbing-specific, then intensify; repeat the sequence | Capacity is at the appropriate level for you |

## Adaptation and injury risk
- Adding capacity adds work for the body: lungs and heart adapt, the nervous system adapts and connective tissue adapts, and you slowly get better.
- **Warning:** Connective tissue lags behind muscular development: as you become able to do more pitches muscularly, check whether joints and tissues feel good. If you feel an ache in a finger, elbow or shoulder, continuing on the same path will result in an overuse injury.
- **Warning:** If it hurts, back off. "Does it hurt?" is a yes-or-no question; if yes, go one step back (more air bike, or a walk instead). Continuing to push into something that will cause a soft tissue injury is the opposite of what you are trying to develop.
- Progress is not linear (not "100 pitches a day by the end of this year"): it is an up-and-down cycle, with real awareness of the feedback the body gives.

## The key
- The big key with capacity: more total time per week, per month, per year.
