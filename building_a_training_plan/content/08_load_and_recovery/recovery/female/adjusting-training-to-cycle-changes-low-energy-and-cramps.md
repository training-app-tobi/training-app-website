# Adjusting Training to Cycle Changes, Low Energy and Cramps

**Type:** Planning, Injury & health · **Trains:** Recovery · **For:** Female

**TL;DR:** Use what you learn from tracking your cycle to adapt training: sync deload weeks, adapt sessions, reduce intensity. If your cycle gets acutely shorter or longer with training, see a doctor first, then cut volume and intensity, deload more often and look at your other stresses. Schedule a deload week in the late luteal phase, and sync the days you get cramps with a rest week of gentle climbing, no harness weights and pain relief ready from the onset.

## Who should do what
| Situation / level | Do this |
|---|---|
| Cycle length changes with training (other causes ruled out) | Reduce the volume and intensity of training (fewer sessions per week) and schedule deload weeks more frequently |
| Low energy before your period | Schedule a deload week in the late luteal phase, or overlapping from the late luteal phase into menstruation |
| Cramps | Sync the phase when you get cramps with a rest week: keep moving gently and reduce load on the lower back and abdomen |

## Using what you learn from tracking
- Once you have tracked your cycle, use the information to decide how to adapt your training. Adaptations can take many forms: syncing your deload weeks, adapting specific training sessions, being more aware of your stress levels, introducing more freedom at times of lower motivation, stepping back from structured training, or reducing intensity to help with energy levels.
- Dropping or easing sessions is not "failing" at training. The key in training is consistency over months and years, so simple changes that improve the consistency of your training are likely beneficial in the long term.

## Cycle varies a lot in length, or missing periods
- Low energy availability: when energy expenditure exceeds energy intake. Energy is needed for movement (climbing, training, walking to the shops) and for life processes such as hormone production. When there isn't enough, the body always prioritises movement and down-regulates other life processes such as hormone production.
- Energy intake − energy expended (movement) = energy availability (energy left for life processes).
- The menstrual cycle and periods are governed by hormones, so they can be disrupted when hormone production is down-regulated by low energy availability.
- **Warning:** Low energy availability is a diagnosis by elimination: always seek medical advice if tracking shows significant irregularity in your cycle.

If other reasons are ruled out and your cycle gets acutely shorter or longer when you start training or during training, try these adjustments:

| Adjustment | How it helps |
|---|---|
| Reduce the volume and intensity of training | Fewer sessions per week allow more time for recovery |
| Schedule deload weeks more frequently | Less fatigue builds up |
| Take stock of the other stresses in your life | Training may not have changed, but e.g. a new job with a 32 km commute each way, or high emotional stress, adds load |

- **Warning:** The period is only one piece of the puzzle in being a healthy climber, and low energy availability shows in different ways and is individual. Athletes have kept their period yet had recurring injuries, illnesses or stress fractures. Look at the whole picture when judging overtraining or underfuelling: other markers include fatigue, poor concentration or memory, low mood or motivation, and needing more and more coffee to get going.

## Lower energy and slower recovery in the week before your period
- Lower energy is common in the week before the period; depending on the person, a few days before and a few days into menstruation (the overlap between two cycles). This is the late luteal phase.
- Energy levels are influenced by the role of estrogen and progesterone in the brain, muscle protein synthesis and recovery. Sleep disruption is a common premenstrual symptom, and sleep is integral to recovery, coordination, concentration and mood.
- Schedule a deload week in the late luteal phase (commonly called PMS), or overlapping from the late luteal phase into menstruation.

What to do in this deload week:

| Area | What to do |
|---|---|
| Overall volume | Reduce the overall volume of training to allow more rest |
| Climbing sessions | Use lower-intensity climbing sessions to focus on movement efficiency or being playful with sequences |
| Conditioning exercises | Focus on stability or good form rather than pushing weights and intensity |
| Higher-intensity sessions | Still allowed, but cut them in half: keep them short and fewer. Choose which ones to keep: those most important for your goals, or those you enjoy most |

A deload week doesn't mean dropping everything. Pick the option you find easier to motivate for:

| Option | Conditioning | Climbing |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Keep some structured conditioning | Keep climbing unstructured |
| 2 | Reduce to stretching or mobility only | Keep fewer structured climbing sessions |

### Why it works
- With fewer sessions to fit in, the week is easier to arrange flexibly; on a tired or low-motivation day you can push a planned session to another day.
- Fewer sessions leave more energy and motivation for the ones you do.
- Consistency in exercises is key to adaptation, and not dreading sessions you *have* to do helps you stick with training in the long term.

## Cramps that make you not want to climb
- Cramps are one of the most common period symptoms, ranging from uncomfortable to very painful, and can be more pronounced during puberty when cycles are just starting. They don't prevent exercise, but approach this with subtlety and compassion.

### Keep moving, but gently
- Movement has been shown to ease cramp pain.
- Sync the phase when you get cramps with a rest week and use easy mileage or low-angled climbing as gentle movement.
- To give these sessions intention, try mindful movement, coordinating movement with breathing, relaxing your grip and finding well-balanced positions.

### Reduce load on the lower back and abdomen
- Exercises with weight attached to a harness can feel worse with cramps.

| Option | How |
|---|---|
| Move sessions within the week | Depending on how many days the cramps last, e.g. max hangs on Saturday instead of Monday |
| Drop the weights that week | Focus on form in conditioning exercises |

- Example: schedule the rest week for your period and do weighted pull-ups at bodyweight only, focusing on good scapula engagement and smooth movement through the pull-up. The next 3-week training block starts the following week, with weight added again once the cramps are gone.

### Use pain relief
- The "I don't need it / it's not that bad / I'll take some if it doesn't go away in an hour" attitude is widespread. Tracking tells you when cramps are likely, so be ready with aspirin; as an anti-inflammatory it may be more effective because of its effect on prostaglandins. If your cramps are bad, take it from the onset.
- Preparing in advance can be particularly useful for competition climbers, whose competition schedule can't be synced with their cycle: they may be able to adjust training, but must also plan how to reduce symptoms if a competition falls on a cramp day.
