# Deload Weeks: When and How to Take Them

**Type:** Planning, Principles · **Trains:** Recovery · **For:** Beginner, serious climbers, competition climbers, climbing trip, fatigue or aches

**TL;DR:** A deload is a short period of reduced training, usually a week, in which you keep climbing and training at lower volume and/or intensity, so fatigue drops sharply while little fitness is lost. Deloads are not a replacement for load-management skills, self-discipline and good coaching, but fatigue in climbing can't be predicted and some accumulation is plausible, so proactive deloads every 6-12 weeks are worthwhile for most climbers. Deload reactively when performance drops or fatigue rises: about 25% less volume for a little fatigue, about 50% less volume and intensity for aches or significant fatigue, 75-100% less for signs of overtraining. Taper before competitions and climbing trips.

## Who should do what
| Situation / level | Do this |
|---|---|
| Deloading as a precaution or because a little fatigue is creeping up | One week, reduce volume but not intensity, for both climbing and strength training. Start with about a 25% volume reduction, see how you respond and adjust. Simpler alternative: reduce volume, intensity or both any way you like, as long as total work is lower |
| Deloading because of aches and pains or more significant fatigue | One week, reduce both volume and intensity by about 50%, especially activities that load the affected area. If only the fingers feel tweaky and the rest feels fine, keep exercises that don't tax the fingers; if you feel generally run down, reduce all training |
| Deloading because of prolonged muscle soreness, achy joints, chronic tiredness, mood swings, disrupted sleep or frequent injuries | Reduce volume and intensity by 75-100%, possibly for two weeks or more. To prevent a repeat, consider proactive deloads |
| Beginner | Proactive deloads are probably not necessary (optional): beginner gains come at low intensity and ordinary rest days are mostly enough |
| More serious climbers pushing harder, maybe with supplemental strength training | Deload every 6-12 weeks (climbers vary a lot). 12 weeks is the longest, because some forms of fatigue are hard to sense. Less than 6 weeks is usually too frequent, since you lose a significant amount of training and skill practice every month; it is still an option as prevention, especially when starting a new program and unsure you can handle the load |
| Competition climbers | Deload according to the coach's training plan, or every 6-12 weeks, and above all taper before competitions (see the note Tapering Before a Competition or a Climbing Trip) |
| Climbing trip, to maximize sends | Taper in the week before the trip (see the note Tapering Before a Competition or a Climbing Trip) |
| Also regularly doing other sports such as powerlifting or bodybuilding | Deloads may be even more warranted. Bodybuilding aims to maximize muscle damage for growth, i.e. the longest-lasting form of fatigue, which makes fatigue accumulation far more likely |

## What a deload is
- A deload is a short period of reduced training, usually lasting a week: reduced volume and/or intensity of climbing, strength training, or both.
- Most deloads are not a full stop of activity: during most deloads you should keep climbing and training.

| Kind | What it is | When |
|---|---|---|
| Taper | Reduced training, usually a more gradual reduction, carefully timed so the athlete is in peak shape for the substantial fatigue about to come | Before a competition or high-performance phase |
| Proactive deload | Scheduled ahead of time at regular intervals | Often every 4-12 weeks, which often lines up with training cycles |
| Reactive deload | Autoregulated | Only when the athlete feels fatigued or performance has noticeably dipped |

## Why deloads work: the fitness-fatigue model
- Performance ("preparedness") at any time depends on both fitness and fatigue. Training builds fitness and causes acute fatigue; rest clears the fatigue. Resting too long also loses fitness, but much more slowly, so you can almost always recover before detraining. If fatigue is low enough by the next session, performance is high enough for progressive overload and quality technique practice, and fitness and performance rise over time.
- **Warning:** With lots of climbing or hard training, mental and physical fatigue can slowly accumulate between sessions. Once performance is too compromised for progressive overload and skill learning, the wanted adaptations stop. Keeping that fatigue too long, or adding more, risks overtraining: stuck on the same problems for ages, burnout, "tweaky" joints or a more serious injury.
- A deload reduces training load for a short period so fatigue drops sharply while little fitness is lost, and you return at a higher level of performance. As long as you deload before you are deep into overtraining, one week appears to be enough.

## The argument against deloads
- Research on strength training suggests most fatigue from a normal workout resolves within hours, or a day or two depending on intensity. If most or all peripheral and central fatigue dissipates quickly, you can get stronger without long-lasting fatigue, which makes "inevitable fatigue accumulation" doubtful.
- The most significant fatigue occurs locally in the muscle. Extra fatigue from, for example, a back workout does not make the whole body fatigued or unable to adapt in other areas, so a full deload for one fatigued body part is questionable.
- **Warning:** Prolonged fatigue most likely comes from excessive muscle damage (which can also increase central fatigue) or excessive connective tissue damage, which usually means the workouts go way overboard or there is an injury. Needing a deload every month to function means either compromised recovery capacity (sleep habits, stress, illness, etc.) or training way too hard. Both raise injury and burnout risk and lose training and technique time; using deloads to fix this is a bandaid for bad habits, poor programming or poor coaching.
- Rigid deload protocols don't suit every athlete: some respond poorly to an abrupt interruption, especially while progressing well, and athletes don't all benefit from deloads at the same intervals. One deload calendar for everyone is not ideal; programming should adapt to the athlete's needs and preferences.
- No research shows deloads lead to better training outcomes. Research on tapers shows they are effective, but there are no long-term comparisons of athletes who deload and athletes who don't.
- The one somewhat relevant study: after a nine-week training intervention, the non-deload group had better strength results than the deload group. Limits: (1) the deload group stopped all activity during the deload, which is not how most athletes deload; (2) the exercises likely had a significant new-skill-learning element, so the non-deload group got an extra week of practice (supported by similar muscle hypertrophy in both groups); (3) small population and only nine weeks, and the skill-learning element may have kept subjects from pushing hard enough to accumulate fatigue.
- Deloads are not a replacement for intuitive load-management skills, self-discipline and good coaching, but they are still recommended.

## The argument for deloads in climbing
- Fatigue from climbing can't be defined on a large scale because climbing is not one thing: a day of trad climbing in the mountains, a three-hour gym bouldering session, strength training for climbing, crimps and slopers each produce different fatigue.
- Most climbers don't follow an ideal program with optimized recovery, not everyone has the resources to maximize fitness and minimize fatigue, and long sessions are tempting, so most climbers are prone to overdoing it.
- **Warning:** Climbers load the same specific muscles, tissues and joints every session (a bodybuilder can switch to lower body or biceps when other parts are tired). This relentless use makes fatigue in those areas more likely and probably explains the high rate of chronic climbing injuries. Many connective tissues give poor fatigue feedback, so you can't always feel an injury coming.
- Exercise science doesn't know everything about fatigue; some types may be overlooked, and mental fatigue also matters.
- Conclusion: fatigue in climbing can't be predicted, but some accumulation is plausible in the real world, so proactive deloads are worthwhile for most climbers.

## Reactive deloads
- There is no science on the optimal way to deload, only guidelines. Use a reactive deload when you notice a drop in performance or a rise in fatigue (mental or physical).
- Spot it early; that is much easier if you track your training, so every climber should keep a training log.
- Why reduce volume but keep intensity: less volume means much less fatigue; keeping intensity keeps high-quality near-limit skill practice and maybe slightly better retention of neuromuscular efficiency.
- **Warning:** Prolonged muscle soreness, achy joints, chronic tiredness, mood swings, disrupted sleep or frequent injuries almost never appear overnight or without warning signs, so take an honest look at the lifestyle or training choices behind them: a deload is not a complete solution to systemic overtraining.

## Proactive deloads
- Proactive deloads are done much like reactive ones, usually reducing volume rather than intensity; the difference is that they are planned into the training plan.
- **Warning:** If you consistently need a deload every three or four weeks, strongly reconsider your training or recovery habits.
