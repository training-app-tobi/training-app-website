# Training with Reps in Reserve (RIR)

**Type:** Principles, Exercise/protocol · **Trains:** General strength, Power, Power endurance, Endurance · **For:** stalled progress

**TL;DR:** Keeping reps in reserve (RIR), i.e. stopping sets short of the maximum, lowers RPE, keeps form and muscle recruitment consistent, and most likely allows more sets at the same load. Decide what you are targeting (strength, power or muscular endurance) and the load to keep, decide how many reps to keep in reserve, then redistribute the work into more, shorter sets (e.g. 3x8 becomes 4x6 with RIR-2). The same idea applies on the wall: lower the grade or shorten the sets to get more quality efforts. Use it for one phase, track total volume, then come back to it later.

## When and how to use RIR

- Depending on how you manipulate volume and load, RIR can develop strength, power or muscular endurance. The lower the load and the higher the reps completed, the more muscular endurance is trained.
- Alternating blocks of strength and muscular endurance produces great strength results and higher muscular economy for that muscle group.
- In climbing, a "strength" workout can be a project-level bouldering session or an integrated bouldering session; a "muscular endurance" session can be an intensive endurance session.
- RIR can be used during a regular training phase, as a maintenance tool ahead of a performance event, or during a performance phase.

## Why reps in reserve

- Strength workouts usually aim for a specific rep range or rate of perceived exertion (RPE) at a specific load. This is a tried and true method that can produce great strength results.
- Case: an athlete stalled on the front squat; a ladder scheme and a 3x8 scheme with accessory exercises didn't help. Keeping reps in reserve (RIR), i.e. stopping sets short of the maximum, lowers RPE and allows more consistent form and muscle recruitment. After 5 weeks at the same load, the athlete could perform repetitions at roughly 20% more of their previous load. During the cycle the athlete was reminded to practise good form and control and asked after each workout how the sets felt; they reported lower RPE and showed more consistent control than in previous workouts.
- Same case in workout volume: the athlete went from lifting 41 kg 32 times in a workout to 71 times in a workout by the end of the cycle, all at a lower RPE than before.
- Typical problem: 3 sets of 8 reps at 75% of max barbell squat, and the last couple of reps of set 2 are grueling, form gets sloppy (glutes not engaged, back hunching) and you miss reps, so you'd drop weight on the last set. Instead use RIR: it keeps a high load without compromising form or the goal of the workout, lets you manipulate the reps per set, decreases the overall feeling of fatigue from the workout, and most likely allows more sets at the same load.

## How to set it up

| Step | What to do |
|---|---|
| 1 | Decide what you are targeting (strength, power or muscular endurance) and the goal reps or load to maintain throughout the workout |
| 2 | Decide how many reps to keep in reserve |
| 3 | Perform the workout |
| Example | An original 3x8 with "RIR-2" (2 reps in reserve per set) becomes 4x6. This doesn't move the workout away from its original goal as long as the load is correct |

- Load usually determines the outcome of a workout: load creates the intensity of the lift, which determines how many times you can lift it (the volume). In the example this is one exercise at one load (75% of max) and one volume (24 reps). Over a whole workout or phase, total load and volume become more intricate and equally important to understand.

| Muscular endurance example (60% of max barbell squat) | Sets x reps | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Original | 3 x 15 | Starting point: 15 reps at load |
| RIR-3 | 5 x 12 | Significantly lower RPE than 3x15, and 15 more reps at the desired load, which adds up in the long term |

- Using RIR has been shown to improve overall strength and power gains compared with a traditional percentage-of-1-rep-max scheme.

## Volume, load and intensity

- Volume is any amount of exercise or effort given during a particular amount of time; it can also include the time given during an effort. Mechanical stimulus is the duration for which the muscle fibres controlled by high-threshold motor units are activated and shortened slowly.
- This tells you the volume of stimulating repetitions at a given load that produces the desired results: load and volume must be such that the muscles respond to a higher stimulus in order to progress and get stronger. Load is the weight or difficulty of an effort. Intensity is a dependent variable that changes with volume, load or both. A "stimulating repetition" is one at a load where the athlete recruits the most muscle fibres in that movement.
- Perform each rep with excellent form and control to recruit as many muscle fibres as possible. RIR allows this because the athlete focuses on performance under load instead of counting or worrying about hitting a specific number of reps.

## Putting it into practice

Capacity day at the crag (low load for your ability, high volume relative to your normal pitch count), for someone who projects 5.12a:

| Version | Average grade | Pitches |
|---|---|---|
| Original capacity day | 5.10c | 8 |
| With RIR | 5.10a | 10: slightly lower load, 2 more pitches, without sacrificing good technique |

- Power endurance in the bouldering gym (high load, low total volume): to build capacity for power over a longer time, use RIR here too. A power endurance session might be 4x4s, or 3 sets of onsight bouldering, one problem every minute for 5 minutes, with full rest after each set. With RIR you manipulate either the V-grade or the time per set.
- In the original example you climb only 15 problems at a given grade (load). Lower the V-grade: load decreases and volume can increase. Shorten the time per set at the same V-grade: total sets (volume) can increase. Either way the total number of quality efforts goes up and RPE stays manageable.

| Session | Original | RIR version |
|---|---|---|
| On-the-minute bouldering | 3 sets of on-the-minute bouldering for 6 minutes at an average of V5, full rest after each set | 5 sets of on-the-minute bouldering for 4 minutes at an average of V5, full rest after each set: higher volume, same intensity, more high-quality repetitions |
| 4x4 | 4x4 at an average of V5 | 5x3 at an average of V5, or 5x4 at an average of V4 |

## Cautions and when to use it

- **Warning:** RIR creates quality reps and technique in the gym and at the crag. Even though intensity, load or volume may feel less intense, track total volume over a training phase to make sure overtraining is not occurring.
- Like any set and rep scheme, staying with it too long eventually stops producing results: use RIR for one phase, then come back to it later. It is easy to manipulate to achieve the desired results, which supports long-term growth.
