# Using RPE to Measure and Manage Climbing Training Intensity

**Type:** Principles, Exercise/protocol · **Trains:** Finger strength, Power, Power endurance, Endurance, Technique, Recovery

**TL;DR:** RPE (rate of perceived exertion) rates how hard the final rep or effort actually felt, from 10 (true failure, 0 reps in reserve) downwards; training adaptations follow how hard something actually is for you, not its grade or the percentage on paper. Use RPE to adjust load to how you are that day, log it after every set or climb that matters, and read it as a trend over weeks. Keep most sessions at RPE 6–8, save occasional RPE 9–10 efforts for key limit days or projects, and balance them with easier sessions around RPE 4–5.

## Why grades are a poor intensity measure
- Training adaptations (in strength and partly in skill development) happen based on how difficult something actually is for you, not on its grade.
- Grades are hard to align between people: prescribing "a V5" for a V5 climber in a power endurance, strength or skill drill gives no idea of the physical experience. One V5 can be technically complex but physically easy (e.g. an overhang where good beta and a heel hook make it easy), while another is technically simple but physically very hard.

## What RPE is
- RPE = rate of perceived exertion: how hard an effort actually feels while training or climbing, not how hard it was supposed to be on paper.
- RPE is inversely related to RIR (reps in reserve: how many quality reps you could have done before failure).
- RPE describes the intensity of the final rep or effort, not the average difficulty of the whole set: how close you were to your limit at the end.

| RPE | Reps in reserve |
|---|---|
| 10 | 0: true failure, you could not have done any more |
| 9 | 1 rep left |
| 8 | 2 reps left, and so on |

## RPE in strength and endurance work
- In lifting, RPE is straightforward: slow, heavy movements make it clear when you are close to failure (e.g. "that deadlift felt like a 9 out of 10", "I could have done maybe one more pull-up with decent form"). Climbing mixes strength, endurance, problem solving and skin tolerance, so it is less clear.
- Strength-based climbing (hangboarding, limit bouldering, weighted pull-ups): RPE is about how close you are to technical failure, not emotional or motivational failure. High RPE = maybe one more second from falling off the hangboard or from losing a one-arm lock-off. RPE 10 is total failure; RPE 6 is something you could cruise through while chatting. The target number depends on your goal.
- Endurance work (power endurance circuits, repeaters, ARC): endurance RPE builds slowly and then suddenly the arms are gone, while the brain still says "we're fine". Endurance training shouldn't always be 10/10.
- Learn to gauge not just "am I pumped" but how pumped, and whether it is a productive pump or a panic pump (e.g. "this round feels like RPE 7, the right zone to build capacity without falling apart").

| Endurance work | RPE |
|---|---|
| Training below threshold | Might feel like RPE 5–6 |
| Working capacity | Maybe RPE 8 |

## RPE versus percentages (autoregulation)
- Percentage-based training (e.g. hang at 85% of max for 10 seconds, 3 sets, from a max hang or 1-rep-max pull) assumes you show up the same every time. It ignores poor sleep, a double session the day before, central nervous system fatigue, a skin crisis, stress, hydration, motivation and alcohol the night before. RPE adjusts to your actual state that day.
- The body adapts to the stimulus (the actual effort, strain and fatigue), not to the number on the weight belt or hangboard. If the planned load feels much harder than usual, forcing it won't give better results and may drive fatigue or injury; adjusting the load to hit the right zone (e.g. RPE 8) keeps the right stimulus without overcooking yourself.

| Situation | Do this |
|---|---|
| You normally hang with +18 kg for 10 seconds at RPE 8, but today it feels like RPE 10 | Back off to +13.5 kg or +11.5 kg; the effort, and so the stimulus, stays the same |
| You feel strong and the planned 85% hang feels like RPE 6 | Push a little more |

- Percentage prescriptions in a plan should be equated to an RPE to stay consistent across days. Having both is useful: honour the RPE, and keep the percentage numbers when you feel good.
- Autoregulation = adjusting training load to how you feel and perform during a workout. Example: a 6-week block was hit in week 3 by a cold and a triceps strain; instead of sticking to the plan or abandoning it, the load was reduced, the structure kept, whatever was possible was trained, and intensity was scaled by feel; once healthy, training resumed where it left off with no regression.
- Using RPE is a learnable skill: the more you use it, the better your self-awareness and training decisions, leading over time to more progress, fewer injuries and less frustration. It teaches you to notice when you are pushing too hard and when you could have pushed more (e.g. telling a solid RPE 8 from an all-out grind).
- Write a plan, then make small changes based on how you feel that day, either in RPE or by changing the session to something productive when you don't feel great. Don't follow a plan blindly.
- When working with a coach, make sure the plan can be changed based on what happens over the weeks (not a fixed PDF), and report after each session so small adjustments can be made.

## How to log RPE
- Log in the moment or immediately after each working set or climb that matters (max hang, hard boulder, power endurance circuit).
- Notebook, phone app, notes, voice notes, spreadsheet or a chalkboard all work; what matters is consistency.

| Log field | Example |
|---|---|
| What you did | 2 reps of 10-second hangs at +11.5 kg |
| How it felt | Solid form, slight elbow twinge |
| RPE | 8/10 |

- Consistent logs show patterns, e.g. "my limit bouldering sessions start at RPE 7 and drift into 9 by the end" or "my Monday capacity sets feel like RPE 10, so I may need more rest after the weekend", which you can then plan around.

## RPE by session type
| Session type | RPE | How |
|---|---|---|
| Limit bouldering (maximal effort days) | Touch RPE 9s and 10s but don't live there; keep most quality attempts between RPE 8 and 9 | Rest long between efforts, 5–10 minutes, so the nervous system recovers and each attempt is sharp and intentional |
| Technique-focused (movement drills on slab, practising dead points, flowing on easy terrain, breathing drills) | RPE 4–6 | Controlled, smooth and repeatable; you should be able to do dozens of reps without deep fatigue, so the brain stays engaged and the body refines movement |

- Limit bouldering: sometimes as few as 2–4 high-RPE attempts are enough before performance and form start to slide; some people can handle 15–20, but that is not most people, so find where your limit is.
- **Warning:** a common mistake is doing too many high-RPE attempts back to back too early in the session: quality drops, form gets sketchy and you rehearse failure.
- Technique sessions: if RPE goes higher than 4–6, ask whether you are still focused on movement or sneaking in hard sends (a common pattern: a movement session drifts to RPE 9–10 on something that looked technical).

## Load management
- Both "go hard or go home" climbers (who think a session only counts if they end in meltdown) and "only go hard when the vibes are right" climbers (who then plateau in power) need RPE: it lets you scale volume and intensity to what your body can do that day.

| Situation | Do this |
|---|---|
| Power endurance: the plan calls for 4 rounds but after round 2 you are already at RPE 9 | Do 3 rounds today, or keep going with shorter reps or more rest; adjust rather than push through blindly |

- **Warning:** if a hang should feel like RPE 7 and suddenly feels like RPE 9, that is a red flag: not productive fatigue but the start of overload, which may show up a week later (e.g. as elbow pain).
- RPE is also a recovery barometer: if RPEs spike higher than usual across several sessions, consider a deload or more rest.

| Share of sessions | RPE | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Most training sessions | Moderate, RPE 6–8 | Hard enough to improve without entering the risky red zone |
| Occasional efforts on key limit days or projects | RPE 9–10 | Save them for these days |
| Easier sessions to balance them | Around RPE 4–5 | Recovery and technique |

- Training too hard too often is the fastest way to waste progress.
- These RPE ranges don't account for technical complexity. Skill sessions (e.g. footwork project, tension project, mobility project) are physically easy (low RPE) but highly complex, with a low success rate, so they can be very difficult sessions at RPE 4–5. Handless slab can be RPE 1–2 for everything except the toes.
- RPE can be grouped by body part or muscle group, depending on what the training cycle is trying to preserve. After an RPE 9–10 upper-body day, a 9/10-difficulty slab session the next day is fine as long as it doesn't load the upper body or fingers.
- **Warning:** don't follow a 9/10 upper-body or limit session with a 9/10 physical lower-body session the next day: too much central nervous system load and fatigue.
- If you plan a session at RPE 5–6, hold that line. Ramping into RPE 8–9 is not wrong, but do it consciously: you are shifting the week's stress load and borrowing intensity from another day. (Example: a light skill day turned into 12 attempts on a new boulder at RPE 9, leaving fried fingers and no motivation for the next day's planned training.)

## Getting accurate at RPE
- Early RPE ratings are usually inaccurate: people either overestimate how fine they are, or underestimate (rating warm-up hangs as RPE 9). Rating RPE is a skill that improves with intentional use.
- Calibrate by comparing perceived effort with actual output: film your climbs, write down your numbers, and notice changes from start to end of the session (technique breakdown, rep quality, total duration, rest between sets).
- Example: a climber who rated everything RPE 7 had "RPE 7" limit hangs (one-arm hangs with +9 kg) that on film were shaking, with elbows flaring and last-second efforts, i.e. RPE 9. If you log RPE 6 but your third rep looked like a fight, the rating is off.
- Treat RPE as a trend line over weeks, not a single snapshot (single ratings can be off). Example: hangs at +11.5 kg logged at RPE 9 feel like RPE 7 after 3 weeks → measurable adaptation, so load or volume can be increased.
- **Warning:** if RPEs trend higher while the session structure hasn't changed, it may mean under-recovery, too much life stress or the start of overtraining; catching it early can prevent an injury (e.g. "RPE increasing all week → maybe deload"). Same loads rated lower = progress; easy days feeling harder = time to adjust.
- Optional: colour-code RPE in the log and review the month's spread to see whether training was balanced.

| Colour | RPE |
|---|---|
| Red | 9–10 |
| Yellow | 7–8 |
| Green | 5–6 |

| Self-regulation step | Do this |
|---|---|
| 1. Reframe the win | Ask "did I hit the right RPE for today's goal?" instead of "did I go hard enough?" |
| 2. Remove guesswork | New athletes get an RPE target for the day (e.g. technique session: keep RPE under 6, back off if creeping to 7 or above), then reflect after the session on whether they stayed in the zone and why not |
| 3. Value restraint | Leave the session feeling better than when you started, meet the week's goals with energy left, or move a session to the next day when today would be wasted |

- Leaving the gym not wrecked leads to better gains, more consistent performance and fewer plateaus.
- Example of ignored logs: three weeks of RPE logs trending up, all red zone, with no true RPE 6 day in over a month, explained why every session felt harder. Objectivity is hard in the moment and easier with tracking and honest reflection.

## Drill: rest-limited RPE ladder
- Purpose: learn what a true RPE 6–7 feels like (probably the most common training range), as opposed to the higher range, whose attempts carry more fatigue.
- Everything is repeatable with clear stopping points; work should feel purposeful but controlled. Finish each set composed, technically sound and mentally steady, knowing you could repeat it and that more effort was available but intentionally unused. That restraint is the skill trained.
- **Warning:** stop rules: end the set immediately if form breaks down, breathing becomes rushed or uncontrolled, or you feel a sudden emotional urge to push harder. That urge is information, not a green light.

### Round 1
- Choose one repeatable boulder, about 2–3 grades below your onsight level or RPE 5, familiar and non-threatening, one you could normally send comfortably several times (an overhanging one gives more stimulus).
- Climb it 3 times; only the rest changes.

| Attempt | Rest | How it should feel |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Fully rested: rest after the warm-up until breathing is calm and you feel ready | RPE 5: smooth, relaxed, easy |
| 2 | Shortened: start before fully recovered, when you feel "not ready to try my hardest" | RPE 6: very mild pump, movement controlled, effort noticeable |
| 3 | Shorter still: start with some fatigue present | Mild pump, engagement and focus, but no strain or fight |

- If attempt 2 doesn't feel harder, do one more attempt; ideally 3 attempts in total, sometimes it takes 4–5.
- Rules: don't rush the moves, keep the same cadence (only rest changes); stop if you feel the urge to turn it into a max attempt; finish knowing you could repeat the climb.
- Reflection after round 1 (part of the training): write down the RPE you predicted for each attempt and what it actually felt like. If you missed the target, note why (rest shortened too aggressively, moves rushed instead of letting fatigue create effort, ego or urgency). If on target, note what worked (rest timing, breathing, pacing, mental state).

### Round 2 (calibration correction)
- A second repeatable boulder, 1–2 grades below onsight, same ladder, only 3 attempts: full rest → RPE 6, then shortened rest → RPE 7.
- Apply what you learned and adjust rest slightly if needed; don't change effort intentionally, let fatigue do the work. The goal is accuracy to the RPE target, not more intensity.

### Rest and calibration
- Rest between attempts: usually 1 minute to go from RPE 5 to 6 on the second lap; can be 2–3 minutes; people with great recovery may need only 30 seconds. It depends on the climb: longer climbs, even easier ones, leave a more lingering pump; a short punchy one loads the fingers more. Work it out with the reflection step.
- Calibrating RPE 5: you come off with the mildest pump, you had to work but felt strong on the moves, and you could have done the boulder again stacked on top of itself (double it).
- The ladder can be extended up to RPE 8, 9 and 10, e.g. by using the second limit boulder of a limit day for it (not the first).

## Other calibration methods
- Ranked circuit: make a circuit on a board or in the gym and rank climbs from 10 (hardest you can do / one you can't do) down to 1 (e.g. RPE 1 = a particular V0, 2 = a V1, 3 = a harder V1, then a V2 …), so each RPE level is tied to a concrete climb and experience.
- Finding RPE 9: set a sustained, strength-based (not powerful) boulder of about 10 moves where the last move is the hardest; add one move at a time. Do very few laps. Because there is no dynamic fail point, you are likely to be able to do the moves.

| Result on the RPE 9 boulder | RPE |
|---|---|
| Falling | RPE 10: dial back one move |
| Sticking the last move only just | Probably RPE 9 (sometimes sticking it is a 10: any harder and you'd be off) |

## RPE by training type
| Training type | RPE |
|---|---|
| Power | Usually in the upper range, but can be as low as RPE 6 (e.g. bodyweight pull-ups done as fast as possible); can go up to 10, usually in limit bouldering, though less often than max strength |
| Max strength | More often goes to the max |
| Power endurance | Usually not above RPE 9; never fail, always keep a margin; if you are about to fall in a 4x4, stop and switch to something you won't fail on |
| Endurance: capacity | Up to about RPE 8 |
| Endurance: aerobic intervals on the wall | The last interval may go to RPE 9–10 and failure, usually in the last set and only for trained athletes |
| Endurance: ARC | RPE 2–3 |

- With dynamic moves it is hard to reach RPE 10: you usually reach about 9 on a launch, pull or catch.
- Power and campusing: don't go to failure or do something you can't do. Work at RPE 7–8 but move very powerfully (e.g. a 15 ladder, or brought down, a 1-3-6 campus ladder that feels like RPE 7–8). Stop as soon as it feels harder than before in any degree or you move slower, i.e. the second you are no longer at peak. A long rest may allow 1–2 more, but it is usually better to stop or move on to the other part of training.
- With endurance and power endurance, the more advanced the athlete, the higher the RPE they can push and still adapt; very advanced athletes can push into the highest ranges, but only briefly (maybe the last rep or last set).
- RPE is mainly a load-management tool rather than a way to gauge which energy system is being used, because RPE can vary widely for any type of training and depends on how long the bout lasts.

## Rating specific situations
- Falling off from pump or fatigue mid-move is RPE 10 only if you were trying your absolute hardest. If you are just tired (e.g. trying a known V6 straight after a 4x4), you probably weren't trying your hardest, and fatigue degraded form (central nervous system and stabilisers tired), so it isn't a 10.
- More high-RPE attempts on a limit day comes from capacity and recoverability: build general climbing capacity, then capacity in the higher range, mostly at RPE 7–9 max (going to 10 all the time is not an effective way to build capacity); usually split into two capacity blocks.
- Tension and footwork projects at RPE 5–6: you do try hard, but through technical engagement (coordination, body placement, shifting into the right positions), not the whole body at max; legs shouldn't need to go to 10. You shouldn't come off exhausted or fall because it was physically too hard; you should fall because of a technical movement you didn't execute correctly.
- At RPE 7 there should be no technique breakdown at all; form stays the same or even improves because you are more engaged. What changes is that you squeeze harder with the fingers.
- For learning RPE, use grip-intensive climbs: RPE matters most for fingers and forearms (effort in biceps or back is felt mostly through the grip).
- Project with tries left: if a session plans 6 tries on a project and you send on the 4th, it's your choice: repeat it (you learn the moves better and become more efficient) or start learning another climb (2 tries just open the account on a new one, but count as part of the total attempts across sessions). It depends on the climb, the situation and which option you are more psyched on and will give more effort to.
- Rate RPE by your own internal feedback, not by what others see: shaking, or others saying a climb looked easy and smooth, doesn't match internal effort (shaking can happen at RPE 7–8 and look like 9–10; people also tend to be polite).
- Track RPE in outdoor sessions too, not just indoors.
- Think of a day-level RPE as a separate lever from exercise-specific RPE, set by what the day contains and what you are trying to preserve that day (often the fingers). A skill day shouldn't be very hard. Example: a day with RPE 9 finger load, then a session with RPE 6 finger work followed by pull training that reaches RPE 9; the "day RPE" is for the fingers or energy system in focus.

## RPE in rehab
- Typically stay under RPE 8 at all times until the movement is ready to be brought back fully, and even then not often; you want to reach RPE 10 eventually, at the very end of rehab.
- For most of rehab you are rebuilding tissue, and the starting RPE depends on which RPE brings on pain.
- Example (knee): at first the most possible was RPE 1 (basically a heel hook) before reaching 3/10 discomfort; RPE 2 caused 9/10 pain. After a little over a month and a half of laddering RPE from 1 up to 10, RPE 10 is possible with no pain, but only after warming up (some pain at the start of the session). Most of the work was done at RPE 8, with RPE 10 reached in one drill.
- In a rehab session that becomes too much, it can be right to cut the last sets entirely (e.g. 3 sets skipped) to protect a key session two days later.
