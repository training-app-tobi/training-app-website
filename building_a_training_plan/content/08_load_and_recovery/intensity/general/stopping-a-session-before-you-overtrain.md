# Stopping a Session Before You Overtrain

**Type:** Goals & mindset, Planning · **Trains:** Recovery

**TL;DR:** Overtraining within a session comes from squeezing in one more attempt, move or route past your limits, and the next day's performance suffers. Before each session, write down how many attempts, how long and which skills; use mantras to resist "just one more"; and plot each session on a green/yellow/red growth graph. Stop when you know you're done and stick to the plan.

## The problem
- Overtraining within a session happens when you get carried away and squeeze in one more attempt, one more move or one more route past your limits. It is an emotional issue as well as a physical one: eventually the body says "enough" and the next day's performance suffers.
- Cost of the habit: overdoing a session every month or two set performance back by two full grades for weeks at a time. After adopting the tools below, the mistake happened far less often and its consequences were much less severe.

## Strategy 1: Pre-session journaling
- The most important step: before the session starts, write down exactly what you plan to accomplish: how many attempts, how long to train, and which specific skills to focus on. Writing it down solidifies the session's goals, keeps you on track and prevents the temptation to overdo it.
- Once the plan is on paper, the chance of sticking to it rises sharply; a clear, defined goal makes you much more likely to follow through. This step alone doubles your rate of improvement right away, with faster progress and fewer setbacks in the long run.

## Strategy 2: Mantras
- In the heat of the moment, when you feel strong, your mind tells you to "just do one more" or "you're feeling great, so why stop now?". Have mantras ready to counteract this and guide you back to the right decision.
- "Be a big cat!": approach climbing with patience, discipline and strategy, like a big cat hunting its prey; strong but strategic. It keeps you calm, controlled and focused.
- "Green Days Only": your best days are the ones where you train smart, stop early and leave the session feeling good, not exhausted. Variation: "Save it; it'll be more fun next time." Even when you feel great, cutting the session short leads to a more enjoyable, productive session next time.

## Strategy 3: Growth graph
On graph paper, plot your progress after each session:

| Session | Colour | Line moves |
|---|---|---|
| Short, focused, and you felt better the next day | Green | Upward |
| Slightly longer but still productive | Yellow | Sideways |
| Long, left you sore or performing worse afterwards | Red | Downward |

- Why it works: the graph gives a clear, objective picture of your growth and reinforces that shorter, more effective sessions move you forward while long, overdone sessions hold you back. Over time you see the pattern and become more motivated to cut sessions short when necessary: quality beats quantity.

## Bottom line
- Stop when you know you're done. Stick to the plan.

## Bonus tips

### Timer
| Step | What to do |
|---|---|
| 1 | Record how long your typical sessions last |
| 2 | Set the timer for 15–45 minutes less than that |
| 3 | Stop when the timer goes off |
| 4 | See which length works best for you |

### Listen to your body
- Learn the subtle signs of fatigue: slower movement or reaction times, sore fingers or body, a drop in concentration. These mean it's time to rest.

### Rest days
- Plan more rest days into your weekly schedule. Knowing that rest is as important as training makes it easier to stop when you need to, and the more rest you get, the more powerful your next session will be.
