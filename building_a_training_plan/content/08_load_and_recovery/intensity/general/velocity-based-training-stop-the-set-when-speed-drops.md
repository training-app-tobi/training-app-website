# Velocity-Based Training: Stop the Set When Speed Drops

**Type:** Principles, Exercise/protocol · **Trains:** General strength, Pulling strength, Power, Power endurance

**TL;DR:** Velocity-based training (VBT) tracks the speed of a lift and stops each set when speed slows noticeably (around 25–35% velocity loss), instead of grinding to failure. This keeps you in the zone of strength most useful for climbing and matches the effort to the day's readiness. Apply it to one or two key exercises, such as weighted pull-ups or hangboard protocols, with reps capped at 5 and loads heavy enough to make you stronger.

## Who should do what
| Situation / level | Do this |
|---|---|
| Goal: maintain capacity, and reps per set are dropping | Add one or two more sets |
| Goal: pure strength | The lower volume is still enough |

## Why not train to failure
- **Warning:** Typical weight training takes basic movements to the point where the muscles can no longer sustain the load, sometimes further into failure. This does not optimize strength: the fatigue creates more of a muscular-endurance and hypertrophy stimulus, and many sets that deep can need many days of recovery. For high-skill athletes this is not acceptable: the days truly ready for hard training are few, so each one should be used well.

## What velocity-based training is
- VBT tracks the speed of a lift, a direct measure of force production and fatigue. It brings objectivity to programming and keeps progress consistent: you adjust on real-time velocity feedback instead of relying only on subjective effort or an estimated 1RM.
- For climbers, whose strength-to-weight ratio is critical, it dials in training intensity without going to failure in the weight room.
- Daily readiness: percentage-based training assumes consistent performance, but stress, sleep and nutrition change capacity from day to day. VBT trains within a target velocity range, matching the effort to the day's readiness. Especially relevant for athletes who combine technical skill with strength work, as climbers do.

| Bar speed | What to do |
|---|---|
| Slower than the target speed | Adjust the load or prioritize recovery |
| Faster than the target speed | Push harder, or progress sooner in the training cycle |

## What the research says
- Training with velocity zones helps optimize power production and improve neuromuscular efficiency. It is particularly effective for developing explosive strength, which carries over directly to dynamic climbing movements. It can reduce the risk of overtraining by identifying fatigue earlier.
- Linear position transducers or wearable accelerometers make VBT easy to implement, but their cost and accessibility remain barriers for almost everyone.
- The most effective VBT is at around 25–35% velocity loss within a set, which is fairly easy to feel in practice. Knowing the principle matters more than precise measurement: stop a set when speed slows noticeably, and you might not need anything else.
- **Warning:** Stopping a set out of boredom, because you reached ten reps, or because the clock said so may undershoot the target zone. Grinding until failure forces you to stop leaves the zone of strength most useful for climbing.

## Pull-up example
Goal: strength and explosiveness. Reps capped at 5, because many more lead to endurance adaptations that aren't wanted here.

| Workout | Load | Sets | Reps per set |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Bodyweight | 5 | 5-5-5-5-5, no velocity loss |
| 2 | Bodyweight + 4.5 kg | 5 | 5-5-4-4-3 |
| 3 | Bodyweight + 5.5 kg | 6 | 5-4-3-3-3-3 |

## How to progress
| Sign of progress | What it shows |
|---|---|
| Maintaining velocity for more reps | Power endurance |
| A higher load at a fixed number of reps | Power |

## How to use it
- **Warning:** Don't apply VBT to everything. Pick one or two key exercises and do them carefully, with the intent of maintaining high speed.
- Loads still need to be heavy enough to make you stronger; it's fairly easy to tell when you're going too light.
- Climbing isn't all about speed: it's still fine to do some slow training, some static training and some training at normal speed.
- For climbers, VBT can mean prioritizing a strength quality such as peak power or endurance under load, depending on the goal of the current phase. Use it to refine weighted pull-ups or hangboard protocols, so you work in the most effective intensity zones.
- Keep it simple and consistent. Its value comes when paired with a thoughtful training plan and clear goals. Focusing on speed and intent builds strength and a stronger connection between training and performance on the wall.
