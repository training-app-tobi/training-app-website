# SMART Goals for Climbing

**Type:** Goals & mindset, Planning

**TL;DR:** A specific goal with the right level of challenge improves motivation, progress tracking and performance. Combine long term goals with clear short term goals, and pair outcome or performance goals with process goals you fully control. Make goals SMART (Specific, Measurable, Attainable, Relevant, Time based), break big goals into small steps, write them down, tell people and don't set too many.

## Why goal setting matters
- Goals range from "I just want to get better in general" to "I want to do this project by the end of spring". Both are valid, but a more detailed approach to goal setting raises both the motivation to train and the likelihood of achieving the goal.
- A goal that is specific and gives the right level of challenge improves performance. Studies in a broad range of settings show that people with a specific goal perform better than people simply told to do their best.
- The more challenging the goal, the better the performance, provided the goal is not so difficult that it seems impossible or overwhelming.
- Goals don't only help motivation: they also help track progress and may actually improve performance.
- Don't pick a goal out of thin air or copy your friends' goals. Goal setting brings other psychological elements to consider, such as expectations and fear of failure, so think about your mindset and be honest about what you want.

## Goals by timescale
| Goal type | What it is | How to set it |
|---|---|---|
| Long term | Futuristic goals you wouldn't expect to achieve in a short time. The timescale differs between climbers depending on experience, time climbing and character, among other things. | They don't need to be as specific, but they must mean a lot to you: they need to align with your identity and affect your self-image positively in order to help motivation. |
| Short term | The building blocks and the pathway to your long term goals. | Must be more specific, so they are quantifiable and measurable, and above all realistically achievable in a relatively short time. Start with the long term goal and work backwards. |

- Clear short term goals combined with long term goals have been shown to be most effective for success and sustained motivation.
- Example: long term goal "be a proficient all-round rock climber" → defined as feeling comfortable and performing consistently in bouldering, sport climbing, trad climbing and on big walls → if bouldering is the main weakness, set short term bouldering goals to work towards it.

## Goals by how they are measured
- Not all goals come as a climbing grade, so it isn't wise to measure all progress by whether you climbed a harder grade.

| Goal type | What it is | Examples | Strengths and limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Outcome | Largely out of your control | Winning a climbing competition | Can be extremely motivating, but can increase anxiety, which can hurt performance. Set them with a good understanding of their role in the bigger picture of your climbing. |
| Performance | Similar to outcome goals but based on beating your own previous best; still affected by some other factors, but not by other people's performance | Flash a particular boulder or route; achieve the next grade; flash a boulder in the qualifiers; flash a particular grade outdoors | Very motivating, but can also cause anxiety. |
| Process (also called intrinsic) | Focus on specific elements of the task rather than the end result; not driven by external forces (medals, money, fame, recognition from friends) | Brush the holds before each attempt; spend 10 breaths reflecting on a sequence before pulling on again; climb through an insecure sequence without hesitating on a certain foot move | You are in complete control of achieving them, so they are achievable whatever the outcome; they nurture a good mindset, lead to better concentration and improve your ability to control negative thoughts. |

- **Warning:** Outcome goals can increase anxiety and hurt performance: you may climb your best, keep your nerves under control, work out the sequences under time pressure and still not win.
- **Warning:** Performance goals can also cause anxiety: you could perform your absolute best and still fall just short of the chains.
- Because of this, set process goals to accompany outcome or performance goals.
- Brushing the holds also gives time to relax and focus, and ensures chalky holds won't affect performance.

## Breaking a big goal down
- Break a big goal into steps per year, month, training day and training hour until each step looks achievable.
- Example: a 100 m backstroke target of 55.5 s in 4 years, from 4 s off that time. The target was reached with 55.49 s.

| Period | Improvement needed |
|---|---|
| Per year | 1 s |
| Per month | 1/10 s |
| Per training day | 1/300 s |
| Per training hour | 1/1200 s |

## SMART goals
| Letter | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Specific | Direct, detailed and meaningful. This helps you break a long term goal into short term goals that lead to the main goal. |
| Measurable | Quantifiable so you can track progress, and you know when you have reached the goal. |
| Attainable | Realistic, and you have the tools and/or resources to attain it. A big dream goal is fine, but you should be able to reach it through smaller stepping-stone goals. |
| Relevant | The goal aligns with your mission. Example: if you already have really strong fingers, lots of finger strength goals may not be the best place for your focus. |
| Time based | A deadline helps you focus and fully commit to the goal. |

## Making it happen
- It has to be important to you: goals given by a boss or coach that you are not invested in won't motivate you or increase performance.
- Write your goals down: some studies show this increases your chances of achieving them, and you can look back later to see how far you've come.
- Tell people: telling friends or your coach provides accountability; a coach can also direct you towards the goal and give feedback on it.
- **Warning:** Don't set too many goals: too many goals in too many areas may conflict with each other and lower your chances of achieving them, e.g. goals in running, skiing and climbing at the same time.
