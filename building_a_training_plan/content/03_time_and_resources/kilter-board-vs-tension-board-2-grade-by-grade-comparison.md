# Kilter Board vs Tension Board 2: Grade-by-Grade Comparison

**Type:** Gear & boards · **Trains:** Finger strength, Power, Technique

**TL;DR:** At 40°, the Tension Board grades more consistently, with a much smoother grade progression, and demands more hand strength, accuracy and intentional movement. The Kilter is comfortable, about 1.5 to 2 grades soft, has many more climbs in the V0 to V6/7 range, and trains movement variety, power and big spans. From V0 to about V8 either board gives similar results over a realistic time frame; for harder climbs, lofty ambitions or hard, steep outdoor bouldering, bias towards the Tension Board. Neither replaces a home wall built to your needs, but most of the time either beats a Moonboard or a generic spray wall.

## How the boards were compared
- Both boards at a fixed 40° (the Tension Board 2 was fixed, so the Kilter was kept at 40° too).
- Climbs: classics/benchmarks (Kilter: highly rated climbs) chosen to look fun and varied, a few per grade from V0 upwards.

## Grade by grade
| Grade | Kilter | Tension Board |
|---|---|---|
| V0 | Juggy and easy to hold, but so incut they are a bit reminiscent of a crimp; realistic V0s that most people who climb regularly could do. Slightly nicer for sheer comfort | Juggy and easy to hold, but so incut they are a bit reminiscent of a crimp; realistic V0s that most people who climb regularly could do |
| V1 | Quite a bit harder than V0; still reasonably welcoming and accessible | Quite a bit harder than V0; still reasonably welcoming and accessible |
| V2 | Clearly easier than the V1 | — |
| V3 | Holds still pretty juggy but rounder: more engaging, but soft for V3 | Looked significantly harder |
| V4 | Holds very incut but comfortable, no tweaky moves so far; not much harder than before: a Kilter V4 could reasonably be graded V2. Still fairly welcoming | Clearly getting harder; around V4 it starts to get difficult for a lot of people |
| V5–V6 | Still felt like V3 or V4; the holds are too good for V6. The holds vary but all hit the same spot of the fingers, which quickly started to aggravate the skin | Each grade inarguably harder than the previous one, without extreme jumps. The V6 was the first climb demanding accuracy, tension, dynamic movement and power, and a variety of grip strength |
| V7 | Bigger moves, edgier holds, slippy feet. Holds and feet forgiving enough to experiment with momentum; very ergonomic, no real risk of tweaking anything | You actually have to crimp. The wood feet skate easily if you're not paying close attention, even the ones that aren't small. Good variety and options for climbers of different sizes. Spot-on for V7 |
| V8 | Still unrealistic for V8, too easy, but distinctly harder than the previous ones, so there is a reasonable progression. Comfortable holds; it paced the movement, started asking questions and nudged towards a good solution | Demands more focus: punishes you if you're not focused and intentional |
| V9 | Rounder, less juggy holds as climbs get harder are pleasant; the jugs bite more | Some holds are just the right size to stab into the finger creases and are unnecessarily sharp. A second V9 was potentially soft and much more comfortable |
| Above V10 | — | At 40° only six classics, only one V11, and they are a large jump up from the V10s |

- Grades felt surprisingly similar between the two boards up to V2.
- **Warning:** on both boards the jump between V0 and V1 is a concern for newer climbers.
- From V3 to V6, the Kilter holds stayed comfortable and ergonomic as problems progressed, while the Tension Board demanded more accuracy, power and finger strength as grades increased. Over this range the Tension Board did a better job of offering a consistent grade progression; the Kilter's problems were fun, but the grades fluctuated a bit more.
- Up to V9 the Kilter is about 1.5 to 2 grades soft, but since it keeps getting harder, this doesn't matter as long as there are enough climbs at the top end.
- **Warning:** on the Tension V9, one move became tweaky when the pinky came off (rotating the foot made it much easier), and the undercling split the pinky crease on the first try.

## Board comparison
| | Kilter | Tension Board |
|---|---|---|
| Lighting | More light problems than on other boards (a patch of lights out, some holds dimmer or brighter than others; seen on other boards too), annoying on a board costing around $20,000 | — |
| Reading the climb | Feels more like onsighting: from the ground most holds look the same and are hard to read (except some pinches) | You can tell much better what you are getting into |
| What it trains | A balance of hand/grip strength with physical pulling and body-tension power | Also taxes bigger muscles and tension, but the crux has mostly been hand strength; demands more finger and upper-body strength |
| Style | Gives options: lets you feel things out and decide how to do a move. Forgiving, with holds that let you experiment and use creative footwork | Asks more questions: punishes you if you're not focused and intentional |
| Grading | Fairly consistently almost two grades soft; problems skew slightly easier and feel more ergonomic overall | More consistent, with a much smoother grade progression; grades progress logically |
| Climb density | Much more density in the V0 to V6/7 range, possibly a benefit for some climbers | — |
| Overall | Surprisingly fun and comfortable; adds movement variety, power and big-span generation, which are good to practise and rehearse, especially with moves that big | Gets the overall verdict |

- Neither is better for what it trains: it depends on your goals. Both have pros and cons.
- The Kilter is worth more training time than previously thought.

## Which board for which purpose
| Purpose | Board |
|---|---|
| Pushing your limits or simulating outdoor cruxes | Tension Board: a better training tool |
| Hard, steep outdoor bouldering | Bias towards the Tension Board |
| Newer intermediate climbers learning and practising momentum | Kilter V7s: holds and feet are forgiving enough to experiment with that type of movement, and very ergonomic, with no real risk of tweaking anything |
| Training big spans and dynamic skills | Kilter: enjoyable, diverse movement |
| Overall enjoyment, movement variety and a more ergonomic session | Kilter |
| Newer climbers | The Kilter's density in the V0–V6 range might be ideal |
| Newer climbers in the V0 to about V8 range | Either board: probably similar results over a realistic time frame |
| Harder climbs or lofty ambitions | Tension Board: the top end of what you can achieve is probably higher (people who grew up climbing mostly on Tension Boards are now climbing at the very top grades), assuming nothing else differs |
| Climbing V10+ or aiming for elite outdoor performance | The Tension Board may help you get there |

- Climbing only on a Tension Board (or something similar) would remove much of the need to hangboard.

## Grades
- Grades on boards are not clearly differentiated: an 8 can be harder than a 10 and also easier than a 6.
- People often get stuck at a grade (V1, V5, V9 …) and hesitate to try harder climbs until they have "built their pyramid" or "earned it". You don't need to earn the right to try harder climbs.
- When getting into board climbing, bounce around a fair amount and try things: be guided by grades at most, but not beholden to them. Trying a variety of grades can help you improve faster.
- A board being a bit soft or a bit hard doesn't matter: you can acclimate to it. Inconsistent grades do matter: it's like a weightlifting gym where nothing is labelled, so you want to bench 61 kg or 90.5 kg but don't know whether you are loading 11.5 kg or 20.5 kg plates. Consistency, whether the grading is high or low, is what counts.

## Same board, different gym
- Boards are meant to be identical from gym to gym worldwide, but they aren't: holds are hard to make exactly the same, setters interpret the layout differently, holds may be shallow-cast or not turned quite right, and the board angle may be slightly off (in some gyms certain holds are almost vertical when they should be at about 45°).
- This doesn't change the board's usefulness. If you climb a grade lower on another gym's board, don't feel bad about it.

## Reach
- Reach example (Kilter V8): with a 1-11 reach, the foot is at 12, so you can't just stand on the starting hold unless you are quite tall, at least 188 cm with a positive ape index.

## Moonboard, spray wall and home wall
- **Warning:** the Moonboard 2016 set is very tweaky, and a lot of people have blown pulleys on it. The Moonboard made these boards possible (proof of concept) and has clearly been used effectively by many people.
- Both the Kilter and Tension Board feel quite a bit more ergonomic than the Moonboards and offer much more variety. Most of the time either is preferable to a Moonboard, though it is partly preference and what you have access to.
- Both boards are preferable to a generic spray wall in a gym, unless the gym has done a particularly good job with it. Of the standard boards available, choose one of these two.
- A home wall crafted to exactly fit your needs beats any general model, so neither board replaces it.
- The wood holds force you to engage and be much more attentive.
