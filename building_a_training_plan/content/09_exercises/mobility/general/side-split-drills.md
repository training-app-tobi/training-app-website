# Side Split Drills: Hip Position, Muscles and End-Range Strength

**Type:** Exercise/protocol · **Trains:** Mobility

**TL;DR:** For the side split, roll the hips forward or externally rotate the legs so the femur doesn't jam against the hip joint; a straight knee stretches the gracilis more, a bent knee the other adductors. Train end-range strength like strength training: about 8 reps with a 3 s hold, with a weight that pushes you just a little deeper, about 3 sets per exercise, twice a week, 45 minutes at most. Four drills: tailor's pose, weighted wide squat, isometric side split (45–60 s holds, going wider as you progress) and side leg lift.

## How these drills work
- They are loaded progressive stretching: active training that builds strength through range, adding only enough load to push you a little deeper. The methods, and why flexibility training is individual, are in the note Flexibility Training Is Individual: Loaded Methods and How Long It Takes.

## Side split: hip position
- **Warning:** The most common mistake is lifting the leg directly to the side (pure hip abduction) with the hip neutral and the feet facing forward. The neck of the femur jams against the rim of the hip joint; bone on bone doesn't flex, so you can't get into a side split this way.
- To create space, change the orientation of the hip, in one of two equivalent ways:

| Option | How |
|---|---|
| Roll the hips forward | Arch the back, sit back slightly / stick the bum out; feet stay facing forward |
| Externally rotate the legs | Feet point up to the sky; pelvis unchanged |

- Feet up goes with hips back; feet forward goes with hips rolled forward.

## Side split: muscles and knee angle
- The side split mainly stretches the adductors on the inside of the leg; most of them attach along the inside of the femur up to the hip. One, the gracilis, crosses the knee and inserts on the tibia, so the knee angle changes which adductors are stretched:

| Knee angle | Emphasis |
|---|---|
| Straight leg | Locks the knee and stretches the gracilis relatively more |
| Bent knee | Relaxes the gracilis and puts more focus on the other adductors |

- A common misconception is that flexibility training should always be passive (relaxing into a held stretch). Active flexibility is the ability to lift yourself into a position. It matters in climbing because you need to lift your foot, reach, pull on holds and use your range of motion with force.

## End-range strength drills: how to dose them
Train end-range strength like strength training, with sets, reps and tempo.

| Variable | Dose |
|---|---|
| Reps | About 8 |
| Tempo | Hold the stretch position for 3 s on every rep, to add time in the end-range position |

- Choose the weight that pushes you just a little deeper than you get on your own (e.g. in tailor's pose when you can't quite get the knees down), so you contract through that last bit of range. Building strength and range there makes the body "feel safe" in the position, which then allows you to go a little deeper.
- **Warning:** Too much weight does the opposite: the body contracts too hard and doesn't relax (e.g. 40 kg each side in tailor's pose would be far too heavy and you'd end up higher). Find the sweet spot.
- Very flexible people: elevate the hips and feet so they can sink below floor level.

## Side split drills

| Drill | Knee angle | Position | Dose | Progression |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Tailor's pose | Fully bent | Sit in tailor's pose (knees bent, legs open) with a weight that pushes the knees just a little deeper | General dose above | — |
| 2. Weighted wide squat | Between bent and straight | Hold a dumbbell; feet facing forward, hips rolled forward (arch the back, bum up). Set the stance width by counting steps (e.g. 5) so it is repeatable. Keep the torso up; the tendency is to sink forward | General dose above | If you can lower your hip crease below your knees, the width is too easy; go to 7 steps, then 9, and so on, up to a full split. This progresses the drill from beginner to advanced |
| 3. Isometric side split | Straight | Hold the side split hands-free: hands away from the body, not on the thighs. Feet flat → hips rolled forward. Choose a width between too wide (can't hold it) and too narrow (too easy to hold for 60 s) | About 3 sets, 45–60 s hold (45 s is the usual intro) | When you reach 60 s, next session go a little wider (use markers on the floor) so you fail somewhere between 45 and 60 s; once you hold 60 s at the new width, go wider again |
| 4. Side leg lift | — | Kneel on all fours (quadruped), hip neutral, sole of the foot facing up. Lift the leg out to the side to touch a marker (e.g. a partner's hand), hold 3 s, lower. Without a partner, hang a gymnastics ring or a sling on a carabiner from a bar as the marker | 3 s hold per rep | — |

- Drill 4 trains the abductors: the glute medius, glute minimus and TFL run down the side of the leg and lift the leg out to the side; they are the muscles that lift you into a side split, e.g. when you put a foot on a high hold.

## Weekly dose
These drills are active training, like strength and conditioning, for strength in the end-range position.

| Variable | Dose |
|---|---|
| Sets | About 3 per exercise |
| Frequency | Twice a week |
| Session length | 45 minutes maximum |

- Not something to do every day (unlike the typical two-hour passive stretching session).
