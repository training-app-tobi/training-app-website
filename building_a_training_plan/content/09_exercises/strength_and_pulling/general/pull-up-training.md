# Pull-up Training: Max Strength, Capacity, Negatives, Lock-offs and Power Pull-ups

**Type:** Exercise/protocol, Planning · **Trains:** Pulling strength, Power · **For:** Beginner, Advanced

**TL;DR:** Max strength pull-ups use 3–4 reps at roughly 90% for 4–5 sets; capacity pull-ups use 8–12 reps for 3–4 sets; negatives follow the max strength model with a 2–3 second lowering; lock-offs start with 10-second holds, 4 sets at 90 degrees and 4 at 125 degrees; power pull-ups use 2–4 fast reps for 3–4 sets. Do pull-up sessions after climbing or on their own, never before, except power pull-ups, which often go before a climbing session. Start with once a week.

## Who should do what
| Situation / level | Do this |
|---|---|
| New exercise, or one you haven't done before | Once a week |
| Progressing in your training | Increase to twice a week; consider pairing two different exercises, e.g. lock-offs on one day and conventional pull-ups on the other |

## Max strength pull-ups
- Max strength pull-ups train maximum strength, recruiting the muscles to make really hard moves on the climb. Working at these high intensities is great for recruiting maximum strength.

| Reps per set | Sets | Intensity |
|---|---|---|
| 3–4 | 4–5; build up to this number of sets as your training progresses | The rep range sets the intensity, roughly 90%: add or remove weight so that you can do 3–4 reps |

- Adding weight: many climbers have a harness, and hanging weight from it works. With a lot of weight, use a dipping belt or weight belt instead, because it spreads the load better.
- Removing weight (if you need it to complete 3–4 reps): use a pulley system or a few resistance bands wrapped around the bar.

## Capacity pull-ups
- At times it is also important to build your capacity to do bodyweight pull-ups.

| Reps per set | Sets | Intensity |
|---|---|---|
| 8–12 | 3–4 (reduced because the rep range is higher) | The rep range sets the intensity: adjust the load to match |

## Negative pull-ups
- In a negative pull-up the muscle works eccentrically (it lengthens), so it can tolerate higher forces.
- Negatives are great for beginners who can't yet do a conventional pull-up at bodyweight, and also useful for advanced climbers with pull-up experience who want to break a plateau.
- Technique: the focus is the lowering phase. Use a chair or bench to get into the top position, gripping the bar with the chin at bar level. Lower the body in a controlled way. At the bottom, use the chair or bench to get back to the start position.

| Reps per set | Sets | Intensity |
|---|---|---|
| 3–4 | 4–5 | Set by the rep range, and by the lowering speed: aim for a slow eccentric of 2–3 seconds. If you can't keep this slow pace, reduce the load |

## Lock-offs
- Lock-offs are static (isometric) contractions; as with negatives, the muscles can produce more force this way than in conventional pull-ups.
- Lock-off strength develops at the specific angle trained: lock-offs trained at 90 degrees transfer well to using that position on the wall.
- Because strength is angle-specific, train more than one angle: 90 degrees, and 120–125 degrees (a straighter elbow).
- **Warning:** Avoid training very deep lock-offs, especially beginners, because of the extra stress on the elbow: lock-off training generally works at higher forces.
- Start each lock-off at the target joint angle: don't jump into the lock-off; use a chair or bench to get into the angle you want to train.
- No reps (isometric): the dose is time under tension, i.e. how long you hold the position, as in fingerboard training with 5- or 10-second holds.

| Hold | Sets | Load |
|---|---|---|
| 10 seconds (a good starting point) | 4 sets at 90 degrees, followed by 4 sets at 125 degrees | The hold time sets the load: for 10-second holds, roughly 80–90% of your maximum, so that you have a few seconds in reserve |

- As with fingerboarding, change the hold time for the outcome you want:

| Hold time | Outcome |
|---|---|
| Shorter, e.g. 5 seconds | Works more on maximum strength |
| Longer, 20 or even 30 seconds | Develops capacity, i.e. muscular endurance |

## Power pull-ups
- The key focus of power pull-ups is velocity, so they transfer best of all these exercises to dynamic moves on the wall.
- Setup and movement pattern are like a conventional pull-up, but the intention is to produce as much power and speed as possible in the concentric (pulling) phase, getting at least the chest to the bar. If you can't reach chest height, use a resistance band for assistance. Lower at a slower rate and keep the shoulders engaged throughout.

| Reps per set | Sets | Intensity |
|---|---|---|
| As many as you can keep the velocity, generally 2–4 before it starts to drop | 3–4 | Velocity decides the number of reps. Once velocity drops there is little point continuing, because you are no longer training velocity |

## Where to place the sessions
| Session | Placement | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Pull-up sessions (all except power pull-ups) | After climbing (while still in a good state and well rested) or on their own, never before your climbing training | Most of these sessions aim at maximum strength, so you need to be fresh to do them at high quality; at the same time they are less specific than your climbing sessions |
| Power pull-ups | Often before a climbing session, where they can help potentiate (recruit) power for the climbing that follows | They must be done at such high velocity that even a small amount of fatigue hurts their quality; because the rep range is low, they don't generate much fatigue |

## Choosing an exercise
- The sessions serve different aims, from building the starting strength for a first conventional pull-up to developing power for dynamic moves and competition climbing.
- To choose where to start, look at your main goals in climbing, pick the most specific exercise for them and start with that one.
