# Finger Strength Exercises Ranked: From Max Hangs to Board Climbing

**Type:** Exercise/protocol, Principles · **Trains:** Finger strength, Power, Power endurance, Endurance · **For:** Advanced, injured or returning

**TL;DR:** Board climbing, max hangs and off-the-ground block lifts rank highest (S tier); one-arm hangs, frequency hangs added to other training and repeaters follow (A). Max hangs: half crimp, about 7 to 10 seconds, at a submaximal 80 to 95%, starting with about 5 seconds in reserve and dropping to about 2 seconds the next week. Small edges, density hangs and 7-53 sit in the middle; campus boarding, single-finger work, twice-daily hangboarding on its own and pull-ups on edges rank lowest, because most people don't need them, they are easy to get wrong or they carry more risk.

## Ratings
| Exercise | Tier | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Max hangs | S | — |
| Minimum edge depth (small edges) | B | Very beneficial for some people |
| Hangboarding twice a day, on its own | C or D | Without something like max hangs for higher recruitment |
| Frequency hangs, in addition to other training | A | Combined with max hangs it could be S, but A |
| Campus board | C | — |
| Block lifts / no-hangs | S | Not above max hangs, because hanging is better for climbing than lifting |
| Single-finger lifts or hangs | C | Most people don't need it and it is easy to get wrong |
| Density hangs | Borderline A/B | — |
| Pull-ups on edges | D | — |
| One-arm hangs | A | Exclusive: like max hangs, for the right level of athlete |
| 7-53 | B | Very simple, but it doesn't build as much strength as max hangs with longer rest |
| Repeaters | A | Used much less often than off-the-ground lifts or max hangs |
| Board climbing | S | — |

## Max hangs
| Grip | Hang time | Intensity |
|---|---|---|
| Half crimp | Around 5 to 12 seconds depending on who you ask, usually about 7 to 10 seconds | Submaximal: 80 to 95% |

- Short duration to build maximum strength, but prescribed at a submaximal level.

| Week | Reserve |
|---|---|
| Start | Around 5 seconds in reserve (e.g. hang 10 seconds while feeling you could hang about 5 more) |
| Next week | Around 2 seconds in reserve |

- Used with pretty much all athletes at some point in their training. It can be done in pretty much any training cycle because it builds maximum strength, and it works at the lower levels as well.
- It creates little fatigue: only around 60 seconds of hanging in a whole workout.
- Max hangs work across all grip positions.
- One of the most cited research studies on hangboard training used max hangs on a large edge of about 18 to 20 mm.

## Minimum edge depth (small edges)
- **Warning:** small edges can make fingers a bit more tweaky. That doesn't necessarily mean injury, but the fingers can't take as much load for as long a period.
- Use small edge depths only for short periods: leading up to a trip or performance, or briefly with a competition athlete to make sure they are adapted. Don't do it for too long.
- Use it only if you need to climb on small edges. Many people going on sport climbing trips, or climbing steep terrain (e.g. on sandstone), don't need small edges, so it won't be in their plan at all.
- Effects: good for the skin pulp (you build a fatty pulp under the skin that prepares it for smaller edges, and this needs only a couple of weeks of training); gets the fingers ready for that very specific grip type; changes the way the forearms work.
- Most effective 4 to 6 weeks before a peak. It gets buy-in because it feels very specific.
- Who it's for: only those who really need to train it. It tends to be for high-level athletes, because you rarely climb on edges of 10 mm or less unless you climb at a relatively high level, or on vertical terrain, where you probably don't need that much finger strength on a small edge anyway.
- **Warning:** it can be aggressive on the joints, particularly if you are quite hypermobile: on anything smaller than 10 mm you end up in a very high-angle crimp position to keep purchase.

## Hangboarding twice a day (frequency hangs)
- The research study on twice-daily hangboarding concluded that the frequency doesn't need to be twice a day or that high.
- **Warning:** most people climb a few times a week and may also do other fingerboard methods like max hangs, so the frequency needs to be massively reduced: don't hang twice a day on top of all of that.
- Loading the tendons at any intensity produces adaptation, and there is a 6-hour window before you can load them again. So consider what you have done in the prior 6 hours before touching a fingerboard; for most people, climbing already gives the fingers and forearms a lot of good conditioning.

If you climb 2 to 4 times a week and also do max hangs:

| Day | Frequency hangs |
|---|---|
| Rest days | Twice per day |
| Climbing days | Once per day, in the part of the day when you are not climbing |

- You can use multiple grip types to acclimatize to different grips. Example: full crimp and front-three full crimp leading into a local peak climbing season, to get used to a disliked grip position; the load is so light that it mainly gets the fingers used to the position, a learning effect.

## Campus board
- Good for buy-in for a short period, because you hit a ceiling quickly. It's fun; give it only to people who are really psyched on it.
- You get the same from a board, which is far better for your climbing. Any campusing is done exclusively on a board or a climbing wall: campusing has a skill element, so doing it on a board transfers better to climbing performance.
- **Warning:** people often campus far too soon, before they can generate power through their arms to make it a powerful exercise: it ends up looking like lock-offs and snatching at holds while already decelerating away from the board. This brings too much risk for lower-level climbers. Don't start too soon: a common mistake is giving it to climbers without enough experience or finger strength.
- Many very high-level climbers still campus a lot, often as a check-in to see how powerful, good and recruited they feel. So it still has a use, more for buy-in and as a check-in than as a training method.
- If you do it, do it for a short period, until you reach your ceiling.

## Block lifts / no-hangs (off-the-ground lifts)
| | Block lifts compared with hanging |
|---|---|
| Advantage | No home setup needed (no hanging from the wall) |
| Advantage | Very easy to do |
| Advantage | Much easier to warm up with before going to the crag or the wall |
| Advantage | Much better for training new grip types, because you can control it |
| Advantage | Better for coming back from injury |
| Advantage | With any shoulder issues, much better than hanging |
| Downside | Doesn't translate as directly to climbing performance; hanging seems to translate a bit quicker (it takes a little time to get the shoulder back into use) |

- Used all the time with all athletes, from 9A boulderers to people just starting out trying their local project, because of buy-in and versatility (e.g. training different grip types).

## Single-finger lifts or hangs
- Single-finger work is more likely done as lifts than hangs. Used with many athletes, mostly professionals.
- **Warning:** the common mistake is testing it as a max test, or doing it too frequently.
- Very good for building injury robustness and the ability to use non-uniform holds. Indoors, holds are usually fairly uniform; outdoors, fingers are splayed and crimps sit in different positions, and this builds tolerance for that.
- Do it at low intensity and build up slowly; do it only when you are climbing outside more.

| Intensity | Hang per finger | Sets per finger |
|---|---|---|
| Low: 4 to 5 out of 10 effort | 5 seconds | 2 or 3 |

- Also used for recovery and rehab, e.g. on a ring finger to help its recovery.

## Density hangs
- A long-duration hang of about 30 to 45 seconds. Meant to benefit tendon adaptation (tendon thickness and strength). Whether it always does exactly that is uncertain, but it has been very useful for developing robustness in a given grip position, possibly through those structural changes.
- Some athletes do density hangs the day before high-intensity performance (e.g. max bouldering): it gets them recruited and makes the fingers feel very good. Why this works (e.g. effects on forearm stiffness) is unclear.
- Useful for coming back from injury.
- You don't need much weight, so there is little hassle.

| Step | Hang |
|---|---|
| 1 | 5 seconds |
| 2 | 10 seconds |
| 3 | 15 seconds |
| 4 | 20 seconds |
| 5 | 30 seconds, then sets of 30 seconds |

- This progression gave a very good strength outcome (strongest ever), possibly because the tendons felt very robust.
- **Warning:** for many people a 30-second hang needs assistance. Use density hangs only when you can comfortably hang 30 seconds at bodyweight or more; otherwise the entry level is high and a pulley system is a hassle.

## Pull-ups on edges
- **Warning:** pull-ups on edges have caused tendinitis and elbow problems ("soft tennis elbow") in athletes, which calls for caution (based on experience rather than data).
- They have a place if you have developed a lot of strength and want to make it more applicable before going outside, but never for a long period.
- **Warning:** if you do them, avoid going all the way to the top: the acute, tight elbow angle at the top is where the elbow issues arise.
- Light pulls on edges are sometimes used, like small-edge training, to get familiar with moving on pockets or certain grip types; heavy half-crimp edge pulls are not used.

## One-arm hangs
- If you can't hang 150% of bodyweight on two arms, don't move on to one arm (a generalized figure, but seen across the board).
- If you can do one-arm hangs, they are very good for developing shoulder strength, training the whole chain (fingers to shoulder) working together, which matters more at higher levels. They also avoid hanging on two arms with lots of weight around the waist, which is awkward.
- They have a ceiling: once you can hold the position on one arm with 10 kg added, you have more or less maxed out their usefulness.

## 7-53 protocol
| Protocol | Hang | Rest |
|---|---|---|
| 7-53 | 7 seconds | 53 seconds |
| Equivalent | 10 seconds | 50 seconds |
| Normal max hangs | — | 2 to 3 minutes |

- A max hang protocol, like repeaters but with a long rest.
- Useful when impatient: much easier, just keep a timer running and go on the minute.
- Can be used for warm-ups: do all warm-up hangs at 7-53, then go into max hangs.

## Repeaters
- Repeaters: hang, rest, hang, rest for a number of sets, e.g. 5-2s (5 seconds on, 2 off), which is close to the duty cycle of bouldering. The key is changing the intensity for the goal.

| Goal | Intensity | Volume |
|---|---|---|
| Critical force, aerobic capacity or aerobic threshold | Low | Long durations (several minutes of repeaters) |
| Strength (90% repeaters) | 90%, very high | 3 to 5 reps |

| Order in one session | Exercise |
|---|---|
| 1 | Max hangs (recruitment) |
| 2 | 90% repeaters |

- This high-intensity workout also does a lot for forearm hypertrophy.
- Repeaters are very applicable to climbing, but if you do lots of climbing in the week they are rarely needed. They don't replace a session, but if you can train 4 times a week and can only get to the wall 3 times, do repeaters as well as, or instead of, hangs.
- When used, mostly in the anaerobic capacity range; lower-intensity aerobic capacity work is better done on the wall when possible.
- If you have to hang below bodyweight, setting up a pulley is a hassle.

## Board climbing
- Board climbing is the best: it trains your climbing and your fingers, and it's fun. Few high-level professional climbers would disagree; some of them find no use for max hangs or fingerboarding except warming up, and build their finger strength almost exclusively on boards.
- **Warning:** that is not the case for everyone: don't conclude you should only board climb. Board climbing on small edges for finger strength is high intensity, with more risk from foot slips and snatchy moves to holds where the fingers can end up in split grips. Don't go straight to it and only it to develop finger strength.
- If you could only do one thing, climb: it develops more than finger strength.
