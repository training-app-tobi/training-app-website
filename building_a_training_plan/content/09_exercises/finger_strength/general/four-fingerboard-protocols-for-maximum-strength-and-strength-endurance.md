# Four Fingerboard Protocols for Maximum Strength and Strength-Endurance

**Type:** Exercise/protocol · **Trains:** Finger strength, Power endurance · **For:** Intermediate, Advanced

**TL;DR:** Finger strength-to-weight ratio is the central physical attribute for climbing better. Beginners shouldn't fingerboard train; experienced climbers with no recent finger, elbow or shoulder injury can use one maximum-strength protocol twice a week as a supplement to climbing, with no more than four rigorous climbing plus finger training days per week. Start with Minimum Edge (12-second hangs at bodyweight), move to weighted hangs on 14–20 mm edges (10-second or 7-53 protocol), and use weighted 7/3 repeaters as a bridge from maximum strength to endurance.

## Who should do what
| Situation / level | Do this |
|---|---|
| Beginner | Don't fingerboard train. Get steady gains in apparent strength simply by climbing a few days per week, optimizing body composition and working on movement and mental skills week over week |
| Beginner and intermediate | Raise strength-to-weight ratio fastest by improving body composition (reducing unwanted muscle and excessive fat, within reason) and by improving quality of movement (apparent strength gains through more efficient climbing) |
| Experienced and elite (high climbing skill, good base fitness) | Improve finger strength-to-weight ratio by training to increase the force the finger flexors can apply to a hold; this is the only way left, and the fingerboard is the best tool for it |
| Experienced, no recent history of finger, elbow or shoulder injury | Use one of the maximum-strength protocols twice a week, as a supplement to climbing |

## Finger strength-to-weight ratio
- Relative peak finger force (the finger force you can apply to a hold in relation to the body mass the fingers must support) is the central physical attribute for climbing better. It improves by increasing finger strength or by lowering body mass.
- **Warning:** Excessive finger strength training is unwise and potentially injurious in a climber's formative months/years: finger tendons take many months (perhaps years) to adapt to the stresses of climbing.

## Frequency and limits
- **Warning:** Total rigorous climbing plus finger training days must not exceed four per week. Rest the fingers at least three days per week for neuromuscular recovery and finger tendon remodeling. Breaking this rule likely leads to injury.

## Warm-up
- Always warm up thoroughly before any of the protocols: ideally a general full-body activity to raise heart rate, or 15–30 minutes of easy climbing or intermittent non-maximal bouldering.
- At home with no wall: jumping jacks, a few sets of pull-ups on jug holds, then a few bodyweight hangs on a variety of fingerboard holds.
- Finish with mild stretching and massage of the forearm flexors and extensors.

## Minimum Edge protocol (maximum strength)
- A low-volume, entry-level fingerboard program: entry-level for fingerboarding, not a program for beginner climbers. A few multi-week cycles bring noticeable finger-strength gains for intermediate climbers and prepare for weighted hangs.
- It produces little or no pump; it mainly targets the anaerobic alactic energy system.
- Setup: a fingerboard with many different-size edges and pockets. Experiment to find the features you can hang on for only about 15 seconds. Hangs stop a few seconds before grip failure, so each hang is exactly 12 seconds.

| Element | Protocol |
|---|---|
| Hold | A feature you can barely hold for 15 seconds with maximum effort |
| Hang | 12 seconds |
| Rest between hangs | Exactly 3 minutes |
| Hangs per set | 5 |
| Effort | Near maximal (RPE 9–9.5 out of 10), but not to failure |
| Rest between sets | 5 minutes after the first set, then a second set of 5 hangs; at least 5 minutes between sets |
| Grips, initially | Half-crimp and open-crimp, one set each |
| Grips, advanced climbers | Add sets on two-finger pockets (open-hand grip) and pinch grip |

## Maximum Weight 10-second protocol (maximum strength)
- When to switch: after a period of Minimum Edge training you end up needing tiny, painful edges to stay within the protocol. The maximum-weight protocols use larger, more comfortable holds with added weight to reach the intensity needed for maximum-strength adaptations.
- Edge size for weighted hangs: 14–20 mm, a little less than one finger-pad depth. This size lessens skin pain, reduces strain on the distal finger joint and has been shown to build maximum strength that carries over to different-size edges on rock.
- Do 2–5 sets of either this protocol or the 7-53 protocol, not both, mainly on half-crimp or open-crimp grips. Advanced climbers may dedicate a few sets to pocket and pinch grips.
- Added weight is significant: generally 11.5 kg up to 45.5 kg for elites. Use a heavy weight vest, several weight belts, or free-weight plates hung from the harness belay loop. Plates on the harness are best for high loads, because they unclip easily to de-load between hangs and sets.

| Element | Protocol |
|---|---|
| Hold | A feature you can barely hold for 13 seconds with maximum effort; adjust weight to meet this |
| Hang | 10 seconds |
| Rest between hangs | Exactly 3 minutes |
| Hangs per set | 5 |
| Effort | Near maximal (RPE 9–9.5 out of 10), but not to failure |
| Rest between sets | At least 5 minutes, then a second set of 5 hangs |
| Load | Adjust added weight for each set and grip to stay within these guidelines |
| Grips, initially | Half-crimp and open-crimp, one set each |
| Grips, advanced climbers | Add sets on two-finger pockets (open-hand) and pinch grip |

## Maximum Weight 7-53 protocol (maximum strength, more advanced)
- A more advanced maximum-weight protocol that trains maximum strength and builds aerobic power: the 53-second rests stress the muscles to increase the rate of CP resynthesis.
- Time-efficient when doing multiple sets.
- Holds: 14–20 mm edges, 20–30 mm two-finger pockets, and deep, tendon-friendly monos if you're strong enough.

| Element | Protocol |
|---|---|
| Hold | A feature you can barely hold for 10 seconds with maximum effort |
| Load | Enough weight to make a 10-second hang extremely difficult |
| Hang | 7 seconds (a few seconds before failure) |
| Rest between hangs | Exactly 53 seconds, so each hang-rest couplet takes exactly 1 minute |
| Hangs per set | 3 |
| Effort | Near maximal (RPE 9–9.5 out of 10), but not to failure |
| Rest between sets | 3–5 minutes after the first set, then a second set of 3 hangs; at least 5 minutes between sets |
| Maximum | 5 sets |
| Grips, initially | Half-crimp and open-crimp, one set each |
| Grips, advanced climbers | A second set for each of half- and open-crimp, or add sets on two-finger pockets (open-hand grip) and pinch grip |

## 7/3 Repeaters (strength-endurance)
- Repeaters mimic the grip-relax sequence of climbing. Most repeater routines are done at bodyweight and high volume, so they train endurance far more than strength.
- This version uses added weight and limits each set to about 1 minute, making it a bridge between maximum-strength training and endurance training.
- Grips: pick 3–7 grip types. Besides half-crimp and open-crimp, choose others such as open hand, two-finger pocket middle pair, two-finger pocket inside pair, wide pinch and narrow pinch.

| Element | Protocol |
|---|---|
| Set | 6 hang-rest intervals of 7-second hang and 3-second rest (about 1 minute per set) |
| Load | Add weight so each hang is challenging but you can still complete the set; much less weight than in the maximum-weight protocols |
| Rest between sets | 3 minutes |
| Sets per grip | 1 (entry-level) to 3 (advanced) |

## Technique on the fingerboard
- Train with good technique on every protocol: keep tension through shoulders and upper torso by engaging the scapular stabilizers and rotator cuff ("shoulders down, chest out").
- **Warning:** Never hang with relaxed, passive shoulders and a hollow chest.
- Lift the knees only slightly to build core stiffness.
- **Warning:** Never train with the full-crimp grip (thumb lock).
- Train the scapular stabilizers and rotator cuff twice a week.
