# Lifting Edge Training: Benefits, Form, Three Sessions and Advanced Uses

**Type:** Exercise/protocol, Session · **Trains:** Finger strength, Power endurance · **For:** injured or returning, Advanced

**TL;DR:** A lifting edge is more controllable than a fingerboard (smaller weight increments, shoulders not limiting), easier to warm up with and portable; its main uses are training new grip types and coming back from injury by increasing the load systematically and slowly. Lift with the weight between your legs, standing up through the legs with straight arms, and lower before the hand opens out. Three sessions: max holds (about 10 seconds per arm, 9 out of 10 RPE, 4–8 sets), lifts (5 lifts per arm, 4–8 sets) and density holds (30 seconds per side, 3–4 sets), always building up over several sets first.

## Advantages over a fingerboard
| Advantage | Detail |
|---|---|
| More controllable and more precise | You can use smaller increments of weight, and shoulder strength is not a limiting factor, so you can gain more finger-strength benefit than from climbing alone or from fingerboarding |
| Much easier to warm up with | Anecdotal, but reported by many athletes: you can build up the weights really slowly and make sure you are ready to pull harder in the session, compared with hanging at bodyweight on a fingerboard or ramping up the weight while hanging |
| Very transportable | Take it when travelling, on work trips or to the crag to warm up, so you can use the same edge day in, day out |

## Main uses
- Training new grip types: the loads can be kept much lower and it is more controllable, so you can train the specific grip types needed for a project and improve more slowly.
- Recovering from injury: come back by increasing the load really systematically and slowly, instead of starting from a bodyweight hang on a fingerboard and building up from there.

## Limitations
| Limitation | Detail |
|---|---|
| You need quite a lot of weight | Especially when training on a bigger edge; some people struggle with this at home, so invest in weights if you want to do it at home |
| Slower transfer to the wall | Some athletes find it takes a little longer to translate finger strength from a lifting edge to the wall, usually because of the connection between the fingers and the shoulder girdle and the rest of the body; it just takes a little time, and the benefits generally outweigh this downside |

## Lifting form
- Lift with the weight between your legs, not at your side: it is far more stable and lets you lift with a much more stable lower back.
- Set the weight at the right height so you are not working from the shoulders but lifting from the legs: straight back, wide feet so the weight doesn't land on your feet, stand up through the legs, back steady and shoulder at the same height.
- **Warning:** Avoid lifting purely from the back: once the loads get much heavier it effectively becomes a deadlift. Not the worst thing, but it is far easier to let the big leg muscles do the work.
- **Warning:** Avoid lifting with the shoulder: this usually happens when the load is too high (the lifting pin too far off the ground), so the shoulder does the work. As you go up, the shoulder stays exactly the same at all times.
- **Warning:** Avoid jolting: more chance of things going wrong, e.g. slipping or the carabiner moving on the weight. Preload slightly, as in any lifting exercise, so there is tension in the equipment, then pull.
- **Warning:** Lower with relative control: not necessarily slowly, but don't just drop it, which upsets the weights and raises the chance of things going wrong or of the weight landing on your feet.
- **Warning:** As with fingerboarding, don't let the fingers open out or slip on the hold. If the hand opens out, the grip position has failed: lower to the ground. When the load feels heavy enough that the hand wants to open, lower it before that happens; this protects the fingers and reduces injury risk.

## Workout types on a lifting edge
- Isometric holds work like fingerboarding, with the same lengths and durations.
- Unique to the lifting edge: repetitions. Lift off the ground and drop immediately; this spikes the load in the fingers up and down, very similar to what happens in climbing.
- Lift repetitions follow the basic structures of other training.

| Workout | Protocol |
|---|---|
| Max hangs | Lift and hold 10 seconds |
| Short hangs | Lift and hold 5 seconds |
| Repeaters | 7 seconds on, 3 seconds off |
| Lift repetitions for strength or max strength | 3–5 lifts |
| Lift repetitions for strength endurance of the forearms | More than 10 lifts in a row |

## Workout 1: max holds
| Element | Detail |
|---|---|
| Hold | Like max hangs: hold the weight off the ground for about 10 seconds on each arm per set |
| Rest | Rest between sides (switching sides straight away leaves you fatigued) |
| Load | The session's maximum load: about 9 out of 10 RPE |
| Sets | About 4–8 sets, depending on how much you want to train that session |

- Choosing the load: by testing, or by RPE, aiming for about 9 out of 10: trying really hard, a lot of effort to hold for 10 seconds, but you could hold on for a couple more seconds if you really had to.
- With max holds or any high load, build up over several sets to reach it; don't start at the maximum load. Once you hit the 9 out of 10 load, do the 4–8 sets.
- Example build-up (specific to one person on a certain edge depth; find what works for you):

| Load | Sets | Effort |
|---|---|---|
| 10 kg | A couple | – |
| 20 kg | A couple | – |
| 30 kg | A couple | – |
| 40 kg | 1 | About 7–8 out of 10 RPE |
| 45 kg | 4–8 | 9 out of 10 RPE: controllable but trying really hard |

## Workout 2: lifts
| Element | Detail |
|---|---|
| Set | A more maximal load: 5 lifts off the ground, rest, then the same on the other arm |
| Each rep | Lift until the legs are straight, pause for a second, lower, pause at the bottom with the weight on the floor, repeat |
| Load | As in the max-hold session, build up slowly over several sets to a load where the fifth rep feels really hard but you could lift it a couple more times if you had to |
| Sets | About 4–8 sets of these lifts |

- **Warning:** Keep the arms straight: don't bend the arm and work the biceps; it's all about the fingers.

## Workout 3: density holds
| Element | Detail |
|---|---|
| Hold | 30-second hold, rest, repeat on the other side |
| Sets | Only 3–4 sets, after building up slowly |
| Grip | Whatever grip position you want to train (example: open three, a comfortable grip that trains what is needed) |

- Density holds build tendon stiffness, as on a fingerboard, but standing while holding the position keeps the work on the fingers and forearms, a nicer way than hanging on your shoulders for 30 seconds.
- It can be added at any point in the week: the load shouldn't be too high, but it is a good stimulus.

## Supersetting with gym lifts
- In a normal gym with compound lifts, take a lifting edge and a lifting pin and superset: train the fingers with the gym weights during the rest periods of compound movements such as bench press, pull-ups, deadlifts or rack pulls. Very time-efficient, gives a full-body stimulus, and uses those rest periods to work the forearms.

## Advanced uses
- For most professional climbers in trad, competition climbing and bouldering, most finger-strength training has been switched from fingerboarding to lifting, looking for very specific gains in well-trained athletes; it can also apply at home.
- Crag warm-up with a force gauge plus a lifting edge: if you know the load you can pull, then at the crag, with your phone and a sling, you can build up slowly and check whether you are ready to perform, instead of a rushed warm-up or an easy route or boulder first. This warms the fingers up specifically for every attempt on the climb.
- Developing specific grip positions, for professionals mostly pockets and open-hand positions. They are very strong on edges from lots of fingerboard training, but pockets feel uncomfortable, and when you are used to high loads or bodyweight hangs it is hard to drop back to barely any load through the fingers. With a lifting edge there is no baseline: for monos or pockets you start from scratch, build up and get a new measuring stick. Repeated with many athletes, the effect has been healthier, stronger hands, much more well-rounded in every grip type.
- If you have a standout weakness in a grip type, or want to get better at something very specific, use a lifting edge to develop it: no baseline, so you can build up slowly and find what works, become more robust to injury and more well-rounded as a climber.

### Mono lifts
- Single-finger lifts make professional athletes more robust to injury and to different grip types on non-uniform holds. Developing this strength takes a lot of time and control.

| Element | Detail |
|---|---|
| Grip positions | Half crimp and open position |
| Hold | About 5 seconds per finger |
| Intensity | Really low: don't push RPE to the highest loads |
| Progression | Develop it over several weeks |

- Effects: much more robustness to injury, and it can mean producing far more force when using all four fingers together.
- **Warning:** Be careful if you are not experienced: the mono method has been used with professional, well-trained athletes, so use your discretion and decide whether it is appropriate for you.
