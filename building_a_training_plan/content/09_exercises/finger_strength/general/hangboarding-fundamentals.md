# Hangboarding Fundamentals: Edge, Grip, Hang Time, Max Hangs and Repeaters

**Type:** Exercise/protocol, Planning · **Trains:** Finger strength, Power endurance · **For:** new to hangboarding

**TL;DR:** Hang mostly on two arms, four-finger half crimp, about 10 seconds. Max hangs: 6 sets at 80–90% of maximum with 2–3 minutes rest, load raised very slowly; repeaters at about 60–80%.

## Who should do what
| Situation / level | Do this |
|---|---|
| Fingerboard session new to you | Once a week, in isolation from climbing sessions, for good recovery |
| Long-time fingerboarders, or climbing little | Often at least 3–4 times a week, weighing wall finger work against fingerboard work |

## Edge and grip
- Micro edges: for very small crimps, later, when more experienced. 20 mm applies indoors and outdoors and still trains small edges. Bigger edges build forearms but apply less to the wall.
- Half crimp is king: little friction and the worst biomechanical position for joints and forearm muscles, so it takes the most strength. Beginners can go slightly more open or closed, without a thumb wrap.
- Mostly use four fingers. Train the grips your goal needs (pockets, really small edges): only using the four-finger half crimp makes the hand tight, so pockets feel like a new movement. Otherwise use general variety. Hand positions vary between people: use what is comfortable and gives the biggest gains.

## Hang duration
- Hangs typically last 5–30 seconds: shorter is much higher intensity; longer is lower intensity and trains endurance.
- Vary duration through the year for multiple stimuli; about 10 seconds is a good place to start.
- With a good climbing foundation and just starting to fingerboard: begin with longer, lower-intensity hangs (about 15–20 seconds) or repeaters, to build a foundation for later high-intensity training.

## One arm or two
- Two arms suit most athletes, even very high-level ones. Go one-arm only for something very specific, or when the added weight gets really uncomfortable.
- Not before you can hang on two arms with 150% of bodyweight (a 70 kg climber with 35 kg or more added), and not while your current protocol still brings gains.

## Hanging position
- Arms straight or slightly bent. To set the shoulders: hang fully relaxed, engage as high as possible, then sink back to the middle; film yourself to check.
- Keep abs and glutes fairly contracted so the lower back isn't doing all the work.

## Max hangs
| Element | Protocol |
|---|---|
| Build-up | After a general warm-up, 4–6 sets from a really low weight |
| Working sets | 6 × 10-second hangs at the target load |
| Rest | 2–3 minutes; longer if needed |
| Intensity | 80–90% of a max test done at the start of a cycle, no more than once every 1–2 months |
| Without testing | Effort 8–9 out of 10: good form, a little left in the tank |

- Still gaining week on week: keep it; many climbers reached a very high standard with it alone.
- Record loads and raise them in really small increments; plateaus are fine.
- **Warning:** Ramping up the weight quickly leads to a plateau and too much finger stress. Start lower than you want.
- Too fatigued: fewer sets or slightly less weight.

## Repeaters
- Load: roughly 60–80% of the max-test load, but it varies massively, so set it by practice: finish each set trying really hard but not failing, and adapt.
- Repeaters and long hangs can go after a high-intensity climbing session; max hangs can't.

## Timing and frequency
- **Warning:** Unfamiliar with these sessions, or doing high volume: don't fingerboard before a session with high-intensity climbing.
- Twice, maybe three times a week depending on style; one max hang plus one repeater session can be enough.
- Keep sessions relatively short. If unsure, talk to a coach.
