# Hangboarding Protocols for Beginners

**Type:** Exercise/protocol, Injury & health · **Trains:** Finger strength, Power endurance, Endurance, Power · **For:** Beginner, injured or returning

**TL;DR:** Hangboarding applies progressive overload to the fingers in a controlled, measurable way, so it can be a safe and effective way to build finger strength and injury resistance at any level, including for new climbers and those returning after a pulley or lumbrical injury. Beginners: start on a 20 mm edge, hangboard 1 to 2 times per week, before climbing, with the next day off the wall and at least 48 hours between finger sessions. Pick a goal and matching method, a grip, a board and an edge, then find a working weight you can hold without your form breaking down. Back off when the hand or fingers hurt.

## Who should do what
| Situation / level | Do this |
|---|---|
| Strength on small edges holding you back | Hangboard: it increases strength on smaller edges |

## When to start hangboarding
- Common opinions say to wait (climb for a year first, wait until V6, or never hangboard), based on the belief that hangboarding causes injuries in newer climbers.
- At any level, hangboarding creates overload that drives growth and strength, whether you have climbed all your life or started this year; the only difference is the starting point.
- So hangboarding can be a safe and effective way to improve finger strength and resistance to injury for all climbers, especially new climbers and those returning to the sport after a pulley or lumbrical injury.

## Benefits of hangboarding
- Every type of training has a risk-reward relationship. Finger strength matters but is not the only factor: a new climber gets most improvement from being on the wall, because climbing trains coordination, technique, strength of most of the body's muscles, comfort on different hold types and finger strength at once.
- Hangboarding increases climbing-specific grip strength in a controlled, measurable way; a 10-week hangboard protocol study showed increased finger strength.
- Progressive overload (train with a challenging load, adapt, increase the load, repeat) is one of the main principles of improvement. Hangboarding applies it in a specific, consistent, regulated way to all finger, hand and forearm tissues, increasing strength and tissue tolerance.
- Stimulating growth in commonly injured tissues can lower their injury risk if they are allowed to recover properly: thicker, more robust pulleys are less likely to get hurt at a given load.
- Evidence on hangboarding and finger pain: a study comparing climbers who hangboard with those who don't found no significant difference in finger pain, except in one group: males climbing 7a (hard V6) or harder who had been climbing less than 6 years, with a peak between 3 and 5 years of climbing. No other group showed this.
- Whether hangboarding itself is the cause is unknown; other variables could explain it: duration, frequency and intensity of hangboard sessions combined with climbing duration and frequency, training methods and grips used, and rest between sessions.

## How to hangboard safely
- Safe hangboarding comes down to understanding what you hang on (board and edge), how you hang (grip and training method) and when to back off.

## Types of boards
| Board | What it is | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hangboard | The most common finger-training tool; most have a variety of edge sizes and hold types. For beginner climbers, a pure hangboard can be too much load because there is no way to train lower than bodyweight. If you can't hang with good form on the edges available, using this alone (or with added weight) should be used as your finger tolerance increases. | Varied edges and hold types for continued challenge; hanging also challenges arm and shoulder muscles | Can't easily and objectively hang at less than bodyweight |
| Hangboard with pulleys | A pulley system takes weight off and makes hanging easier, gives objective numbers to track progress, and adds control when building up to bodyweight hangs on progressively smaller holds | Hang at any weight that is safe and challenging for you | A full pulley system is rare and may need a harness |
| Finger block ("no hangs") | Portable single-arm hangboard; you train the fingers by lifting instead of hanging | Portable, consistent challenge at a safe weight, train at your own pace, shows side-to-side differences | Different arm and shoulder challenge than hanging; needs good lifting form at higher loads to protect the back |
| Pinch block | A finger block designed for pinches, which are hard to train on most hangboards and finger blocks | Easiest and most effective way to train pinch grips off the wall | Niche equipment with only one function |

- No pulley system: hang to tolerance with your feet still on the floor, putting as much weight through the hands as possible. To make it objective, rate effort on the Modified Borg RPE scale (0–10 difficulty) or stand on a scale to see how much your hands lift you off the ground.

## Training methods
| Method | What it is | Trains | How to perform |
|---|---|---|---|
| Endurance hangs | Hanging until fatigue | Endurance | Many variants: grab a hold and hang in good form until you can't, or switch hands every few seconds, which resembles climbing. Duration depends on goals: unless you plan to climb multi-pitch, up to 5 minutes per set is plenty |
| Repeaters | Several hangs with very short rests | Power endurance | Hangboard: hold-rest-repeat for 1 minute, 3 sets, 4–5 minutes rest between sets; common variants 6:4 (6 s on, 4 s off for 1 minute) and 7:3. Finger or pinch blocks: hold-rest-repeat for 1 minute, rest 2 minutes, switch hands and repeat; 3 times per hand |
| Triple hangs | Like repeaters with more weight and much longer rests | Strength and power endurance (more strength, less endurance than repeaters) | 7–10 s on, 53–50 s off |
| Max hangs | The most weight you can hold in good form for at least 5 seconds | Maximal strength | 5–10 s hangs with long rests (about 5 minutes), 3 times, once you have built up to the desired weight |
| Contact strength (advanced) | Training rate of force development (how fast you can activate your strength); the most advanced form of finger training because the loads are higher than static hangs on the same edge | Rate of force development | Methods vary; one is to jump to a hold, hold it briefly, drop off and repeat. Often done on campus boards |

- **Warning:** Max hangs: stop when your form starts to fail, to prevent injuries.
- **Warning:** Contact strength: get hangboard experience before starting it.

## Edge size
- Beginners: start on a 20 mm edge.
- Larger edges can cover the DIP joint (the joint closest to the fingertips), which changes the mechanics of each grip.
- Smaller edges put much more load on the finger structures (the load–edge size relationship is not linear).
- This is a general tip: if you have a reason to use a different edge, do.

## Frequency
- Beginners: hangboard 1 to 2 times per week, depending on how often you climb.
- A finger session is more work than it feels: because it targets the fingers so precisely, it is comparable to a session on the wall as far as the fingers are concerned. So:
  - rest the fingers the next day (no climbing)
  - fingerboard before climbing (the morning before, or at the start of that day's climbing session)
- **Warning:** Training damages tissue so it can rebuild stronger; without time to rebuild, injury risk rises.

## When to back off
- **Warning:** Overtraining happens when training intensity exceeds your ability to recover; it raises injury risk and lowers performance. Pain in the hand or fingers is a sign that something is likely being overloaded in a negative way.

## The protocol
- There is no one-size-fits-all protocol: starting points, preferred climbing styles and goals differ, so experiment to find what fits; the steps below are a framework for building your own plan.

| Step | Do this |
|---|---|
| 1 | Choose your goal and the training method that matches it |
| 2 | Choose the grip to train; half crimp and 3-finger drag are probably the best return because they are the most versatile grips |
| 3 | Choose the board and the edge size |
| 4 | Find your working weight for the exercise (the most weight you can hold throughout the exercise without form breaking down); build up slowly with a finger block or a pulley system |
| 5 | Rest at least 5 minutes, try the last weight you hung successfully, rest again, try again |
| 6 | That completes the first fingerboard session; rest at least 48 hours before the next one |

- Your max hang can be the starting point for setting working weights.
