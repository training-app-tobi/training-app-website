# Technique Drills for Balance, Accuracy, Momentum, Pressure Distribution and Route Reading

**Type:** Exercise/protocol, Technique · **Trains:** Technique, Power

**TL;DR:** Five concepts apply to every technique in climbing: balance, accuracy, timing and momentum, pressure distribution, and prediction. Train each with two drills: one-handed slab climbing and hovering (balance), feet-on doubles and jumping to a hold (accuracy), dynamic one-handed climbing and dynos (timing and momentum), one foothold per move and moving feet or hands while the other pair stays fixed (pressure distribution), flashing boulders and eliminates (prediction). Working on these concepts makes movement more refined and efficient, which is different from just learning a single technique such as a heel hook or a drop knee.

## Who should do what
| Situation / level | Do this |
|---|---|
| Struggle with route reading | Do the flashing drill literally every single week, maybe even at the beginning of every session |

## Five concepts, two drills each
| Concept | Drill 1 | Drill 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Balance | One-handed climbing on a slab | Hovering on a steep wall |
| Accuracy | Feet-on doubles | Jump to a hold |
| Timing and momentum | Dynamic one-handed climbing | Dynos |
| Pressure distribution | One foothold per move | Move the feet, hands fixed (progression: move the hands, feet fixed) |
| Prediction | Flashing boulders | Eliminates |

- Two drills per concept help perfect technique across the whole spectrum.

## Balance
| Drill | How | Progression |
|---|---|---|
| One-handed climbing on a slab | Pick a slabby bit of wall and a climb well below your normal limit, and climb it with one hand, so you have to find the balance point before you can move to the next hold. Get your hips close to the wall so you are in balance before moving to the next hold; go in close and move slowly and in control to the next hold | Do a few boulders swapping hands and see whether it feels different; then challenge yourself on harder boulders |
| Hovering on a steep wall | Constrain your climbing by hovering over each hold: find the point of balance so you can hold yourself above the hold for a couple of seconds before taking it. To find those balance points you will probably have to explore several different techniques so that you can take a hand off and hover | — |

- A climb can be done dynamically or in a balanced way. Climbing dynamically isn't necessarily bad, but hovering is the constraint that teaches you to get into balance positions.
- If you normally climb quite dynamically, the hover drill forces you to learn new techniques such as twisting, drop knees and flags. Example: for a first move, find a tripod position: use the leg and drop the knee, get the hips close, then take the hand off.
- Balance is particularly important for climbs with small holds and for delicate, very technical climbing such as slab. Slow, methodical movement isn't always what climbing needs: you often have to move very fast and dynamically.

## Accuracy
- Accuracy is hand-eye coordination, hand-foot coordination and, importantly, coordinating where your centre of mass is at the beginning, at the end and throughout a movement.

| Drill | How | Progression |
|---|---|---|
| Feet-on doubles (for deadpoints) | Keep the feet on and go for two holds with both hands at the same time, so you have to generate the movement, look at the middle of the holds and aim to hit them. Builds hand-eye coordination and accuracy in hitting holds. Start steady, on something not too steep | Gets harder the more offset the holds are and the smaller the holds are; e.g. move on to a problem with much more offset and quite a bit smaller holds |
| Jump to a hold | Jump off the ground, using another hold as a bit of assistance, and aim to hit another hold accurately, then control the end position. Ideal: hit the hold accurately the first time without having to readjust | Make it more and more challenging by choosing smaller holds or blocked holds, e.g. start with a nice big hold, then a hold with a pocket in it where you have to aim and be really accurate, then a blocked hold where you must hit exactly the right part of it |

- Jump to a hold is a good drill for the end part of your warm-up: it activates the fingers and shoulders.

## Timing and momentum
- Timing and momentum link the other concepts together and can't exist without accuracy. They are about moving through space efficiently: generating momentum through the strong part of the body, and catching a hold with good timing so you can then create tension. All of these matter for efficient movement.

| Drill | How | Progression |
|---|---|---|
| Dynamic one-handed climbing | Unlike the slab drill (aimed at control), move dynamically and use momentum. Move only one hand (e.g. the right), which means all the momentum has to come from the legs: squat, power through the legs, get the hips close to the wall, then release and grab the next hold | — |
| Dynos | A dyno forces you to use momentum: drive through the legs and time it accurately so you can let go and grab the next holds. The aim is to go airborne, but start nice and comfortably (e.g. two good holds). Think about driving through the legs, letting go when the hips are close to the wall, and looking at the holds | Increase the distance, increase the difficulty of the holds you grab, or make them more offset (which needs more thought about timing). Get creative: aim for awkward-looking holds, or aim to plant a foot as well on landing |

- When to let go: make it efficient and don't snatch with the hand. Let go when you have control and are close to the wall: power up through the legs and let go when the hips are almost touching the wall, which gives you time to grab.
- Example: a jump where you have to roll over a foot makes you think about where you generate momentum and where the takeoff point of the move is. The trajectory is a semicircle: pull over the foot, then arc round, so you hit the holds in control.

## Pressure distribution
- Pressure distribution can't be seen clearly from outside: it is about how you balance tension in some parts of the body while relaxing others. Don't just tense everything: very skilful climbers isolate the fingers, maybe the core, and relax other parts at the same time to conserve energy. It also covers using the direction of pull on holds and maintaining tension through the feet and fingers.
- Pressure distribution includes being upper-body dominant or lower-body dominant, sometimes called front-wheel drive versus rear-wheel drive climbing. Ideally, use as much of the lower body (the big, strong, powerful muscles) as possible to do the climbing, which makes it more efficient.

| Drill | How | Progression |
|---|---|---|
| One foothold per move | Prepares you for the stage when you don't always have two footholds for each move and have to work out how to apply pressure with just one. Allow yourself only one foothold for each move, and make the other leg do something useful: press on the wall where you would want a foothold. With this constraint you will probably be forced to flag a lot and to think about lower-body movement, where you apply pressure and where the momentum for the next move comes from | A harder climb, still one foothold per move. You can choose which foot to use for each move: if the left foot feels bad on a move, try again with the right and see which is more efficient. You may end up practising flagging and also pogos |
| Move the feet, hands fixed | Teaches applying pressure through the toes while keeping the whole core tight. On a steep bit of wall, take a comfortable handhold and walk the feet around for about 10 foot moves; get really stretched out so it becomes harder to apply pressure through the toes | Challenge yourself with smaller or slightly sloping footholds |
| Move the hands, feet fixed (progression of the previous drill) | Keep the feet in the same place and move the hands around for about 10 moves (e.g. on a board), which again needs pressure and tension through the toes. Get really extended to challenge yourself, and use quite steep terrain | Start with slightly larger footholds, then go smaller: the steeper the wall and the smaller the feet, the harder it is |

- **Warning:** if you don't apply pressure with the other foot (e.g. you let the left foot dangle), it is dead weight that you have to pull against, and the move becomes much more strenuous and snatchy.

## Prediction
- Prediction is applying these skills more cognitively. It can still be integrated into movement: in timing and momentum, it is predicting where your hip position will finish and making sure you land at the apex of the trajectory.
- Most important, and something to do every time you climb: predict the moves you are going to do, i.e. read the route before you get on the wall so you can execute the techniques already planned in your mind. Route reading has a lot of nuance and small skills; the drills refine and apply them continuously.

| Drill | How | Dose / scaling |
|---|---|---|
| Flashing boulders | The pressure of one attempt makes you predict as much as possible about the boulder. Look at all the holds and predict the best places to hold each one (look for the good bits); brushing a hold can sometimes give a clue as well | Right at the start of the session, when thoroughly warmed up but still fresh; level pretty challenging; around six boulders |
| Eliminates | If you do flash boulders regularly you are likely to run out of new boulders; eliminates recycle boulders you have done before. Choosing holds to eliminate that still leave a viable boulder makes sure you understand how the boulder works. Eliminates also work momentum and power, as they often force quite large moves | Easier boulders: eliminate several holds. Harder boulders: eliminate one hold to create complex movement patterns |
