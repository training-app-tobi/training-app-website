# Gym Training Strategies for Route Climbing

**Type:** Exercise/protocol, Technique · **Trains:** Technique, Endurance · **For:** Off-season training

**TL;DR:** Use the gym in the off-season with intentional practice, not just social climbing. Downclimb routes to sharpen focus and footwork, do speed laps on wired or easy routes about 10% faster each lap, and add the First Touch and Tracking drills. Climbing faster is a skill that could add a full number grade to your redpoint ability in a single season.

## Why train with intent in the gym
- Indoor walls make climbing a year-round activity, so you can improve rather than regress in strength and ability through the colder months; use the off-season to build strength, power and technique.
- Joining a commercial climbing gym or building a home bouldering wall is the single best investment you can make toward improving your climbing.
- Effective indoor training means more than climbing with a group of friends: intentional practice strategies indoors translate to greater economy of movement and climbing ability when you return outdoors in spring, and they enhance skill learning, improve the climber's mindset and increase sport-specific strength.

## Downclimbing routes
- When leading or toproping in the gym, don't just climb to the top and lower off: downclimb as much of the route as possible.
- Benefits beyond doubling the pump: knowing you will downclimb keeps you more observant and focused on the way up, and downclimbing demands intense concentration on footwork, so it helps improve this common weakness.
- At first downclimbing feels difficult, awkward and very pumpy. As hold recognition improves and you learn to relax while reversing the route, it often feels easier than ascending, because eccentric (lowering) strength is greater than concentric (pulling) strength.
- Leading with the feet while downclimbing teaches you to maximally weight them and conserve energy.

## Speed training
- When the rock gets steep and the moves hard, nothing is more important than picking up the pace.
- Climbing quickly is primarily a skill, not strength or power (not lunging wildly up a route). The less strength and endurance you have, the more important this skill becomes.
- **Warning:** There is no benefit to climbing faster if your technique degrades and you botch sequences. Practice speed climbing only on routes you've already wired or climbs well below your maximum ability.

| Element | Prescription |
|---|---|
| Route | Already wired, or well below your maximum ability |
| Laps | Several laps on the same route, resting between attempts |
| Pace | Each lap about 10% faster than the previous one |
| Stop rule | Back off the pace at the first sign your technique is suffering |
| Frequency | A few times a week for several months |

- Result: you naturally move faster when onsighting or redpointing outdoors. This skill alone could push your redpoint ability a full number grade higher over a single season, a much greater gain than strength training alone would give.

## Other drills
| Drill | How | What it trains |
|---|---|---|
| First Touch | Use each handhold exactly as you first touch it, with no readjusting or regripping (unless you fall) | Pre-climb visualization and on-route decision-making, reducing wrong moves and wasted energy when onsighting |
| Tracking | Elimination drill: the feet may use only the exact holds used by the hands. Use it on routes two to three number grades below your maximum ability | High-stepping, hand-foot matches and balanced stand-ups |
