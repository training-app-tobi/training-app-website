# Alactic Interval Training: Progression and Transition

**Type:** Exercise/protocol, Planning · **Trains:** Power, Finger strength, Pulling strength, General strength · **For:** already doing alactic intervals

**TL;DR:** Do alactic intervals to train power (go harder) or capacity (more hard tries in a session or day): they train the highest-power energy system and fast recovery from maximal efforts. Work in 3-min rounds on a rolling 30 s clock (five efforts of 10 s or less, then 30 s rest), done in series with 5 min rest between series. Progress by adding load or adding rounds over cycles of 12–24 sessions, not by shortening rest or lengthening efforts: performance should stay similar round after round.

## Who should do what

| Situation / goal | Do this |
|---|---|
| Want more power (go harder) | Short sets of 3–8 s, very high effort (close to 90%), long rests |
| Want more capacity (many hard tries over a day, e.g. working the crux of a project, a bouldering competition) | Progress toward longer sessions at those high loads, efforts at 75% or harder. Longer total work time over the training cycle is key: don't shortcut it in favor of easier movement |
| Maintaining alactic ability | At least 1 alactic session per week |
| Developing more power or more capacity | 3–4 alactic sessions per week |
| Developing the ability for the first time (most climbers with alactic-specific training) | Longer cycles: 12–24 sessions over 6–12 weeks, about 2 sessions per week (slightly more or fewer is fine) |
| Young, fit and focused on gym training | Up to 24 sessions in 8 weeks |
| Can only do this every 5 days or so | 12 sessions over 8 weeks |
| Rebuilding to a level reached before | Shorter cycles: 8–16 sessions over 4–6 weeks |
| After 12 sessions, want to keep increasing power and strength, or to keep adding other training | Repeat the same structure with higher loads |
| After 12 sessions, want even more capacity and to carry it into lactic (power endurance) efforts | Add more series and rounds |
| Adding capacity, or focusing heavily on the alactic system | Add series, rounds kept at 5 exercises (see Adding series) |
| Combining with a skill-focused bouldering session or a route day | 2 series of 3 rounds (about 25 min for most climbers) |

## What alactic intervals do

- Train the highest-power energy system, the "power at the crux" system, and teach the body to recover quickly from maximal efforts.
- Raise the number of hard tries available in a session or a day of repeated hard efforts (working the crux of a project, a bouldering competition).
- Gains in alactic function are critical to sport performance. You know what trying hard feels like: train at that effort.
- Alactic intervals are only one part of training, during specific parts of the training year. Good programming improves alactic function and also the relationship between alactic and aerobic training.
- Don't jump into an alactic-only phase: the interplay of pure strength and power training with aerobic development of the muscles is critical.
- **Warning:** Training it takes discipline: don't slide into chasing fatigue in the lactic "second gear".

## Session protocol

- 5–10 s efforts with rests of up to 4 min work best but make a long session that doesn't feel like hard work. Putting different exercises in sequence and overloading the system by changing the work within a shorter cycle gives a rolling 30–40 s clock.
- One round = 3 min on a 30 s clock:

| Clock | Slot | Effort | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0:00 | Upper-body explosive or edge hang | under 10 s | Campus ladder 1-3-5-7-9 (medium rungs) |
| 0:30 | Lower-body or total-body explosive | under 10 s | Kettlebell swing × 6 (bell about ½ bodyweight) |
| 1:00 | Edge hang | 10 s | Edge hang 10 s (bodyweight, 10 mm) |
| 1:30 | Upper-body explosive | under 10 s | Campus doubles × 3 (large edges or jugs) |
| 2:00 | Upper-body strength | under 10 s | Power pull-up × 2 |
| 2:30 | Rest | full 30 s | Rest |

- First sessions: several rounds back-to-back as a series (usually 3–6 rounds per series), with a long rest between series: 5 min.
- Set up a couple of tools next to the campus board: there is little time to move around the gym.
- **Warning:** A few exercises can change between series, but don't chase variety: progress is hard to see when things change too often.

## Errors in progression

- Make sessions harder only in ways that help performance. Things done just to feel hard (e.g. 500 push-ups at the end of a session) are taxing but don't help.
- Check: performance should stay similar round after round. If it doesn't, you are likely training the wrong energy system.
- Progress alactic intervals with only one of two methods: add load, or add rounds.
- **Warning:** Shorter rest (e.g. a rolling 25 s clock instead of 30 s). On the 30 s clock most exercises take 10 s or less, leaving about 20 s before the next one, and close to 50 s pass between the campus board and the edge hang. Cutting that rest feels much harder but works against repeated hard efforts such as boulders or cruxes: you rely more and more on the second energy system, and the ability to repeat the same hard exercises round after round drops off. It builds some power endurance, but there are better methods for that, and that system is better trained later in the training year.
- **Warning:** More exercises per round (e.g. 10 instead of 5): fatigue comes back, longer series tax capacity, work quality drops in long circuits, it's hard to keep track of, and, worst of all, the same movements aren't revisited often enough.
- **Warning:** More reps or time per exercise (e.g. 12–15 s work periods instead of 10 s): like shorter rest, it causes high fatigue. Fatigue is not the aim here; keep it for power endurance cycles.

## How to progress

- Stick with a cycle long enough that the first workouts look laughably easy to repeat.
- Sessions trend upward in difficulty, mainly by adding load: more load means more work per set and a much higher demand for work output per session.
- Load can't rise forever, so adding rounds is a gentler way to make the body adapt. It mainly builds work capacity but still adds overall alactic endurance, so it is not an easier or less effective option.

### 12-session plan

- Series start at 3 rounds (9 min of work). Rest 5 min between series, longer if work quality drops from series to series.

| Session | Work | Load |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2 series of 3 rounds | Starting loads |
| 2–3 | 2 series of 3 rounds | Add load in 2–3 exercises |
| 4 | 2 series of 4 rounds | Hold the previous session's loads |
| 5–6 | 2 series of 4 rounds | Add load in 2–3 exercises |
| 7 | 3 series of 3 rounds | Hold the previous session's loads |
| 8–9 | 3 series of 3 rounds | Add load in 2–3 exercises |
| 10 | 3 series of 4 rounds | Hold the previous session's loads |
| 11–12 | 3 series of 4 rounds | Add load in 2–3 exercises |

- After 12 sessions: stop, find the exercises that give the least stimulus or difficulty and change them. Change the exercise order or selection if needed.
- Drop exercises that worked for 12 sessions but are hard to keep progressing (e.g. box jump, medicine ball slam).
- **Warning:** Up to 5 rounds per series go well; beyond that athletes get sloppy. Add series instead, with 3–5 rounds each.

### Progressing load

- Adding load in 2–3 exercises per session doesn't always happen and can be subtler than expected. On days with less to give, it's fine to stay flat for a session or two, or even back off a bit.

| Exercise | Session 1 | Session 2 | Session 3 | Session 4 | Session 5 | Session 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Campus ladder | 1-3-5-7-9 | 1-4-6-8-10 | 1-4-6-8-11 | 1-4-6-8-11 | 1-4-6-8-11 | 1-4-6-8-10 (smaller rung) |
| Kettlebell swing | 5 × 24 kg | 5 × 24 kg | 5 × 28 kg | 5 × 32 kg | 4 × 36 kg | 4 × 36 kg |
| Edge hang | 10 s, 12 mm | 10 s, 10 mm | 10 s, 10 mm | 10 s, 10 mm | 8 s, 8 mm | 10 s, 8 mm |
| Box jump | 5 × 61 cm | 5 × 61 cm | 5 × 61 cm | 5 × 76 cm | 5 × 76 cm | 5 × 76 cm |
| Pull-up | 3, bodyweight | 3, bodyweight | 3, bodyweight | 3, bodyweight | 3, bodyweight | 3, bodyweight |

### When progress stalls

- If load can't progress at all, add rounds: even one extra round at the same load is progress.
- Athletes typically level off after 12–24 sessions, which is why cycles are that long. Then a couple of sessions at the same load confirm the fitness, and it's time to move on to other training foci.

## Transition: A/B split

- These sessions repeat structure and movements a lot. Some repetition helps but isn't necessary.
- The simplest way to add breadth to a cycle: alternate two workouts with the same load and volume progression as the single-workout plan. The progression then runs 24 sessions instead of 12 before it changes.

| Clock | Workout A | Workout B |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00 | Campus ladder 1-3-5-7-9 (medium rungs) | Foot-on campus doubles × 4 (large rungs) |
| 0:30 | Kettlebell swing × 6 (bell about ½ bodyweight) | Trap bar deadlift × 2 (1.5× bodyweight) |
| 1:00 | Edge hang 10 s (bodyweight, 10 mm) | Front-three hang 10 s (bodyweight, 16 mm) |
| 1:30 | Campus doubles × 3 (large edges or jugs) | TRX inverted row × 5 |
| 2:00 | Power pull-up × 2 | Lock-off 5+5 s (90 degrees, pull-up bar) |
| 2:30 | Rest full 30 s | Rest full 30 s |

## Changing exercises between phases

- Change exercises gradually rather than dropping one exercise set and starting another: you get more out of some movements and the body has time to adjust.
- Keep the same five exercises, with a few time adjustments, for at least 5–6 sessions before the first change. After session 12, hold the new five for a few weeks before the next change.

| Slot | Session 1 | Session 7 | Session 8 | Session 10 | Session 12 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Front lever × 2 | Hanging straight-leg raise × 5 | Hanging straight-leg raise × 5 | Hanging straight-leg raise × 5 | Hanging straight-leg raise × 5 |
| 2 | Edge hang 10 s | Edge hang 10 s | Back-three hang 10 s | Back-three hang 10 s | Back-three hang 10 s |
| 3 | Ball slam × 5 | Ball slam × 5 | Ball slam × 5 | Ball slam × 5 | Kettlebell snatch 2+2 |
| 4 | Campus ladder | Campus ladder | Campus ladder | Muscle-up × 2 | Muscle-up × 2 |
| 5 | Power clean × 3 | Power clean × 3 | Box jump × 5 | Box jump × 5 | Box jump × 5 |

- **Warning:** Switching the entire workout too often leaves the athlete sore and over-fatigued. That state is often taken as a sign of a superior session but can damage the training process.

## Adding an exercise to the round

- Extending the rounds with an extra exercise is another effective way to raise total work, but it needs a really good reason: longer circuits tend to worsen single-exercise performance.
- Add it slowly, 1–2 reps at a time, to be sure the athlete can handle the load: one extra pull-up at the basic level adds 6 pull-ups to the session.

| Session | Round |
|---|---|
| 1 | Front lever × 2, edge hang 10 s, ball slam × 5, campus ladder, power clean × 3 |
| 3 | Same + pull-up × 1 |
| 5 | Same + pull-ups × 2 |
| 7 | Same + pull-ups × 3 |

- **Warning:** Small increases bring big adaptations over the long haul, even when bigger jumps seem manageable. Athletes who make huge jumps in volume don't hold any fitness level beyond 8–10 weeks: they usually lose enthusiasm, get sick, or injure an elbow.

## Adding series

- To add capacity, or when focusing heavily on the alactic system, raise the volume of quality work by adding series. Keep rounds at 5 exercises (3 min each).

| Stage | Plan | Total time |
|---|---|---|
| First few weeks | 2 series of 3 rounds (9 min each), 5 min rest between series | 23 min |
| Then, for 2 sessions | 3 series of 3 rounds, 5 min rest between series | 37 min |
| Finally | 4 series of 3 rounds, 5 min rest between series | 51 min |

- Volume rises so much that you don't also add exercises per round, raise loads, or add rounds within a series.

## Let the adaptation happen

- **Warning:** Whatever the progression, give the body time to adapt to each new level of output. Raising load, volume and round duration all at once makes you sore the next day and ends, at best, in an early plateau.
- The goal is not a fitness peak but a foundational change in how the body produces and delivers energy. Let the training happen, and save all-out ("a muerte") efforts for the peaking sessions and sends that follow.
