# Limit Bouldering for Max Strength and Power

**Type:** Exercise/protocol, Session · **Trains:** Finger strength, Power

**TL;DR:** Limit bouldering means trying hard in short, intense bursts of no more than 3–5 moves at your absolute limit, on boulders 1–2 grades above your redpoint and even your projecting grade; sending is not the goal. Rest a few minutes between attempts and end the session as soon as output noticeably dips. Never limit boulder two days in a row, and ideally no more than twice a week.

## Why limit boulder
- Every climbing training plan needs a component of limit bouldering, and that includes purely sport climbers: all the endurance in the world won't help you send if you lack the strength and power to get through the crux. Limit bouldering builds both.
- More than any other workout routine (hangboarding included), limit bouldering gives the greatest gains in functional strength and power on the wall. Expect improvements in force generation and in effective energy management.
- Mental gains: the confidence to "go for it" despite preconceived judgments about a move. Limit bouldering teaches you to separate effort from outcome and to value trying hard for trying's sake; often, deciding to try in the first place is all it takes to unlock the move.
- **Warning:** Limit bouldering is simple but easily misunderstood, and it only gives these benefits when done correctly. Hitting the intended energy system takes patience and practice.

## Projecting versus limit bouldering
| | Projecting | Limit bouldering |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | Working through a climb that's too difficult or complex to send immediately | Trying hard in short, intense bursts of no more than 3–5 moves at your absolute limit |
| Goal | Always the send, whether it takes a few tries, sessions, months or even years | Sending isn't the goal, and fixating on sending undermines it |
| Process | Deciphering the moves → linking sections → highpointing or lowpointing → redpoint burns → send | Working short sequences at your limit, each as a mini-project |

- Why sending doesn't fit: (a) most boulder problems take more than 3–5 moves, and anything longer switches from pure strength and power training into power endurance; (b) if you can send something, it's within your ability, and limit bouldering requires pushing past that point. Climb on things that won't go down anytime soon, or not without the fight of your life.
- Take the pressure off sending and focus on trying hard: consistent high-intensity effort on climbs above your redpoint grade pays off with plenty of sending later.

## How to limit boulder
| Step | Do this |
|---|---|
| Warm-up | Start with a thorough warm-up and escalate through the grades |
| Choose the boulder | Go beyond your max: take your redpoint grade, and even your projecting grade, and go 1–2 grades higher. If not even a single move feels remotely doable, you've gone too far; aim for the sweet spot between impossible and improbable |
| Break it down | Split the climb into 3–5-move sections that demand everything you have. Use all the technical skills in your repertoire together with max physical output; the combination should feel utterly exhausting |
| Work the sequences | Piece together each short sequence: treat each as a mini-project and try to "send" the sequence instead of the climb |
| Rest between attempts | Take a few minutes between attempts, enough to give full effort on every go: fatigue has no place in limit bouldering, and "powering down" means crossing back into power-endurance territory |
| End the session | Keep close tabs on your output throughout; as soon as it noticeably dips, end the session. It isn't a contest of who lasts longest, only about the try-hard |

- Spray walls and LED boards (e.g. Tension, Moon, Kilter) work well for this because they're shorter than typical bouldering gym walls, and you can set your own sequences at the right difficulty if nothing else fits.

## How often to limit boulder
- **Warning:** Rest between sessions matters as much as rest within a session. Never limit boulder two days in a row, and ideally no more than twice a week.
- Space limit bouldering sessions with "quick send" sessions that focus on volume more than intensity, cross-training sessions and full rest days.
- For climbers used to chasing the pump: a limit bouldering session may not feel as productive as usual. You may not walk away exhausted every time, or even get pumped at all; that doesn't mean you sold yourself short.
- Each short sequence should feel like the hardest thing you've ever tried, every time, which is only possible when fresh: show up to the session rested and ready to dig deep.
