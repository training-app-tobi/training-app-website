# Power Bouldering for Dynamic Drive on the Wall

**Type:** Exercise/protocol · **Trains:** Power

**TL;DR:** Power bouldering builds full-body, movement-based power on the wall, which often gives greater and more transferable gains than campusing alone. Climb short sequences of three to six hard, dynamic moves at or near your absolute limit, on bigger holds with larger throws, resting at least 3 minutes between attempts and stopping as soon as output declines. Do no more than two sessions per week, never on back-to-back days.

## Why power boulder instead of only campusing
- The campus board is not the most effective way to build the power that shows up on hard routes and boulders. Climbing power is full-body power: generating force from the toes through the core into the hands. Training movement-based power on the wall often gives greater, more transferable gains.
- The campus board still has value in a structured power phase, but it shouldn't stand alone: add power bouldering to the weekly program.
- What power bouldering trains that campusing doesn't:
  - recruiting feet, legs and hips to drive movement, so the arms aren't doing all the work;
  - power across multiple planes of motion: diagonal, lateral and even downward, not just straight up;
  - applying force on a wide variety of holds and body positions beyond uniform campus rungs.

## Limit bouldering versus power bouldering
- What the two share: both target your upper limits with short, high-intensity efforts; both demand patience, long rests and a mindset focused on maximum effort rather than sending; both are about quality over quantity.
- **Warning:** Once you start getting pumped, you've drifted into power-endurance territory (different energy system, different goal): cut the set and rest.

The key difference is movement style:

| | Limit bouldering | Power bouldering |
|---|---|---|
| Emphasis | Maximum strength | Speed and force production |
| Movement | Often slow, controlled movement, hard lock-offs, small holds, high tension | Dynamic movement with intent and velocity |

Move choice in power sessions:

| Prioritize | Reduce reliance on |
|---|---|
| Dynamic movement: deadpoints, dynos, coordination moves, explosive direction changes | Tiny crimps |
| Bigger holds and larger throws | Static lock-offs |
| Moving with intent and velocity: it's difficult to produce high power at low speed | Slow "piano match" sequences |

- Think quick and committed: minimize hesitation between moves; link movements with flow and snap rather than pause and grind. Faster reactions and decisive execution shift the demand toward power.

## How to power boulder
| Element | Do this |
|---|---|
| Structure | Like limit work: short sequences at or near your absolute limit |
| Sequence length | Three to six hard moves per sequence; create and send several brief (six moves or less) "power boulders" |
| Where | Commercial gym problems are usually longer, so break them into pieces and train the crux segments. Better: build custom sequences on a spray wall. Better still: create your own power problems on a Kilter or Tension Board |
| Rest | At least 3 minutes between attempts; take longer rests if power drops |
| End of session | Stop the session when output declines |

- Custom setting matters because power is body-size dependent: a move that's perfect for a tall climber may be impossible for a shorter one, or too small to be powerful for someone with a longer reach. Personalized sequences make the move demand maximum but achievable force for your body.
- A 30 to 50 Kilter board is the ideal platform for creating high-quality, jumpy power problems.
- Rest like a sprinter: no filler exercises between attempts. Track sprinters protect their rest intervals fiercely; do the same, and treat each attempt like a max sprint effort.
- **Warning:** Skip the "one last try" temptation: save that grit for endurance phases. When power fades, the stimulus is gone, and more attempts just dig a recovery hole.

## How often to power boulder
Power is a high-neural, high-cost quality: train it sparingly and deliberately.

| Rule | Do this |
|---|---|
| Frequency | No more than two power bouldering sessions per week |
| Spacing | Never on back-to-back days |
| Monitoring | Monitor performance closely |
| Power output drops across consecutive sessions | Reduce to once per week until you adapt |
| Days in between | Fill the gaps with lower-intensity climbing or technique sessions while the nervous system rebounds |

- Intensity beats frequency: one high-quality, high-power session creates more adaptation than multiple mediocre ones.
