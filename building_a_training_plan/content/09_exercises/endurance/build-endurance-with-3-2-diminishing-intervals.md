# Build Endurance With 3:2 Diminishing Intervals

**Type:** Exercise/protocol, Session · **Trains:** Endurance · **For:** boulderers, route climbers

**TL;DR:** Even boulderers need endurance: the ability to endure longer durations of power output over a given period of time. Train it for intensity, not for fatigue: 3:2 diminishing intervals shorten work and recovery as the session progresses (3:00/2:00, 2:00/1:20, 1:00/0:40, then 0:30/0:20 until failure), building from one series at the end of a normal session to three series as the main endurance session. Keep sessions to a practical length, address endurance regularly, and commit to at least 4–6 weeks of an endurance build.

## Who should do what

| Situation / level | Do this |
|---|---|
| Boulderers, even ones totally focused on bouldering | Train the ability to endure longer durations of power output over a given period of time: the demands addressed on a spray wall or fixed board are different from the demands actually faced outside on rock |
| Skin becomes as big a limiter as endurance | For a few sessions, alternate rowing on a rowing machine with climbing on open holds (see Alternating with rowing) |
| Pumping out low on a route, losing power, or just feeling the effect of specific exercises | Longer sessions might not be the best way to improve: longer training sessions are mainly aimed at creating greater capacity |
| Want to go long (more volume) | Take a broader approach: combine the intervals with other exercises, or do more targeted training such as simply bouldering hard several days per cycle, rather than five, six or seven sets of the intervals |
| Can't go through at least 4–6 weeks of an endurance build | Your time is probably better spent elsewhere |

## Endurance for boulderers

- Even a climber who is totally focused on bouldering needs the ability to endure longer durations of power output over a given period of time.
- A common trap is thinking bouldering is only about power and strength development. One of the most difficult things for a boulderer to understand is that the demands addressed on a spray wall or fixed board are different from the demands actually faced outside on rock.

## Fatigue is not the goal

- **Warning:** When training for strength or power, climbers tend to seek out only higher outputs; when training for endurance, they too often seek out only fatigue. The typical reasoning: if 30 s of climbing followed by 90 s of rest is difficult, resting only 60 s must be better training, and dropping the rest to 30 s even better, because being more tired at the end of training must be better. Just because it is difficult does not make it good training: it is ironic to seek the highest amount of fatigue possible in the least amount of time in the gym, then carefully avoid fatigue at all costs when performing at the crag.

## Why diminishing intervals

- Instead of a fixed schedule (e.g. 60 s of work followed by 2 min of rest for several rounds), research indicates that athletes can keep maintaining intensity by shortening the intervals as the session progresses.
- Maintaining high intensity is as important as, if not more important than, high volume in an interval workout.
- The research idea behind this structure is called high-intensity decreasing interval training.

## The 3:2 diminishing intervals session

- Work and recovery always stay in a 3:2 ratio.

| Interval | Work | Recovery | Mode |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 3:00 of continuous climbing | 2:00 | Open holds on a spray wall |
| 2 | 2:00 | 1:20 | Open holds on a spray wall |
| 3 | 1:00 | 0:40 | Campus board with a kick rail |
| 4 | 0:30, repeated until you fail to complete the full 30 s of activity | 0:20 | Campus board with a kick rail |

## How to progress

| Stage | Series |
|---|---|
| Start | 1 series, attached to the end of a normal training session |
| Then, for a few sessions | 2 series |
| Finally | 3 series as the main training in an endurance-oriented session |

- Build up until you reach three full rounds. This isn't too bad for a lot of climbers.

## Session variants

- Research and best practice on these variants are at a very early stage.
- All the variants keep the 3:2 work-to-recovery ratio: assumptions and compromises are unavoidable, and the ratio is the one held constant.

### Alternating with rowing

- When skin becomes as big a limiter as endurance: for a few sessions, alternate rowing on a rowing machine with climbing on open holds.

| Interval | Group 1 work | Group 2 work | Rest |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 3:00 climb on open holds | 3:00 rowing | 2:00 |
| 2 | 2:00 rowing | 2:00 climb on open holds | 1:20 |
| 3 | 1:00 climb on open holds | 1:00 rowing | 0:40 |
| 4 | 0:30 rowing, repeated until failure | 0:30 climb on open holds, repeated until failure | 0:20 |

- Effect: a whole lot more local fatigue in the arms and back than climbing alone; easier on the skin, but not as specific to the endurance needs of climbing. Climbing is slow-paced, and non-specific cardiovascular modes can be very unlike climbing.
- Alternating in non-specific work like this is good, but rowing alone might not be the best mode: it tends to feel a little harried.

### Better option: one mode per block

| Block | Mode |
|---|---|
| 1 | One full block of the intervals with just climbing and board work |
| 2 | Air bike or rowing machine |
| 3 (if you do a third block) | Switch back to climbing |

- This seems to keep all the benefits of the original session but is a lot more manageable.

### Longer version: start at 8:00

| Interval | Work | Rest |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 8:00 | 5:20 |
| 2 | 6:00 | 4:00 |
| 3 | 4:00 | 2:40 |
| 4 | 3:00 | 2:00 |
| 5 | 2:00 | 1:20 |
| 6 | 1:00 | 0:40 |
| 7 | 0:30, repeated until failure | 0:20 |

- The first intervals have to be much easier: extensive-endurance intensity, around 50% of max, mostly on established but easy problems with reasonable downclimbs and few shakeouts.
- Effects: far more fatigue than the shorter sessions (as expected), and recovery between series needs to be much longer: routinely 20 min between series, and only two series possible in a day.
- Total climbing time is close to 25 min per series, and sessions routinely push against 2 hours.
- There may be cases where this volume of training is appropriate, but it was beyond what was needed for the projects in mind. Chasing the clock was also a bit stressful, and the sessions ended without much more than moving around on easy ground.
- **Warning:** Be reluctant to develop endurance only on simple moves and big holds: that is almost never where you end up in the performance environment.

## Session length and volume

- Keep sessions within a practical duration for training. Climbers' ability to recover between sessions and their need for specific duration vary quite a bit: it isn't uncommon to find high-level climbers doing 10 or more pitches per day.
- **Warning:** Before ramping up to higher-than-ever volumes, first consider what you have actually been doing in your training. An elite climber training four hours a day doesn't mean you can quickly adapt to a program that size.
- Longer training sessions are mainly aimed at creating greater capacity. So if you are pumping out low on a route, losing power, or just feeling the effect of specific exercises, longer sessions might not be the best way to improve.
- To go long, take a broader approach: five, six or seven sets of these intervals will probably be less effective than combining them with other exercises, or than more targeted training such as simply bouldering hard several days per cycle. You can only move the needle so far with a given session.
- Regularly addressing these energy-system demands is more important than how hard you go in any individual workout.
- If you don't feel you can go through at least 4–6 weeks of an endurance build, your time is probably better spent elsewhere: the adaptations endurance training requires are big and don't happen overnight.
