# Mobility Work During Rest Periods

**Type:** Session, Principles · **Trains:** Mobility

**TL;DR:** Mobility is strength through a full range of motion, and climbing needs a lot of it: 30 minutes a day for each decade of your life. Stretch whenever you will actually do it; before training, keep static holds under about 45 seconds. The most practical way to get it done: put 30-60 seconds of mobility work into the rests of strength sessions, and take shoes-off mobility breaks on bouldering days.

## Who should do what

| Situation / level | Do this |
|---|---|
| Teenager | Mobility practice one day a week is fine |
| In your thirties | Mobility practice 3 times per week |
| Older | Easiest to do mobility practice every single day |

## Stretching and mobility

- Stretching feels good and may create greater range of motion, but the real benefits come when stretches are combined with movement in the joints. This is mobility: developing strength through a full range of motion.

## When to stretch

- Prolonged static stretching as part of the warm-up lowers performance: slower speeds on the track and lower numbers on the lifting platform have been noted since the mid twentieth century, and a large volume of research backs this up. A 2012 meta-analysis shows maximum strength can drop 5-6%, and power about half that, when strength and power exercises follow prolonged static stretches in the warm-up.
- A drop of about 2% in maximal power probably doesn't affect most sessions. If before exercise is the only time someone will stretch, stretching then is worth it.
- **Warning:** static stretches held for less than about 45 seconds don't tend to produce these drops in strength and power: if you stretch before training, keep the holds short.
- Stretch whenever you will actually do it: before exercise, after exercise, on days away from exercise, or during sessions. The end of a session would be ideal, but climbers too often squeeze in one more problem and leave the gym without an intentional flexibility and warm-down routine.

## How much

- Climbing requires a high degree of mobility, so a lot of time should go to mobility practice. Dose: 30 minutes a day for each decade of your life.
- Plan to address all of the joints in each session. If you are trying to gain ground on a particular joint or muscle, add more targeted work for it.
- Working on flexibility and working on mobility both benefit you. As with strength training or bouldering, don't stick to "pet" exercises: if you can do the splits but can't hold your arms straight overhead, stop doing splits and work on the overhead position.

## Which joints need it most

| Joints | Structure | Mobility limitations |
|---|---|---|
| Elbows and knees | Fairly simple | Few people have mobility limitations there |
| Wrists and ankles | More complex | Not normally big limiters for athletes |
| Shoulders and hips | Multi-plane, heavily muscled | The biggest problems: they must be stable for sport movement yet capable of full ranges of motion |

- Climbing needs high levels of strength together with good movement quality. Simplest way to balance them: include high-force movements in most sessions, and include mobility exercises in those same sessions.

## Intra-set mobility in strength sessions

- Recommending stretching after the session didn't work, and neither did programming stretching in the last 5 to 10 minutes of a session: people too often cut the stretching short and left the gym.
- The rest-period problem: athletes either rest too little (to get a more fatiguing feeling from the workout) or finish a set, pull out their phone and zone out for five minutes. Five-minute rests are fine; leaving the training mindset during a workout is not. Solution: in strength or bouldering sessions, assign mobility work during the rests instead of sitting around (intra-set mobility).

| Structure | Sequence |
|---|---|
| A | Strength exercise 1 → 30-60 seconds of mobility exercise 1 → Strength exercise 2 → 30-60 seconds of mobility exercise 2 |
| B | Strength exercise 1 → Strength exercise 2 → 30-60 seconds of mobility exercise 1 |

- Do either structure for 2 to 4 rounds, depending on the session. A typical session: about six strength exercises and three mobility exercises.

### Integrated Strength sessions

| Circuit order | Exercise |
|---|---|
| 1 | General strength exercise |
| 2 | Finger strength exercise |
| 3 | Mobility exercise |

- Three circuits, three rounds of each circuit, about 15 minutes per circuit; three circuits take about 45 minutes.
- This is probably not optimal for strength, and probably not optimal for mobility either, but it is practical: people can and will do it.

## On bouldering days

- Bouldering days are an ideal time for mobility work: skin and feet wear out long before power fades, so slowing the pace of the session with time on the floor is a big win.

| Method | How |
|---|---|
| By count | Take off your shoes and do a single mobility drill after every 5 problems or attempts. Good for skin and tendons, and you may drink more water |
| By time | 10 minutes of bouldering, then shoes off for 5 minutes of mobility and rest. Repeat 3-6 more times |

## Good habits

- Mobility is trained with mobility drills, but it also develops through better movement habits.
- Use a full range of motion on strength exercises. Common failures: squat depth, pull-up range, push-up range and overhead press range. If you think your form looks good, film it and assess it, or ask a strength coach to watch you.
- **Warning:** never sacrifice range for reps.
- In climbing, work on longer reaches, higher steps and spread stances. This is easier in warm-ups and cool-downs, but pushing movement range is essential to be able to use it when you are at your limit.
- Sit on the floor or on the ground when possible: it addresses a lot in the hips and legs. If you can't sit there for more than a few minutes, this is a clear area to improve.
- Between exercises, stick to shorter, dynamic stretches and drills. Save long holds for stretching-only sessions, preferably late in the day.

## Progress and motivation

- It is difficult to improve flexibility as an adult, but maintaining your current flexibility is much more manageable.
- Mobility improvement takes a long time and is hard to measure, so judge it by movement: can you get your hands and feet into the proper positions on climbs smoothly and efficiently? Can you stem, high step and draw your body into the wall well? Use video and a sense of mastery: ask "Is my sequence and movement worthy of a beta video? An instructional video?"
