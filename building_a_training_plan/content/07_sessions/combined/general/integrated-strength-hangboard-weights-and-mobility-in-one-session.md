# Integrated Strength: Hangboard, Weights and Mobility in One Session

**Type:** Session, Planning · **Trains:** Finger strength, General strength, Mobility · **For:** Intermediate to advanced, in-season maintenance, no weight room

**TL;DR:** Combine a finger strength set, a heavy resistance set and a mobility drill in a short circuit: the three differ enough that each works as rest for the other two, so you get the long rests heavy strength training needs without wasting time. Do three groups of three rounds each (about 15 minutes per group, about an hour with a warm-up), training the hip hinge, upper body press and squat, with three hangboard positions held for 10 seconds. Train 2-3 sessions per week in a strength phase and one every week or 10 days in-season, as little as possible to get the desired results.

## Who should do what

| Situation / level | Do this |
|---|---|
| Bouldering phase, high-volume alpine training phase or peak redpoint phase | Keep a base of two Integrated Strength sessions per week, so strength keeps moving forward |
| Long periods of the year with next to nothing on the rock or in the mountains | Use this minimalist program to stay fit enough to get back into shape quickly when the opportunity arises |
| Not ready to take on a project ("park bench") | Use Integrated Strength as a holding-pattern session |
| Going for a project ("bus bench") | Increase the training loads, reduce the hold size on the hangboard sets and get going |
| Strength phase | 2-3 sessions per week |
| In-season | One session every week or 10 days as strength maintenance; don't increase loads, but don't regress either |
| In-season and getting weaker | Raise your high-load training again or train strength more often |
| Intermediate to advanced trainee | Session 2 (one-arm 10-second hangs in three arm positions) |
| No weight room close to your hangboard | Session 3 (bodyweight only, 3-3-3 hangs) |

## Why combine hangboard, weights and mobility

- Training for climbing is an exercise in compromise: focus on power and you lose endurance; spend too much time getting your fingers strong and your redpoint grade drops. Many programs are more or less taken from training plans for other sports, which brings a whole host of issues.
- The stronger climbers both avoid injury and send quickly. All facets of fitness are derivatives of strength: power = strength × speed; endurance = strength ÷ time. The stronger you get, the better everything else gets.
- **Warning:** strength training takes time, both to execute and to recover from, and recovery is the biggest issue. People who stall in strength training often get load, volume and frequency right but under-recover, because strength overload doesn't "feel" like hard exercise: the sets are short, the cardiovascular component is almost nonexistent and there is never any nausea.
- To get stronger you need to really overload your system; that requires a lot of rest between efforts; long rests get boring and you start the next set too soon. Solution: find something productive to do between sets.
- Heavy strength training and finger strength training pair almost ideally: the high total-body loads of the strength sets don't affect the finger strength sets, and vice versa. Simply alternating the two can still give too little rest, so mobility work is added between them, giving effective training and an efficient use of time.

| Unit | Content | Duration |
|---|---|---|
| Circuit | A finger strength set, a heavy resistance set and a mobility exercise | About 5 minutes |
| Group | Three rounds of a circuit (the ideal training duration) | About 15 minutes |
| Session | Three different circuits | Under an hour |

## Where your training is going

- Common pattern: either training for something big or doing nothing at all: ramp up for a couple of months, send the route, then hang back; or opting for long, difficult programs that look fabulous on paper but are never executed. Better: make the underlying goal a readiness for the sport that you carry at all times. You can only drive hard part of the time: "If you are always training hard, you're never training hard." This program gives a solid foundation on which to build specific fitness.
- "Park bench" vs "bus bench" workouts: you're on the bus bench when you are going somewhere, on the park bench when you're not.

## Resistance training

- The stronger you are, the easier everything else in sport becomes. All sports differ from each other; the common link is the human being. Climbers generate force like everyone else; they balance strength with focus, route reading and fear management, but the mastery of tension is the base of everything.
- Climbers need very sport-specific training like every other athlete, but the fundamental human movements are best developed in non-specific environments: you can't generate enough force in climbing-specific positions to progress to your full athletic potential.
- The four basic movement patterns give the biggest return: upper body press, upper body pull, hip hinge and squat. They force several muscles to work at once to produce force, which is efficient and effective at producing high levels of hormonal activity (half the reason to do them).
- This program trains three of the four: hip hinge, upper body press and squat. No pulling, because you are probably already overloading the pattern, the hip hinge is a big pull, the finger strength sets are a pull, and bouldering or route climbing means pulling all day anyway. The goal of most training is not to improve what you are already elite at, but to shore up what holds you back.
- Choose exercises you know, that you can do in your facility and that you can progress correctly.

| Pattern | Suggested exercises |
|---|---|
| Hip hinge | Deadlift, kettlebell swing, single-leg hip thrust (bodyweight option) |
| Squat | Single-leg squat, rear-foot-elevated split squat, rack squat |
| Press | One-arm kettlebell press, one-arm push-up, one-arm kettlebell bench press |

- **Warning:** performing the exercises correctly is critical: if you don't know them, don't do them. Find a good strength coach and invest some time and money in learning correct form and loading.

## Session format

- The hangboard sets and the mobility tests and drills are described in the notes Hangboard Sets for Integrated Strength: Three Positions, 10-Second Hangs, Hip and Shoulder Mobility Tests: Frog Stretch and Fist Reach, and Hip and Shoulder Mobility Drills.
- Under-program rather than over-program: it's better that athletes add more at the end than risk not finishing the workout.
- Each session: three hangboard exercises, three strength exercises and three mobility drills, grouped into three small circuits, each done three times before moving to the next (e.g. an edge hang, a set of squats and a prying cobra, three times). Move slowly and deliberately between the three exercises: the movements and loads differ enough that each serves as rest from the other two. Each circuit takes about 5 minutes for most climbers, each group about 15; with a good warm-up the session takes about an hour. Don't hurry: going heavy matters more than going fast.

### Basic format (each group 3 rounds, resting as needed)

| Group | Hangboard | Strength | Mobility |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Hangboard position 1 | Hip hinge | Hip mobility |
| 2 | Hangboard position 2 | Upper body press | Shoulder mobility |
| 3 | Hangboard position 3 | Squat | Hip mobility |

- There is little reason to add more groups, rounds or sets: the volume is high enough to make you stronger but low enough not to tap you out for the week.
- If you need a little more work: add some easy bouldering or route laps at the end. If you are in the weight room and can't climb right after, add a barbell or kettlebell complex.

### Session 1: 10-second hangs (each group 3 rounds, resting as needed)

A standard session that can be done in most good gyms.

| Group | Hangboard | Strength | Mobility |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Open-hand hang, 2 arms × 10 s | Deadlift × 3 | Frog × 60 s |
| 2 | Pinch block 7.6 cm × 10 s per side | One-arm kettlebell press × 6+6 | Kettlebell arm bar × 30 s per side |
| 3 | Half crimp, 2 arms × 10 s | Single-leg squat × 3+3 | Tug of war squat × 60 s |

### Session 2: one-arm 10-second hangs in three arm positions (each group 3 rounds, resting as needed)

Good for an intermediate to advanced trainee.

| Group | Hangboard | Strength | Mobility |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Half crimp, 1 arm, straight arm × 10 s per side | Kettlebell swing × 10 | Frog × 60 s |
| 2 | Half crimp, 1 arm, bent arm × 10 s per side | One-arm kettlebell bench press × 6+6 | Lat/rhomboid foam roll 60 s |
| 3 | Half crimp, 1 arm, lock-off × 10 s per side | Rear-foot-elevated split squat × 3+3 | Tug of war squat × 60 s |

### Session 3: bodyweight only, 3-3-3 hangs (each group 3 rounds, resting as needed)

For those without a weight room close to their hangboard. 3-3-3 hangs run on a 5-second clock: hang 3 s, let go 2 s, hang 3 s, let go 2 s, hang 3 s, then move to the next exercise.

| Group | Hangboard | Strength | Mobility |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Half crimp, 2 arms, straight arm × 3-3-3 | Single-leg hip thrust × 10+10 | Prying cobra × 60 s |
| 2 | Pinch block 7.6 cm × 3-3-3 per side | One-arm push-up × 3+3 | Overhead squat 60 s |
| 3 | Pocket hang, second pair, 2 arms, straight arm × 3-3-3 | Pistol squat × 3+3 | Tug of war squat × 60 s |

## Programming

- In-season the session also works as a test: don't increase loads in-season, but don't regress either.
- Week to week, schedule strength where it is least invasive: usually at the end of a climbing day or the day after climbing.
- **Warning:** train as little as possible to get the desired results: if you are getting stronger on three days a week, back off a bit and see if the gains still come. The common mistake is thinking more training can speed up the slow process of adaptation. Strength comes slowly.

## Exercises: hip hinge

### Deadlift
- Pure strength, no cheating the range of motion: you pull from the ground and don't stop until you're standing. It works the legs but is primarily a posterior-chain exercise (hamstrings, glutes, low-back muscles). For building pure total-body strength, no exercise comes close.
- Technique: stance shoulder width or slightly narrower, toes slightly out or neutral; hips above knees, shoulders above hips; shins vertical with the bar touching them; grip outside the stance, around shoulder width; head, neck and spine neutral. Before lifting, take the slack out of the bar: tense the whole body as if to lift without lifting; proud chest, squeeze the bar, flex the lats, fire the glutes. Pull the bar up the shins with hips and shoulders rising together and a neutral spine. At the top, lock out fully by squeezing glutes and quads. Reverse the movement; once the bar is past the knees you can drop it to the floor.

### Kettlebell swing
- Used with almost all athletes, almost all the time; great for lower-body power and integration of movement. Start with the bell on the ground 30 cm or more in front of you, reach down and "hike" it back to start the reps. Squat only enough to get the bell between the legs (handle passing above the knees) and snap the hips forward to raise the bell to sternum height.
- **Warning:** swinging higher (the "American swing") diminishes the power component, turning it into an easy knee-dominant movement instead of a hip-dominant one. Many coaches caution against swinging overhead because of the dynamic lumbar flexion most athletes show at the top.

### Single-leg hip thrust
- Bodyweight hip hinge for when no tools are available; inferior to deadlifting and swinging. Lie on your back, bend one knee to 90 degrees with that foot planted, hold the other leg off the ground. Drive the heel of the planted foot into the ground and lift the hips until the spine aligns with the femur of the working leg ("straightening" the hip). Full control throughout. Do all the reps on one side before switching.

## Exercises: squat

### Single-leg squat
- The top of the food chain in leg training for athletes; requires high strength plus a lot of balance and mobility. It is motorically difficult and often hard enough at first to discourage athletes from practising it; over time it should become a staple.
- Technique: stand on a box or bench at least 41 cm high, one leg held in the air to the side, inside of the planted foot at the box edge. It can be done at bodyweight, but starting with a little weight in the hands helps balance at the bottom: a pair of 2.3 kg weights suits most athletes to start. Sit back and, as the hips drop, raise the weights to shoulder height in front of you. Squat until the top of the thigh is parallel to the floor; keep the heel planted and drive from there.

| Stage | Progression |
|---|---|
| Start | A pair of 2.3 kg weights in the hands |
| Progress | Add a little weight to the dumbbells |
| Weights over about 7 kg | This becomes problematic: progress to a pistol squat or add weight with a vest |

### Split stance
- Most adults lack the mobility to squat correctly without some training. Until that mobility comes, train strength in a split stance (split squats, lunges, step-ups): one leg at a time builds strength effectively and is very specific to sport movement.

### Rear-foot-elevated split squat
- A very good strength and balance exercise that is easy to master; usually the precursor to the single-leg squat but a staple on its own. Hold a pair of kettlebells or dumbbells (kettlebells preferred, easier to put on the ground if you fail). Stand tall in a lunge-like split with the rear foot, top of the foot down, on a 41-46 cm box or bench. With an upright spine, squat until the front thigh is parallel to the floor, then return to the top. Start with the weaker leg forward and always match reps with the stronger leg forward.

### Rack squat
- A front squat with kettlebells; for most athletes switching to kettlebells is a substantial improvement in the movement. The rack position forces a solid core and lets you move quickly between exercises (cleaning the bells and squatting is faster than setting up a squat bar). Start with two bells side by side on the ground, grab the handles and clean them to your shoulders (if you don't know the clean, learn it). In the rack: standing tall, bells in the crooks of the elbows, elbows tight against the ribs, forearms vertical, wrists straight. Sit "between the heels" while keeping the upright position. It's an easier squat variant; full range of motion is easier than with barbell variants.
- **Warning:** look for a clean, smooth movement; if there are hitches anywhere in the range of motion, back off a little on the weight. At the end of the set, reverse the clean to set the bells down, rather than dropping them in a side-to-side curl movement.

## Exercises: press

### One-arm kettlebell press
- A more advanced movement requiring significant core stability. Kettlebell preferred over dumbbell for overhead work: the offset centre of gravity helps open the shoulder and ensures full overhead extension. Start in the rack position and rotate to palm-forward at the top. Can be done with one or two bells; the one-arm version is simpler.

### One-arm push-up (progressed by inclining the upper body)

| Step | What to do |
|---|---|
| Set-up | Non-dominant hand on a countertop or box (or an Olympic bar in a rack at waist height); solid foot stance, feet slightly wider than shoulder width; brace the abs as if for a punch in the stomach |
| Rep | Lower into a full-range push-up until the working hand is within 2.5-5 cm of the chest; press up under full control |
| Other side | Repeat on the dominant side |
| Progression | When you master the prescribed reps at a given height, lower the implement by 5-7.5 cm and work back up to your max reps |

### One-arm kettlebell bench press
- Self-limiting; it forces two form corrections: the elbow stays alongside the torso, and you must brace from the core to stay on the bench. Set-up: stand with your back to the end of the bench, clean one bell to the rack, squat to the bench and sit; brace the bell with the non-working arm as you lie back. The bell stays in the rack position, though the elbow naturally drops back (down) as you lie down. Set a wide foot stance and press through a full range of motion. After the set, reverse the set-up: brace the bell with the non-working hand, sit up to a squat, stand, reverse the clean and carefully put the bell on the ground.
