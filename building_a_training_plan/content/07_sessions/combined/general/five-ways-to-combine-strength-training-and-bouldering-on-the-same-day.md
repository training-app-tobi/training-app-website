# Five Ways to Combine Strength Training and Bouldering on the Same Day

**Type:** Session · **Trains:** General strength, Power · **For:** needs full rest days between hard days

**TL;DR:** If you respond well to full rest days between intensive days, put strength training and bouldering on the same day instead of on separate days. Pick the intensity and duration of the strength work so it won't negatively affect your bouldering: high-skill climbing comes first, and lifting should support climbing, not replace it. Five formats: alternating boulders and strength sets on a rolling clock, blocks of boulders and strength, boulders then a density circuit, a full bouldering session then one lift, or a split day with strength in the morning and bouldering in the evening.

## Who should do what
| Situation / level | Do this |
|---|---|
| Respond well to full rest days between hard days | Instead of bouldering on Tuesday and Thursday and strength on Wednesday and Friday, put strength and bouldering on the same day |

## Why same-day strength and bouldering
- A training week can mix many things (e.g. bouldering on Tuesday, campusing on Friday, lifting weights a couple of days, climbing on the weekend). Putting strength and bouldering on the same day is a matter of balancing the two.
- Putting strength in its place: pick the intensity and duration of the strength sets so they won't negatively affect your bouldering. Going as hard as possible (the heaviest squat you can lift, then the heaviest deadlift you can lift) is probably not necessary for most climbers.
- High-skill climbing is always the most important; the only goal is sending harder and harder grades, not how much you can lift. Lifting should support climbing, not replace it.

## Variation 1: one boulder and one strength set on a rolling 5-minute clock
| Step | What to do |
|---|---|
| Each round | Climb one hard boulder problem, take off your shoes, walk over to e.g. the squat rack and do one set of squats, then rest the remainder of the rolling 5-minute clock |
| Total | About 15–20 boulder problems, but not 20 sets of squats |
| Block 1 | 4–5 boulder problems, each followed by squats |
| Block 2 | Next block of boulders paired with pull-ups |
| Block 3 | Next block of boulders paired with a core exercise |

- The typical strength session is stretched out between all the boulder problems.

## Variation 2: five boulders on a rolling 3-minute clock, then a 3x3
| Step | What to do |
|---|---|
| Bouldering | 5 boulder problems on a rolling 3-minute clock (15 minutes) |
| Strength | Take off your shoes, move to the weight training area and do the "3x3" (around 15 minutes for most athletes) |
| Break | Short break, then go back to bouldering |
| Repeats | Go through this 2–3 times |
| Total session | 1 to 1.5 hours |

- Recovery between boulders is tight, but with only 5 problems you don't dig too deep a hole.
- You recover from the bouldering during the strength work and from the strength work during the bouldering.
- If the strength work is burying you in a hole: most athletes can gain strength without getting close to their max. Do exercises you can do for 5–8 repetitions, and rest enough between sets to recover.
- **Warning:** Don't go CrossFit-style, seeking fatigue: you will be wiped out when you come back to the boulders. The goal is force production, doing a good job with both the bouldering and the strength training; fatigue should not be sought. This format breaks up fatigue and training overload rather than intensifying it.

## Variation 3: 15 boulders on a rolling 4-minute clock, then a 15-minute density circuit
| Step | What to do |
|---|---|
| Bouldering | 15 problems on a rolling 4-minute clock (one hour) |
| Break | 5–10 minutes |
| Density circuit | Pick an upper-body exercise, a lower-body exercise and a core exercise; do 5–7 repetitions of each with a load you could probably lift for 10 reps (a little lower intensity); rotate upper body, lower body, core, resting as needed, for 15 minutes |

- The rolling clock is not there to intensify the session but to keep you from going too hard.
- If you get fatigued and start to get pumped between problems, move to a rolling 5- or 6-minute clock: fatigue does not improve power and strength.
- Aim for a high-quality boulder problem every time you get on the wall, at a level that is challenging for you. On a rolling 1- or 2-minute clock you can only climb at a really low bouldering level, which isn't enough to develop skills or strength.
- This format works well when the weight training area is far from the bouldering area, and when the weight training area is busy.
- Set the circuit up in one spot of the weight training area and stay there for the 15 minutes, e.g. in a squat rack: deadlifts, pull-ups on the bar above the rack, toes-to-bar. Moving between three stations with other people around means waiting for others or having your equipment moved.
- The density circuit is a little fatiguing, so end the session after it instead of going back to climbing. If you want a bit more climbing, stretch it out, but don't go beyond about 15 minutes on the density circuit.

## Variation 4: a full bouldering session, then one lift a day
| Step | What to do |
|---|---|
| Bouldering | A complete, normal bouldering session (it can be the 15 problems on a 4-minute clock) |
| One lift a day | Pick one exercise only and do 4–6 sets at a high intensity, resting as much as needed between sets |
| Exercise choice | A big-bang exercise: a squat variation, a deadlift variation or one of the big upper-body movements |

- A typical hard to limit bouldering session is 60–90 minutes at most; most people get little strength and power benefit after that.
- No second exercise (no core, no legs on top). Don't add core, side abs, arms, calves and so on: one heavy big-bang exercise (e.g. a deadlift or a Turkish get-up) done with intensity is plenty for that day, and single exercises give more than you'd expect.

With two bouldering sessions in a week:

| Session | Followed by |
|---|---|
| First bouldering session | Deadlifts |
| Second bouldering session | Heavy weighted pull-ups |

## Variation 5: split day
| Time | What to do |
|---|---|
| Morning | A 30–45 minute strength session, where you can do each and every rep and could do more if you wanted, but stop at 45 minutes |
| Evening (after work) | Back in the gym, warm up and do a normal high-intensity bouldering session |

- One session in the morning, one in the evening. It needs a special schedule: the time, the motivation and the ability.
- A split day is not meant to accumulate more fatigue; it splits a single session into two to reduce fatigue.
- Do strength training in the morning and bouldering in the evening. It seems backwards, since bouldering is the priority, but it keeps the strength training in perspective: do strength training that still lets you have a full, hard bouldering session at the end of the day.
- If you're down (below your usual level) in the evening because of the morning strength session, correct it in the next iteration of the cycle: fewer sets or slightly lighter loads.
- **Warning:** If your strength numbers are going through the roof but your bouldering numbers are going down, that is not a training session to keep doing. The goal is progressing in climbing and staying strong enough to support it, not the other way round.
