# A 10-Minute Warm-Up for Climbing

**Type:** Session, Injury & health · **Trains:** Finger strength, Mobility, Antagonists & core

**TL;DR:** Three steps in about 10 minutes, with a single theraband, anywhere: tendon glides (10 cycles per hand), "No Moneys" for the shoulder external rotators (10-12 reps), and recruitment pulls on a ledge (5 pulls of 5 s per grip position, about 3-4 sets). They warm the finger flexors and extensors, stabilize the shoulder and prime nerves, muscles and pulleys so you pull at or close to full strength before getting on your project. Nordic hamstring curls are an optional extra for heel hooks and toe work.

## What the warm-up does
- A three-step warm-up that needs only a few minutes and a single theraband, and can be done anywhere. It targets the important muscle groups and tendons, stimulates neural activity, increases oxygen uptake and improves the activation potential of those muscle groups, all in about 10 minutes or less.
- These three activities are proposed as the optimal ones before climbing, not the only ones: other routines are fine, know your body and what works for you, and a longer warm-up (even 60 minutes) is fine if you have the patience.

## Why warm up at all
- Warm-up activities evoke temperature, metabolic, neural and psychological effects, including increased anaerobic metabolism, faster oxygen uptake kinetics and post-activation potentiation. Result: the body is primed for exercise, with more body awareness, better performance and less chance of injury.

## Step 1: Tendon glides
Pick three rows on the hand and touch the pads of the fingers to each row with as much range of motion as possible.

| Part | Movement |
|---|---|
| 1 | Fully flex the fingers to touch the pads to the top row of the hand, then open them all the way back up |
| 2 | Touch the pads to the middle of the palm, then open and fully extend the fingers again |
| 3 | Making an "L" shape with the hand, reach the pads to the base of the palm/wrist, then release and fully extend the fingers |
| Dose | Parts 1-3 = 1 cycle. Do 10 cycles on each hand |

- Can be done while walking to the crag or driving to the gym (one hand at a time if driving).
- Tip 1: put effort into it: squeeze harder and open wider. This creates more gliding of the tissue and warms the tissue more, through a greater contraction.
- Tip 2: move the wrist through flexion and extension during the glides. This causes even more excursion of the tendons and activates and warms up more muscles.

| Position | Effect |
|---|---|
| "Fist" | Maximum excursion of the flexor digitorum profundus (FDP) tendon |
| "Straight-fist" | Maximum excursion of the flexor digitorum superficialis (FDS) tendon |
| "Hook" | Maximum differential gliding: moves the FDP much more than the FDS |

### Why it works
- Tendon gliding has long been used in rehab: to treat tenosynovitis and degenerative joint disease, decrease swelling of the hand and the synovial spaces, improve hand dexterity and break up adhesions of the tendons to neighboring structures.
- Tendon gliding mainly moves the FDP and FDS, the two main muscle/tendon units used in climbing. The FDP takes the most force in crimps; the FDS helps more on slopers. It also improves joint mobility, which climbing tends to wear down.
- Tendon gliding also forces the extensor tendons to activate and glide. Climbing puts a lot of work on the outer elbow (lateral epicondylitis, tennis elbow), so getting those muscles and tendons moving and warm helps.
- The "L" position also activates the intrinsic hand muscles.
- The two flexor tendons in each finger have different excursions and can glide separately.
- Trauma and disease often produce adhesions that bind a tendon to its sheath, bone and neighboring tendons, causing loss of tendon gliding and joint motion, and therefore loss of strength and dexterity.
- Effort and wrist motion greatly increase tendon excursion in the fingers and the thumb; wrist motion increases gliding even more than effort.
- Tendon gliding can displace excess interstitial fluid, which can improve synovial edema and swelling, and produces isometric contraction of the extrinsic muscles and stretching of the intrinsic mechanism.

## Step 2: Shoulders (external rotators)
"No Moneys": bilateral external rotation with scapular retraction, with a resistance band.

| Part | How |
|---|---|
| Setup | Hold the band with both hands, squeeze the shoulder blades down and together (no shrugging) |
| Movement | Rotate the arms out to the side while keeping the elbows near the body |
| Hand position | Palms up preferred; thumbs up is also fine |
| Resistance | The band should be hard enough to stop you at 45-60 degrees out to the side; if not, use a harder band, double the band up, or hold it with the hands closer together |
| Dose | 10-12 reps, holding at the end of each rep for at least 1 second, not moving too quickly |

- Optional extra: external rotation at 90 degrees of shoulder elevation, which is even more specific to climbing because shoulder stability matters most at or above 90 degrees. Leave it out if you want to keep the warm-up short.

### Why it works
- The exercise trains the scapular retractors and the external rotators together, creating synergy between the two groups. This builds shoulder stability and shares the forces between the two groups instead of placing them all on the shoulder.
- The external rotators stabilize the shoulder, especially at or above 90 degrees, a position climbing puts you in often. They are 2 of the 4 rotator cuff muscles (50%).
- Research: external rotation should be done in neutral (0 degrees of abduction) to maximize isolation of the infraspinatus.
- Research: subscapularis and infraspinatus activated before all other muscles during the external rotation and internal rotation perturbation trials respectively.
- Research: the rotator cuff muscles pre-activate in anticipation of a perturbation, before their global synergists, so they are on and ready to stabilize the shoulder before a perturbation that could cause instability. Example: on a dry fire the force suddenly shifts; good strength and activation of the external rotators improve shoulder stability.
- Research: the middle and inferior parts of the infraspinatus, the teres minor and the posterior deep supraspinatus act as lateral (external) rotators.

## Step 3: Recruitment pulls
- Before getting on a project, load the tissue the way climbing will. Use a ledge (a rock) or a portable ledge and go through the grip positions the climb needs: half crimp, open hand or even full crimp. With extra time, and to maximize the warm-up and minimize injury risk, go through all the positions.

| Per grip position | Dose |
|---|---|
| Pull | As hard as you comfortably can for 5 s |
| Rest | Let off and rest 1 s |
| Reps | Repeat 4 more times (5 pulls) |
| Sets | Multiple sets, about 3-4, before you climb |
| Climb with more crimps | At least 2-3 sets in that position |

- You get stronger with each set, because nerve conduction speed improves, muscle contractility improves and the tissue warms up.
- One hand or both: can be done with both hands together or one at a time; one hand is recommended. It is more realistic for climbing, and it lets you reach a maximal contraction: you probably can't pull your body off the ground with one hand, so the contraction is truly isometric.
- **Warning:** Don't pull on your project before you can pull at or close to full strength: that invites injury and you start the session unprepared. Recruitment pulls make you stronger and safer on the wall than climbing without a warm-up.

### Why it works
- Recruitment pulls improve nerve conduction speed, the contractility of the muscle unit and warm up the muscle and tendon units. This improves recruitment patterns of muscles such as the FDP and FDS and primes the pulley system for the strain of climbing.
- Why a move suddenly "goes" after a few nearly impossible attempts: partly small adjustments in how you hold the hold, partly the same thing recruitment pulls do: nerve conduction velocity and motor unit strength improve, and the brain learns the hold is OK to hold onto, where on the first pulls it let go quickly. Recruitment pulls reproduce this so you hold on more securely from the start, and load the tissue in a way that tells the brain what is OK and what is not, which further helps injury prevention.

## Optional: Nordic hamstring curls
- Warming up the hamstrings and posterior chain can be critical for climbing performance: strong heel hooks, and good toe work keeping you close to the wall.

| Part | How |
|---|---|
| Setup | No partner or machine needed: put a pad under the knees, scoot the feet back against a wall (or rock) with the foot dorsiflexed (toes pulled up toward the face), back as far as you can go: that is the start position |
| Stabilize | Engage the core and squeeze the glutes (really important) to stabilize the midsection before moving |
| Movement | Lean forward slowly, feeling the hamstrings work. Start with very small motions and go further and further |
| Reps | Don't reach failure (falling forward) until about 8 reps in |

- Single-leg and rotation-based variations exist; move to them only once you are familiar with the basic exercise.
