# Making the Most of Warm-Up Climbs

**Type:** Session, Technique · **Trains:** Technique · **For:** limit or projecting days

**TL;DR:** The first few climbs are an extension of the warm-up on the ground, building towards peak activation until you're ready to climb at your limit. Make every warm-up climb count: use it to practise one lagging technique, take an intentional fall to get the fear out early, and pick easier climbs that mimic the style of the day's project.

## Warm-up climbs are part of the warm-up
- The warm-up doesn't end when you pull onto the wall. Treat the first few climbs as an extension of the warm-up on the ground: each warm-up climb takes you closer to peak activation, until you're fully ready to climb at your limit.
- **Warning:** Warm-up climbs should carry weight of their own. Picking routes at random and getting flash-pumped (or not pumped at all) adds little to your projecting or your growth as a climber. Make every climb count instead of logging "junk miles".

## Work your weaknesses
- Warm-up climbs shouldn't tax you much: start from zero and work your way up. Low challenge doesn't mean low effort. Because you don't have to decipher complex beta or navigate tough cruxes, the energy saved can go into dialing in technique that slips on harder climbs, where you're focused on simply staying on the route.
- Pick one technique that lags behind your overall skill set and use the warm-up climbs to bring it up. Climbing below your limit lets you make the climb what you need, because you're not constrained by specific beta or discomfort on the terrain.

| Weakness | How to use the warm-up climb |
|---|---|
| Footwork | Structure the climb with a drill (e.g. target practice) and place your feet as delicately and accurately as possible |
| "Revving the engine" (power) | Make every move powerful and dynamic, even if the climb doesn't need it |

- Over time these climbs add up to hours of practice; spent on weaknesses, they turn a mindless routine into a training opportunity.

## Take the whip
- Often it's not physical ability that holds climbers back on redpoint attempts but fear of failing and falling: if you're too afraid of falling to try hard enough that you might fall, you never reach your potential.
- Take an intentional fall on a warm-up climb. Choosing the fall puts you in control instead of having it forced on you on harder climbs; practice falls prime the mind for the sensation of falling so it is less of a threat later and remove the intimidation factor. Get the fear out of your system early so you can go into harder terrain with confidence later in the day.

## Mimic your project
- Choose warm-up climbs that mimic the style of the limit climbs you're working up to: easier versions of the day's main objectives.
- Climbs that replicate the angles, textures, grips and lengths of the project carry over best; also consider mental factors such as runouts and fall zones. Example: slab won't prepare you for a cave project, or vice versa.
- This builds confidence on similar terrain and wakes up the muscles you'll need.
