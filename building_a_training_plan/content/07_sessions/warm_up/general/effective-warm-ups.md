# Effective Warm-Ups

**Type:** Session, Principles · **Trains:** General strength, Finger strength, Power, Endurance, Mobility · **For:** Youth, Older, Advanced, projecting at your limit

**TL;DR:** Training is what makes you stronger; warming up right is what makes training work. Tailor the warm-up to the session: a general warm-up (5–15 min of steady, easy activity until you feel warm), a specific warm-up (5–10 min of movement preparation similar to the coming session), then, depending on the session, a progressive warm-up (10–30 min), a crag warm-up (15–60 min) or a bouldering warm-up (15–30 min). Simply doing some climbing is a fair warm-up, but optimizing it can mean the world to a climber pushing against the hardest climbing they've ever done.

## Who should do what

| Situation / level | Do this |
|---|---|
| Younger athletes | Do, and need, less warming up |
| Older athletes | Do much more warming up (they sometimes joke that warming up is all they do) |
| Before a route that requires a lot of power | A long, pumping route first thing in the morning is not the best choice (it may help you warm up for similar climbs) |
| Warming up for endurance climbing | Bringing a hangboard to the crag is not the best choice |
| Outdoors, low-intensity activities (hiking, easy alpine climbs) | The walk from the tent to the cliff is more than enough to prime the body for the climb |
| Gym sessions | Start with low-intensity work, such as walking on a treadmill for 10 min. It's boring; do it anyway |
| Before climbing | Include progressively harder upper-body movements, get the fingers ready for small holds, and get the hips and shoulders ready for full-range activity |
| Especially intense sessions | Follow the specific warm-up with a progressive warm-up: progressively more intense versions of the training exercises |
| Campusing and hard bouldering | Start easy, keep each set under 10 s, and work your way up until you feel primed |
| Roadside crag | Take a short walk before the "approach" begins. You should feel physically warm by the time you reach the crag |
| A day of many pitches at onsight level or easier | Volume warm-up: simply ramp up the grades from the warm-up |
| Projecting an endurance-oriented route | Endurance warm-up (see the crag warm-up table) |
| Routes with hard moves or difficult grips, crags with tweaky pockets | Warm up off the rock: at the gym or at the car, as much as an hour before climbing |
| More advanced climbers | Make the bouldering warm-up series longer and more intense |
| Climbing at your limit | Optimizing the warm-up takes a little more effort, but can mean the world to a climber pushing against the hardest climbing they've ever done |

## Why and how to warm up

- Training is what makes you stronger; warming up right is what makes training work. The warm-up gets the body working right for hard activity: it increases blood flow and respiration, raises body temperature a couple of notches, and gets the mind in the right place.
- Most climbers warm up by feel (start easy, and after a few minutes of gentle activity feel ready to go) or just climb to get going. There is probably a better and more effective way to prime yourself for a good session.
- Like training, warm-ups should be specific: tailor the warm-up to the requirements of the session.
- Most organized warm-ups have a general and a specific component. More intense sessions also have a progressive warm-up: slowly ramp up intensity with lighter versions of the main exercises of the coming session.

| Phase | Duration | Purpose and content |
|---|---|---|
| 1. General warm-up | 5–15 min | Steady, easy activity. Raises the body's operating temperature, primes the blood vessels for activity and increases your capacity for delivering oxygen |
| 2. Specific warm-up | 5–10 min | Movement preparation with movements and durations similar to the coming session |
| 3a. Progressive warm-up | 10–30 min | For especially intense sessions: progressively more intense versions of the training exercises |
| 3b. Crag warm-up | 15–60 min | Ramp up on the rock, matched to the day's goal (volume, endurance, strength and power) |
| 3c. Bouldering warm-up | 15–30 min | A series of boulder problems adding up to your limit grade |

## Step 1: general warm-up (5–15 min)

- Outdoors this happens naturally on the approach to most crags.
- In a gym it's hard to get this first step done the same way, and starting a climbing or weight session with low-intensity work, such as walking on a treadmill for 10 min, is boring. Do it anyway.
- Dose: steady, easy activity for 5–15 min. By the end you should feel warm (taking off warm layers, possibly starting to sweat a little), with breathing up and heart rate starting to make itself known.
- Keep it simple and conversational: it's the time to transition from the outside world to training time, catch up with training partners and finalize the details of the session.

## Step 2: specific warm-up (5–10 min)

- Follows directly after the general warm-up. For many, it is a movement-preparation sequence: 8–10 exercises that take you through full ranges of motion and start to cross over into strength training, e.g. bodyweight squats, inchworms, inverted rows and toy soldiers.
- The specific warm-up should feature movements and durations similar to the coming session.
- **Warning:** at the crag the specific warm-up is more complicated: it's very easy to over- or under-shoot, and simply doing some easier pitches doesn't always work. You still follow a slow intensification of the moves of the sport, but it varies with what you're training for (see the crag warm-up below).

## Step 3, variation 1: progressive warm-up (10–30 min)

- For especially intense sessions, follow the specific warm-up with progressively more intense versions of the training exercises. This ramps the body up to the intensity the session requires and primes the movement patterns you plan to use.

Max-strength example, deadlift as primary lift:

| Step | Content |
|---|---|
| 1 | General warm-up, 5–10 min |
| 2 | Heavier work such as push-ups, light goblet squats and swings |
| 3 | A few reps at 61 kg |
| 4 | A couple of reps at 102 kg |
| 5 | A couple of reps at 143 kg |
| 6 | Final warm-up set of 2 about 10% below training weight, e.g. 165.5 kg |
| 7 | Work sets, e.g. a few sets of doubles at 183.5 kg |

- **Warning:** keep warm-up reps below 4 or 5 even when the weight is light, to avoid leaving alactic metabolism.
- Take as much time between warm-up lifts as necessary, and do jumps and other quick movements in between to keep reminding the body to be powerful and quick.
- This part of the session is critical to success and should take as long as you need: anywhere from 10 to 30 min.

## Step 3, variation 2: crag warm-up (15–60 min)

- The approach to most crags is an ideal general warm-up.
- Once at the objective, almost all boulderers and climbers can start their specific warm-up with an easy pitch or a few very easy boulder problems.

| Day's goal | Warm-up sequence |
|---|---|
| Volume: a lot of pitches at onsight level or easier | Simply ramp up the grades from the warm-up, e.g. 10a, 10c, 11c, then into the day's 12a and 12b routes (or whatever your level) |
| Endurance: projecting an endurance-oriented route (e.g. 12c) | 1. One easy pitch (10a). 2. A back-to-back pair on a route 4–6 grades below the objective (e.g. 11b): first lap slow and precise, second lap solid and quick, no break between laps other than lowering to the ground, aiming for a slight pump toward the end of the second lap. 3. One more pitch 1–2 grades harder (11d). 4. Rest about 20 min before trying the project |

- **Warning:** warming up for power-oriented and strength-dependent climbs is more difficult: bouldering on tiny holds is hard to prepare for by bouldering on big holds, and for routes with hard moves or difficult grips a warm-up circuit at the crag is of limited value.
- Portable hangboards are now light and well designed, which makes warming up at the crag easy.

8-minute warm-up on a suspended hangboard:

| Minute | Content |
|---|---|
| 1 | One pull-up every 10 s |
| 2 | Hang, large edge, 20 s |
| 3 | Hang, large edge, 20 s + 2 pull-ups |
| 4 | Hang medium edge 10 s, rest 10 s, hang medium edge 10 s |
| 5 | Hang small edge 5 s, rest 20 s, hang medium edge 10 s |
| 6 | Hang, large edge, 20 s + 3 pull-ups |
| 7 | Hang small edge 5 s, rest 5 s (×4 rounds) |
| 8 | Hang small edge 5 s, rest 5 s, with elbows at 90° (×4 rounds) |

- The grips in the sequence can be swapped for the specific grips on that day's project.

## Step 3, variation 3: bouldering warm-up (15–30 min)

- Comes after the general warm-up and movement prep in a gym session. Done as a simple, fun series, longer and more intense for more advanced climbers, using boulder problems on as many angles and hold types as possible.

Series, each stage adding up to your limit grade (example: limit V9):

| Stage | Problems | Pace | Then |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | As many easy V1/V2 problems as it takes to add up to your limit grade (e.g. V2, V1, V1, V2, V2, V1) | Slow and in control | Rest and stretch a couple of minutes |
| 2 | Three problems in a row with little rest that add up to your limit grade (e.g. V2, V3, V4) | As quickly and explosively as possible | Rest a few minutes |
| 3 | Two problems that add up to your limit grade (e.g. V4, V5) | Your normal pace | |
| 4 | Some specific hold positions on the hangboard, if necessary, depending on the nature of the problems you're working | | |

## Is it worth the effort

- Simply doing some climbing is a fair warm-up. Optimizing performance takes a little more effort, but can mean the world to a climber pushing against the hardest climbing they've ever done.
