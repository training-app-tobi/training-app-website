# Order Within a Session: Finger-Intensive Work First, Conditioning Last

**Type:** Session, Principles · **Trains:** Finger strength, Power, Power endurance, Endurance, General strength

**TL;DR:** After a really thorough warm-up, put anything highly intense on the fingers first (max hangs, campusing, board climbing, project repetitions): it carries the highest injury rate and works best fresh. Endurance and some power endurance go in the middle to end, and general strength and conditioning for the larger muscles can go at the end, even when you want a stronger deadlift. If you are 50% off your usual pace, skip the intense work; keep any endurance warm-up short and super light.

## Order within a session
- **Warning:** for anything highly intense on the fingers, do a really thorough warm-up, then make it the first part of the session. It carries the highest injury rate (catching holds quickly, engaging at high intensity). E.g. never leave a max hangs session on a fingerboard until later in the session: don't pre-fatigue before high-intensity strength or power training, particularly for structures that are more likely to be injured.
- Fingers are more delicate structures, so very high-intensity finger work is the absolute priority. Climbers who do it first get better results from their training and see their injury rate go down.

| Part of the session | What goes there | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Start (after a really thorough warm-up) | Finger-intensive work: fingerboard max sessions for strength, campus work, intense bouldering such as board climbing or repetitions on projects. On a split day, it goes in the first session of the day, in the same order | Highest injury rate; fingers are delicate structures |
| Middle to end | Endurance and some power endurance work | Lower intensity than maximal work, so you can still hit the intensity you aimed for |
| End (can go here, doesn't have to) | General strength and conditioning for the larger muscle groups (weights, floor work, bar work) | Those muscles aren't heavily fatigued by climbing-specific work, so quality stays high |

- Check on quality: if you can operate at or close to your maximum in the session, you made the right choice. If you know you normally manage this fresh but you are 50% off the pace, you are too tired: don't do the really intense work, as you are not at the intensity needed for the session's aims.
- Weight training goes towards the end even if you want to improve max strength: a weightlifter puts it first, but a climber's priority is the climbing-specific work. It will compromise your deadlift and bench press, but if lifting always comes first, your body trains to be a lifter instead of a climber.
- Longer endurance work at the end of a session, while fatigued, forces you to move more efficiently: moving better makes you feel less tired, a positive feedback. A good way to get less efficient climbers to move more efficiently.
- **Warning:** endurance at the start of a session as a warm-up is fine, but keep it really short and super light. Boulderers especially tend to overcook it and go too hard, which hurts the quality later in the session. There's no harm in going lighter than you think.
