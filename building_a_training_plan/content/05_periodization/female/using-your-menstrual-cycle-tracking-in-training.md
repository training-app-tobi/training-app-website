# Using Your Menstrual Cycle Tracking in Training

**Type:** Planning, Injury & health · **Trains:** Recovery · **For:** Female

**TL;DR:** After 3–6 months of tracking your cycle, use the patterns you notice to adapt your training. In low-motivation phases, shape your climbing around what motivates you; schedule phases of reduced recovery as rest days, lighter days or a deload week; and adjust session intensity so your RPE stays in the correct range. Inconsistent or changing cycle lengths could indicate low energy availability: review your load and recovery, and see a doctor if you are concerned or miss periods.

## After a few months of tracking
- After 3–6 months of tracking your cycle, you may notice days or phases where your training feels affected. Not everyone notices a change in their training with their cycle.
- Use what you notice together with the strategies below.

## Trends in motivation
- Motivation helps long-term consistency. A repeating pattern of lower motivation for days or short phases may be due to your cycle.
- Work out what really motivates you: which sessions you enjoy, and which feel like the path of least resistance.
- During the low-motivation phase, shape your climbing and training around what motivates you at that time. Example: if social climbing gets you to the wall, schedule fun climbs around that time.
- This can keep you consistent in the long run and motivated when your structured training comes back around.

## Syncing training with your cycle
- Training has several pillars, e.g. recovery, progressive overload and getting the training intensity right. If your tracking data shows your cycle interacting with one of these pillars, you may want to adapt your training.
- Example: disturbed sleep in the premenstrual phase makes recovery feel reduced. If tracking shows this consistently just before menstruation, schedule that part of your cycle as rest days or lighter days.
- Most training plans (and less structured climbing schedules) have some flexibility. Options for days where recovery feels reduced:

| Option | What to do |
|---|---|
| Schedule around them | Nothing changes in the overall plan; you simply schedule around the days where recovery feels reduced |
| Deload | Use that time for a deload week or lighter-load week |

## Trends in rating of perceived effort (RPE)
- If the same exercises feel harder, more tiring or more demanding at different points in your cycle, change the intensity of your sessions so your RPE stays within the correct range for your training from session to session, even if this means reducing the intensity.
- Example: at one point in your cycle you may do your endurance training on a lower grade than the previous session but reach the same RPE.
- Check how you are feeling in the session; do this as part of your warm-up.

## Changes in cycle length
- Inconsistent or changing cycle lengths could indicate low energy availability.
- Look at your recent climbing and training: has the load been high? Has it changed? Are other things in your life demanding your energy?
- Evaluate your energy expenditure, load and time available for rest and recovery, and consider adjusting them.
- **Warning:** If you are concerned about a change in your cycle, or you miss periods, go to your doctor.
