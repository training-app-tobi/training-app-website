# Strength Training and the Menstrual Cycle: Gain Then Maintain

**Type:** Planning, Principles · **Trains:** General strength, Finger strength, Power · **For:** Female

**TL;DR:** Hormones fluctuate throughout the whole cycle, so training can be adjusted to work with your physiology, without letting cycle phase dictate everything. Focus on making strength gains in the follicular phase (low progesterone, rising estrogen, better muscle protein synthesis and recovery) and maintain them in the luteal phase: keep strength training in both phases, with more sessions in the follicular phase (e.g. 4 a week in the follicular phase, 3 a week in the luteal phase).

## Why optimise around the cycle
- Hormones fluctuate throughout the whole menstrual cycle, not only during the period or the premenstrual phase, so the approach to training can be adjusted throughout the cycle to work with your physiology.

## Body literacy
- Getting the most out of training with the menstrual cycle is largely about body literacy: building awareness of the cues your body gives you, which differ across the phases of the cycle, and of how these cues affect your training.
- There are two ways to optimise training: look at what is going on in training (hitting the right training stimulus at the right time) and look at what is going on around training (fuel, stress and sleep).

| Way to optimise | How you feel | Not likely to be optimal | Do instead |
|---|---|---|---|
| In training | Bloated and fatigued | Trying to increase the load on pull-ups | A lighter aerobic capacity session, so you hit the training stimulus more easily |
| Around training | After a poor night's sleep | High-coordination sessions | Simple max hangs, so the training stimulus may be met more easily |

- Optimising training in line with the menstrual cycle is never about not doing something because of your cycle.

## What is realistic
- Considering your cycle phase in everything you do is undesirable and unsustainable; there is a balance to find. Cycle phase may affect both psychology and physical performance, but a certain amount of "pushing through" is needed in training and climbing, and cycle phase should not be the sole dictator of what you do and when.
- **Warning:** Don't let your experience one month predetermine how you will feel the next month. Trying to adapt training for every aspect of the cycle is like trying to hit a moving target.

| Step | What to do |
|---|---|
| 1. Track | Track your cycle symptoms and how they affect your training for a period of time. Don't do it forever and don't get obsessive about it; it gives useful information about changes you could make. |
| 2. Pick the "low hanging fruit" | Make a few changes, e.g. arrange your rest week during the PMS (premenstrual) phase. |
| 3. Keep the changes and check in | Continue with the changes for a few menstrual cycles, then reflect on whether they helped (analysing every cycle is not useful). If things went well, revisit your tracking and introduce another change, the "higher hanging fruit", e.g. changing the focus of your training throughout a cycle. |

## What the research says
- Sports performance research has historically been done with male participants, partly because of the complexity and expense the menstrual cycle adds.
- Results on cycle phase and performance are extremely variable: you cannot say that on day X of your cycle your performance will be a certain way. Many studies looked at acute performance (a one-off performance on a given day) in a simple strength or endurance test (e.g. leg press, time to failure on an exercise bike), and a recent meta-analysis found little difference between phases.
- A large portion of the studies in that meta-analysis are considered to have "poor" methodology, because research on the menstrual cycle in sport is relatively new and the best methods are still being established.
- Researchers look for statistically significant differences, which may not match a meaningful change for an individual. Most studies use basic strength or endurance tests, which may not reflect performance in a complex, multifaceted sport such as climbing (climbing performance cannot be distilled down to a weighted pull-up). This may be why athletes overwhelmingly perceive the cycle to affect their sport although research has drawn no solid conclusions.
- There is much less research on adaptation to strength training over the course of the cycle: studies that compare different distributions of training across the cycle to see which gives a better outcome. Syncing training with the cycle to optimise performance comes from this area.

## Gain then maintain: the follicular phase
- The follicular phase runs from day 1 of bleeding to ovulation. Progesterone is low; estrogen starts low and rises throughout, ending in a spike that stimulates ovulation.
- In theory the follicular phase allows better adaptations to strength training and shorter recovery times (adaptations to training happen during recovery), so it may be a good time to focus on strength and high-intensity training.
- Day 1 of the low hormone phase is day 1 of bleeding, so shifting the focus to strength and high-intensity training may depend on the symptoms you have during menses, such as cramps.

## Gain then maintain: distributing strength sessions
- Focus on making strength gains in the follicular phase, then maintain them in the luteal phase. This does not mean strength training only in the follicular phase and none in the luteal phase: distribute strength sessions between both phases, with more sessions in the follicular phase. Reducing the volume of strength training in the luteal phase increases the recovery time between high-intensity sessions.

| Phase | Strength focus | Example: 4 strength sessions a week (two bouldering, one fingerboard, one pull-up session) |
|---|---|---|
| Follicular | Make strength gains | 4 sessions a week |
| Luteal | Maintain strength gains | 3 sessions a week |

- Instead of doing 4 sessions every week throughout the cycle, the aim is to keep the quality of the sessions and allow time to recover from them, which could lead to better adaptations, more consistent energy levels and less risk of injury from desperately trying to match what you did the previous week.

## Options instead of dropping a session
Example: a boulderer whose training is mostly strength and power on short, powerful boulders.

| Option | How |
|---|---|
| Reduce the intensity of some (not all) strength sessions in the luteal phase | Do mileage or repeats on boulders you have already completed, or keep the weights you use in fingerboard or other conditioning sessions the same rather than pushing them |
| Reduce the volume of higher-intensity sessions | Reduce the length of the session or the sets/reps in higher-intensity bouldering or conditioning sessions |
| Swap a strength session for aerobic work | Do a short interval-style aerobic session instead; a level of endurance is still important for boulderers because it helps PCr (phosphocreatine) recovery, the energy system that fuels high-intensity exercise |
| Simplify movement in the premenstrual phase | Use conditioning exercises with basic movement patterns for strength training, as reduced coordination is commonly experienced in this phase |

- Pushing followed by consolidation is a good way to make consistent and "safe" (lower injury risk) training gains; here it is simply synced with the cycle. It may feel like it slows the rate of progress, but it generally leads to a more sustainable process over time.

## Why it works
- Progesterone has catabolic effects on protein metabolism and estrogen is anabolic (catabolic processes break things down, anabolic processes build things up). Muscle protein synthesis (MPS) is anabolic, so low progesterone and rising estrogen in the follicular phase allow better MPS and recovery than in the luteal phase, when progesterone is high.
