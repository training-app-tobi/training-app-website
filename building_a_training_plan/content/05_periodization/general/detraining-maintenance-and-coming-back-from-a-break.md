# Detraining, Maintenance and Coming Back from a Break

**Type:** Principles, Planning · **Trains:** General strength, Finger strength, Power, Power endurance, Endurance · **For:** time off or travel, returning after a layoff, injury or illness, Older

**TL;DR:** Maximal strength, power and muscle mass hold for weeks without training, while strength endurance and aerobic endurance fade within one to two weeks; older adults lose fitness about twice as fast. Doing a little bit is profoundly better than doing nothing: on a week away, climb every 3–4 days, hang a bit, do 20–25 min of strength work a couple of times and walk briskly 30 min every day. After more than 2 weeks immobile, rebuild capacity first, then restart at 50–60% of previous intensity and build back over 3–4 weeks.

## Who should do what

| Situation / level | Do this |
|---|---|
| Older adults | Expect around 2× the strength loss of younger athletes across any time period, and a sooner and quicker decline in every facet |
| Parts of the season focused on other performance factors | It's OK to let strength endurance go: it can be rebuilt much faster than strength or power |
| A full week away from climbing | No need to obsess over finding a gym with everything you train with at home. Follow the one-week maintenance plan below |
| Immobile for more than 2 weeks | Rebuild capacity for activity first: a few walks and maybe some light weight training for a week or two, not an easy climbing session. Then follow the return progression below |

## Less climbing for a season, still training a little

- Case: a month (September) with much less climbing because another seasonal activity takes over, but still training on the days spent at the gym or on a home wall. Result: not completely detrained, a good general level of capacity held, and expected to be back within 10% of the best climbing fitness of the past few years by November.
- Embracing the change of activity works because getting back into climbing doesn't take long, and simply knowing the fitness will come back takes a lot of stress off (no need to carry a portable hangboard everywhere).

## The four facets of fitness

- Detraining is any reversal of performance potential in a given facet of fitness. Depending on the facet, it occurs anywhere between a few days and nearly a month of complete cessation of training.
- Research on detraining focuses on four facets of fitness: strength (and power), strength-endurance, hypertrophy and aerobic endurance. Their rates of detraining, need for maintenance and rebuilding windows all differ a little.

| Facet | How fast it detrains | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Maximal strength | For about 4 weeks it is pretty much unaffected by stopping all activity | After a month off you won't pull on the crimps of a hard boulder (e.g. the Mandala) with quite the same high force, but you're probably not far off. Leg strength, pulling strength and core tension will probably stay close to their top values for that month, then taper off precipitously |
| Power | Losses are less profound than strength losses, but for all practical purposes classified the same | The difference might be due to the relatively smaller gains in power than in strength. A few weeks without explosive training won't affect the top end much |
| Strength endurance (also called power endurance or aerobic power) | Declines sooner and more profoundly than strength and power: a couple of weeks away from strength-endurance training will leave you feeling profoundly detrained | Likely because of the complex combination of factors behind this kind of endurance. Data vary by muscle group, age and duration of training. Can be rebuilt much faster than strength or power |
| Hypertrophy (muscle mass) | More persistent than strength, power or strength endurance: mass stays relatively steady for 7 or more weeks after stopping training | |
| Aerobic endurance | Declines within about a week of stopping training | Being very fit aerobically is "expensive" for the body, so it detrains at an accelerated rate when you stop; this is why runners, swimmers and triathletes have to train almost daily to improve |

- Older adults see around 2× the strength loss of younger athletes across any time period (a 2013 meta-analysis): after 4 weeks, a 50-year-old will see a huge decline compared with what she would have lost 20 years earlier. As with all the other facets, older adults decline sooner and more quickly than younger ones.
- Climbers generally aren't concerned about maintaining muscle mass, partly from the common belief that lighter is better and partly from the understanding that bigger muscles probably aren't that important to hard climbing. What is essential is to mitigate the loss of the muscle mass you have if you want to maintain or increase your performance in the future.

## Maintenance during time off

- Doing a little bit is profoundly better than doing nothing.
- A portable hangboard in your carry-on luggage is totally worth bringing on a trip, even if you use it just minutes a day a couple of times during the trip.
- Doing almost anything, even going for a walk a few times a week, significantly reduces the effects of detraining: even just being active is really important.

One-week maintenance plan (a full week off climbing):

| What | How often |
|---|---|
| Short climbing session | Every 3–4 days |
| Hang by your fingers | A bit |
| Strength training, 20–25 min | Probably a couple of times |
| Brisk walk, 30 min | Every day |

### Hotel-gym maintenance workout

- Equipment: a hotel gym can be counted on for some ellipticals, a treadmill and usually a set of dumbbells up to 18–22.5 kg. Bring a stopwatch.

| Part | What to do |
|---|---|
| Warm-up | A 5 min "approach" hike on the treadmill at the steepest grade it allows; be really warm and breathing hard by the end |
| Main set | Set a 20 min timer and do as many rounds as possible of: 8 push-ups, 8 front squats, 8 dumbbell rows each side, 8 step-ups holding 2 dumbbells (one each side), 15 s hardstyle plank |
| Rest and load | Rest as needed. Load each exercise so every set is hard but doable; if your tempo slows at the end of any exercise, reduce the load. Usually 5–6 rounds fit in the 20 min; getting through much faster means the loads are too light |
| Hangboard | A 10–15 min session of 10 s hangs at various arm angles and on varied grips does the trick |

- This isn't a pure strength workout, but it does a great job of maintaining strength for a few days, and the whole hotel-gym visit takes less than 30 min.

## Coming back from a break

- Even some random light exercise keeps you around the same muscle mass for months and around the same strength and power levels for 4–5 weeks.
- **Warning:** on bed rest with very little activity, you lose fitness fast.

Return after more than 2 weeks immobile:

| Step | What to do | Move on when |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Rebuild capacity for activity | A few walks and maybe some light weight training for a week or two (not an easy climbing session) | You can do 30–60 min of movement without soreness or fatigue |
| 2. Restart training | Start back into your normal training routine at 50–60% of your previous intensity (the general wisdom) | |
| 3. Progress | A slow progression, normally 3–4 weeks | Back to your pre-immobility values |
