# Periodization Basics: The Training Pie, Variation, Specificity and Simplicity

**Type:** Principles, Planning

**TL;DR:** Periodization is the planned manipulation of training variables to maximise adaptation and prevent overtraining syndrome; its main lesson is to avoid maximal intensity with high volume for much of the year and to build up to it slowly. Training, climbing and recovery share a pie of fixed size, so when one slice grows another must shrink. Do a specific session at least 10 and at most 20 times per cycle before changing it, and keep the program simple enough to stick with.

## Periodization
- Periodization: planned manipulation of training variables (load, sets, reps) to maximize adaptations and prevent overtraining syndrome, in which strength and endurance drop and athletes get symptoms ranging from mental health issues to poor sleep and more frequent sickness; the body is pushed into a hole so deep it can't recover.
- Main takeaway of all periodization models: avoid training at maximal intensity with high volume for much of the year, and build up to it slowly. Include periods of lower intensity and volume to avoid overtraining. The detail of the different models isn't needed for climbers unless they are the best of the best.
- The "training pie": training, climbing and recovery take differently sized slices that change (and should change) through the year, but the size of the pie never changes; when one slice gets bigger, another must get smaller. Slice sizes depend on the individual.
- Variation: changing the variables that alter the overload stimulus (sets, reps, rest, exercise type, prescribed intensity) and exercise selection. Not changing exercises every week or two, but avoiding doing the same exercises so long that gains plateau.
- Guideline: do a specific session at least 10 times in a training cycle but no more than 20 before changing things around a bit.
- Specificity (mechanical): how closely an exercise's attributes resemble the demands of the sport: contraction type (eccentric-concentric or isometric), rate of force development (rapid or slow), and amplitude and direction of movement.
- Simplicity: consistency matters most. Complicated periodization, load manipulation and other strategies are for elite athletes who have exhausted all other avenues to improvement. Put together a simple program you'll stick with, progressively overload, sleep well, recover between sessions and stick with it for a long time.
