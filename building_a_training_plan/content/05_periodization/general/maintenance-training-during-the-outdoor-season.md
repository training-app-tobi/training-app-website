# Maintenance Training During the Outdoor Season

**Type:** Planning · **Trains:** Finger strength, Pulling strength, Power, Mobility · **For:** outdoor climbing season

**TL;DR:** During the outdoor season, neither stop training nor keep all of it on top of climbing: do maintenance training to slow or prevent a dip in maximal strength and power. Keep intensity high but cut volume (e.g. half the sets), keep loading consistent, and take deload weeks more often (e.g. 2 weeks on, 1 week off). Prioritise a few qualities, such as finger strength, shoulder mobility and max pull strength, at static loads set from a test before the season.

## Why the two usual approaches fail
- Stopping training for the outdoor season: as the season goes on, strength and power decline, and shoulders start to complain from hours spent slumped in the harness. Focusing on one project improves that set of moves while other planes of movement and grip types suffer; you rely on gaining specificity faster than you lose strength. At the end of the season, getting back to training feels hard, hanging off a fingerboard feels foreign, and benchmark sessions usually come in below where you were, so effort goes into getting back to the previous level.
- **Warning:** Keeping all your training and adding outdoor climbing on top: you may feel good for a couple of weeks, then performance takes a nosedive, fingers feel tweaky and you feel fatigued every morning. You then have to dial back both training and climbing and hope to recover enough to enjoy climbing outdoors, or you spend the summer nursing complaints in fingers, shoulders and elbows and the first training cycles after the season go on rehab. Either way, strength is not maintained.
- A balanced approach between training and climbing is best: maintenance training.

## Why maintenance training
- Aim of maintenance training: slow or prevent a dip in maximal strength and power, and maintain functional movement and general health during a peak season. This matters for year-on-year improvement and for reducing injury risk.
- Outdoor climbing aims to make movement as efficient as possible, using all muscles and physical attributes together, so a single element such as finger strength is not targeted the way it is in training and becomes relatively detrained.
- Focusing on one route or style of route builds specific strength for certain terrain, movement styles and grip types, which reduces variety of movement and can overwork certain muscle groups and tendons.
- **Warning:** Going all out on projects puts a lot of strain on the body; it is harder to walk away, and you want to make the most of days out. Pushing hard on projects makes it easy to pick up niggles and injuries.

## How to do maintenance training
- Mindset: expect maintenance, not big gains, during this period.

| Principle | How | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Keep intensity high, reduce volume | Keeps top-end performance without adding too much fatigue | Cut the number of sets in half |
| Consistent loading | Keep the number of sessions and the intensity constant, so there are no training peaks and no variable fatigue to manage | Maintain max hang fingerboarding, doing only half the sets, twice a week |
| More frequent deload weeks | The maintenance phase prioritises outdoor climbing and recovery; outdoor climbing may be consistent (e.g. reliably out twice a week), and more frequent deload weeks from training stop fatigue from the maintenance training building up | Winter 3 weeks on, 1 week off → summer 2 weeks on, 1 week off |

## What to maintain
- What to focus on depends on: what you naturally maintain by climbing outside, your relative weaknesses and the gains that were hard-earned for you, your training age, and your general capacity. There is no golden set of sessions or session count for an optimal maintenance phase.
- One option, given the demands of climbing and common training focuses: maintain finger strength, shoulder mobility and max pull strength.
- You cannot maintain everything: prioritise, so training volume comes down, based on your individual situation.
- **Warning:** Don't introduce new movements or protocols in a maintenance phase: learning new exercises brings higher neurological fatigue and possible DOMS.
- Match maintenance training to what you do outside. Outdoor sessions differ (working moves on a project, doing good links, full redpoint mode); plan maintenance training around how much recovery you need to be fresh at the crag.
- Training sessions can boost or crush confidence. Training performance does not dictate how you will feel on your project. If you drop the volume so you feel good in short, high-intensity sessions, the training helps maintain confidence in your basic physical strength.
- **Warning:** High intensity does not mean going to failure on every set or rep. Save 10/10 effort for outside.

## Setting loads for the season

| Method | How to set the load | Example session |
|---|---|---|
| Test and percentage | Test before switching to outdoor climbing: max fingerboard hang over 5, 7 or 10 seconds (by preference or focus) and 2-rep max pull-up. Train at a percentage of these results and keep the loads static all season: the aim is to maintain, not progress, without unnecessary fatigue | Short sessions at about 95%: 4 max hangs, or 3 sets of 3 pull-ups |
| RPE (rate of perceived exertion) | When focusing on sport climbing outside, finger strength will likely decrease as aerobic power increases, so some climbers set session loads by RPE instead of max testing and percentages | Max hangs: a load that gives 9/10 effort (trying very hard but able to hold finger position for the whole hang) |

- Long outdoor season: it can help to do some "top-up" training, reducing the outdoor focus a bit and pushing the loading in training for a short cycle.
