# Minimum Effective Dose: Maintaining Strength over Summer

**Type:** Planning, Principles · **Trains:** Finger strength, Pulling strength, General strength, Power, Recovery · **For:** outdoor climbing season

**TL;DR:** Over summer, train the smallest amount you can indoors to maintain adaptations (typically strength), leaving maximum time and energy for the rest of your life. Maintenance needs far less than building: cut volume to ~30-60%, frequency to ~50-80%, and keep intensity at ~80-95%. Treat outdoor climbing as the primary training stimulus, add one minimal finger strength session per week and one 45-minute compound strength session once or twice a week.

## The summer dilemma
- Three usual options for summer: keep the full training regime and add all the summer activities on top; take the summer off training entirely; or something in between, which raises the question of how much is enough.
- **Warning:** Keeping the full training regime and adding all the summer activities on top leaves you exhausted at the end of the summer, and possibly injured, when you should feel rejuvenated.
- Taking the summer off training entirely means coming back in autumn weaker.

## What minimum effective dose means
- Minimum effective dose (MED), a concept borrowed from pharmacology: the least amount of training stimulus needed to produce or maintain a meaningful adaptation. It is not the maximum recoverable volume, nor the volume that makes you feel you have worked hard; it is just enough.
- Change the question from "How much training can I fit in?" to "What is the smallest amount of training I need to maintain performance while prioritising the rest of my life?"
- Goal of summer training: train the smallest amount you can indoors to maintain adaptations (typically strength), leaving maximum time and energy for the rest of your life.
- Strength and muscle mass can be maintained on a fraction of winter training volume, provided intensity remains high.

## Total load in summer
- Climbers don't plateau because they train too little but because they are chronically under-recovered ("more is better" trap). This peaks in summer in two ways: outdoor weekends on top of indoor training, and allostatic load.
- **Warning:** Layering intense outdoor weekends on top of indoor training: climbing performance is highly sensitive to cumulative fatigue. Your normal gym workload alone may be manageable, but adding outdoor climbing pushes total workload past what you can recover from, so every session feels mediocre because you don't start it fresh and rested.
- Allostatic load: the combined physical and mental strain from life outside the gym (irregular sleep, childcare, travel, camping, socialising more after work). Indoor training is rarely adjusted for it. A higher allostatic load drives up your RPE (rate of perceived exertion), making your project feel harder.
- A summer training plan adjusts total workload to fit around your life instead of carrying on as before.

## Core principle: train less
- Maintaining an adaptation requires significantly less training volume than building it: you might need months of progressive overload to gain a 10% increase in finger strength, but preserving it requires surprisingly little.
- General rule for maintenance across strength research. Key idea: volume drops the most, intensity drops the least.

| Variable | Build phase | Minimum effective dose |
|---|---|---|
| Volume | 100% | ~30-60% |
| Frequency | 100% | ~50-80% |
| Intensity | 100% | ~80-95% |

Maintenance examples by quality:

| Quality | Maintenance dose |
|---|---|
| Fingers | One high-quality, high-intensity finger strength session per week maintains maximum recruitment and strength |
| Power | A short, low-volume board session or a few focused explosive movements on boulders preserve strength and power |
| Strength & conditioning | 2-3 sets at high intensity, 1-2 times per week per muscle group |
| Skill | Outdoor climbing itself maintains real-world movement skills and tactical efficiency |

## Layering summer priorities

| Priority | What | Dose |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Outdoor climbing | Outdoor climbing is not an extra on top of a training plan, it is the primary training stimulus | Whenever conditions are good |
| 2. Preserve baseline finger recruitment | A minimal finger strength protocol, without accumulating fatigue | Once a week. Example: a 20-minute home hangboard session of 5-6 high-quality maximal hangs, with rest before your next outdoor or try-hard session, preserves baseline finger strength through summer |
| 3. Time-efficient strength maintenance | Off-the-wall strength and conditioning, mainly shoulder and pull strength: short, hard sessions with minimum time and maximum output | One targeted 45-minute session once or twice a week |

- If performing outdoors is your main goal, training doesn't get more specific than outdoor climbing; rock climbing develops technique, movement efficiency, route reading and pacing.
- **Warning:** Treating outdoor climbing as supplementary to a full gym workout is how overuse injuries happen. A full day at the crag already provides a significant portion of your weekly training load.

Compound movements for strength maintenance:

| Area | Exercises |
|---|---|
| Pull strength | Pull-ups, low rows |
| Push strength, shoulder stability | Push-ups or dips |
| Posterior chain, lower body stability | Squats or deadlifts |

- Why compound movements: they target several muscle groups at once, so you stimulate a wide range of muscles with fewer exercises, which minimises recovery debt.

## The "two good sessions" rule
- Two high-quality sessions beat five compromised ones: session quality and intent matter more than total hours logged.
- Some of the most effective sessions are those you finish with a little left in the tank: enough stimulus to adapt without accumulating unnecessary fatigue.
