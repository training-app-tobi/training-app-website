# Off-the-Wall Strength Training Through the Year

**Type:** Planning, Principles · **Trains:** General strength, Pulling strength · **For:** Weekend warrior

**TL;DR:** The best plan is tailored to you, follows progressive overload and is done consistently for a long time. Vary off-the-wall strength training through the year along a continuum: a short General Strength block (heavy, stable lifts, 3 days per week), most of the year in Stability Strength (medium loads, less stable exercises, 1–2 days per week), and Specific Strength (low loads, unstable, climbing-specific exercises, 1 day per week) in performance phases. Strength training builds tissue resilience and the capacity to handle more load, so you can practise climbing more.

## Who should do what
| Situation / level | Do this |
|---|---|
| Weekend warrior, not climbing 3, 4 or 5 days a week | May benefit from 2–3 days a week of Stability Strength training (instead of 1–2) |

## The best training plan
- No general "best training protocol" exists: everyone starts from a different place, with different goals and different weaknesses. The best plan is tailored to the individual, follows the principle of progressive overload and is done consistently for a long time.
- Common mistake: applying a new "best protocol" for a couple of weeks, then jumping ship when the short-term gains stop and progress plateaus.
- Off-the-wall training should change through the year like the seasons, to keep challenging the body and be most prepared for sending when the season arrives.
- In season it shouldn't be thrown out entirely: it can give an extra boost when temperatures drop and the focus shifts to hard projects, and it can help prevent injury and keep you healthier while pushing on physically demanding projects.

## Training physiology
- Progressive overload is the cornerstone of any training program: a program that challenges you enough makes you fatigued; over time the body adapts and it no longer feels as hard. The only way to keep improving is to raise the level of training so it is challenging again, through higher intensity, variation or new stimuli.
- In practice: choose intensity (how much weight), volume (sets and reps) and rest time so you are challenged but not maxed out.
- High intensity is the target, but it can't be reached from the start of a new program unless you have an extensive training history: build up to it through progressive overload. High intensity is relative: if you are new to strength training, you can safely train close to failure early on, with proper technique and guidance, and still gain strength.
- Strength is the maximal force a muscle or muscle group can generate, usually measured as a 1-rep maximum (1RM): the most weight lifted for a single rep of an exercise.
- Gains in maximal strength are optimal at loads of 60–100% of 1RM, with low volume and long rest periods.
- Athletes with a more extensive training history need higher intensities to see greater strength gains.

| Training goal | Load (% 1RM) | Reps |
|---|---|---|
| Strength | ≥85 | ≤6 |
| Power, single-effort | 80–90 | 1–2 |
| Power, multiple-effort | 75–85 | 3–5 |
| Hypertrophy | 67–85 | 6–12 |

## Strength training and injury
- "Injury prevention" has nuance: acute injuries are often freak events (landing wrong, falling too far). But research suggests regular strength training is protective: college athletes with a higher back squat 1RM are significantly less likely to have a lower-extremity injury in their sport.
- Regular strength training increases the overall resilience of tissues, which may protect against overuse injuries, and can still give some protection against acute injuries where a tissue's capacity is suddenly exceeded (e.g. a foot slip and popped pulley, a tweaked shoulder).
- A 2014 meta-analysis found strength training gave the biggest reduction in injury rates compared with other exercise interventions such as stretching or proprioceptive training.
- With consistent, progressive strength training, muscles and tendons produce more force and handle more overall training load (a function of volume and intensity), so they are better equipped for the demands of climbing.

## Why climbers strength train
- The greatest benefit: climbers aren't training to be powerlifters, so gym numbers only matter to track progress over time. Regular strength training raises the body's ability to handle more overall load and so to practise climbing more. A well-rounded, capable full body lets you spend more time practising climbing and avoiding injury.
- Upper-body pulling strength and finger strength matter for hard moves, but in a sport with a large technical component, more time on the wall practising moves on varied terrain is what ensures steady progress.
- Strength and technique aren't independent (greater strength can enable technical development); neither is everything and both must be balanced.
- **Warning:** Strength isn't everything: climbers who rely too much on strength handicap their technical development because they don't need to find the best beta to get through a section. Many get a long way pulling through on strength, but at some point meet a section that needs more than brute strength.

## The Strength Continuum
A framework for off-the-wall strength training that varies through the year with the seasonal nature of outdoor climbing and performance.

- The "training pie": training, climbing and recovery take differently sized slices that change (and should change) through the year, but the size of the pie never changes; when one slice gets bigger, another must get smaller. Slice sizes depend on the individual.
- More on periodization (variation, specificity and simplicity) is in the note Periodization Basics: The Training Pie, Variation, Specificity and Simplicity.

| Phase | Loads | Character | Stability |
|---|---|---|---|
| General Strength | High | High CNS demand | Highest |
| Stability Strength | Medium | Sport-specific integration | Medium |
| Specific Strength | Lower | Sport simulations | Lowest |

- Why loads drop along the continuum: highly stable exercises (bench press, squat, deadlift) need little effort to control the movement, so effort goes into moving as much weight as possible. In less stable exercises (single-arm, single-leg) more effort goes into control, so maximal force output and external load must drop. The movement should still be hard.

### When and why

| Phase | When | Goal | Training pie |
|---|---|---|---|
| General Strength | Not much of the year: a short 4–6 week block in winter, and perhaps an equally long one in the heat of summer if conditions are less suited to hard projecting, or after returning from a long climbing trip and getting back into training | Overload stable movements and drive up the muscles' overall force-producing capability | Keep some climbing volume, but most of the pie is gym training, which won't necessarily look like climbing |
| Stability Strength | Much of the year: after a General Strength phase and before short periods of maximal performance | Perfect form throughout. Weight challenging but not barely eking out the reps; the last rep should look very similar to the first. Subjective 8/10 effort rather than a specific load | Pre-season or in season, climbing takes a bigger piece of the pie |
| Specific Strength | Periods of max performance. Duration is inversely proportional to the Stability Strength phase: if most of the year is spent in Stability Strength, this phase is reserved for periods of max performance, and vice versa. How much of the year each takes is highly individual | High-quality movement; maintain strength with minimal gym time | Most of the pie is performing on rock: keep the focus on climbing |

### Dose and exercises

| Phase | Gym days per week | Sets and reps | Exercises per session | Example exercises |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| General Strength | 3 | 10–15 total reps per exercise, about 5 sets of 2–3 reps. HEAVY | 2–3 upper-body and 1–2 lower-body, on top of any mobility or accessory work | Standard bar pull-up or weighted pull-up; a stable squat variation (back, front or goblet squat) |
| Stability Strength | 1–2 | 2–3 sets per exercise, 5–10 reps per set | 2–3 upper-body and 1–2 lower-body in major movement patterns, rotated to cover the whole body over the week | Explosive pull-ups, offset pull-ups, single-arm rows on rings or with a dumbbell, split squats |
| Specific Strength | 1 | Low session volume: 2–3 sets of few reps per exercise | Any weight training: full-body sessions with 2–3 upper-body and 1–2 lower-body | Specific board climbing moving slowly, using big footholds and controlling small or challenging handholds ("wall crawls" or "crimp crawls"); lock-offs on a hangboard or pull-up bar; pistol squats |

### General Strength
- Exercises target major muscle groups and movement patterns: horizontal and vertical pulling and pressing (upper body), hinging and squatting (lower body). Which exercises and how many aren't fixed.
- If these traditional powerlifting-style exercises are unfamiliar: spend time getting familiar with the movement, start with a challenging but conservative load, and continue to near failure to get strength gains.
- If familiar with the movements, use the "last set max reps" rule: on the last set of an exercise for the week, do as many reps as possible with the prescribed weight instead of stopping at the prescribed number.

| Last set result | Next week |
|---|---|
| More reps than prescribed | Increase the load |
| Can't complete all prescribed reps | Right weight: keep it |

### Stability Strength
- Exercises are slightly more unstable than in General Strength, so maximal force output is a bit lower; some sport specificity comes in.
- **Warning:** Pre-season or in season, climbing takes a bigger piece of the pie in this phase: don't leave the gym with excessive soreness, so don't overdo the volume.

### Specific Strength
- These are the most unstable variations and the most climbing-specific exercises.
- **Warning:** Focus on high-quality movement: doing these exercises poorly is risky.
- **Warning:** These exercises stress the same tissues that are stressed heavily in a performance phase, so it's easy to overdo them. More is not the answer: don't be the "training hero" constantly pushing for more; with these exercises it's a recipe for injury.

## When to see a professional
- If pain is limiting you from loading heavily in a strength program, see a licensed physical therapist or a qualified strength and conditioning specialist.
