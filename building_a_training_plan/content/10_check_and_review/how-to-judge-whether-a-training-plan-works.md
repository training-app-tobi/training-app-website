# How to Judge Whether a Training Plan Works

**Type:** Planning, Principles

**TL;DR:** A plan is only effective if it can be followed consistently, because without consistent execution adaptation cannot occur. Judge the system behind the plan, its full logic, not individual sessions or isolated variables. A good plan fits the athlete's real constraints, is usable, matches the goal, progresses load safely, and changes only when stable behaviour patterns justify it.

## What "effective" means
- Effectiveness can mean strength gains, improved movement, fewer injuries, better performance, or simply feeling that you are progressing. More fundamentally, it means whether the athlete can follow the plan consistently enough for adaptation to occur.
- Training happens under real constraints: sleep, work, childcare, weather, motivation, travel, injury history and facility access all decide whether a plan is usable. A plan can be theoretically perfect yet practically ineffective if it does not fit the athlete's context.
- A plan is only effective if it can be followed consistently.

## How a plan should be built and adapted
- A plan is built from the athlete's discipline, ability, experience, availability and goals.
- Change the plan only when stable behaviour patterns appear: repeated missed days, a consistent RPE difference, or accumulated fatigue. Single outliers never trigger a change; adaptations should follow real behaviour, not noise.

## Why whole plans are hard to evaluate
- Climbing has no established method for evaluating whole training plans. Research looks at isolated mechanisms (e.g. rest intervals on a fingerboard, the volume needed for power endurance). These findings are useful but do not show whether a whole plan behaves sensibly under varied conditions. A training plan is a subjective system shaped by judgement, context and behaviour.
- Mental fatigue is not one fixed state: two climbers can start a session with the same mental load and finish with very different fatigue. Fatigue is better seen as a dynamic regulatory process shaped by mental load, time, motivation quality and allostatic regulation. These decide when someone reaches their cessation threshold: the point where continuing no longer feels worthwhile.

## What a training plan must cover
- A climbing training plan is a complex system of interacting parts that must work together over weeks and months, not a list of workouts. Its parts: training categories, multi-session progressions, load and intensity rules, safety boundaries, scheduling constraints, discipline- and grade-specific requirements, behavioural feasibility, and real coaching decision patterns.
- The coaching decisions a plan must reflect: why certain weeks follow a particular structure; how progression evolves in different contexts; when load increases are appropriate; where safety boundaries must be enforced; which training categories belong together and which do not.

### Four dimensions that govern a plan
| Dimension | What it covers | What it decides |
|---|---|---|
| Structural | Time availability, frequency, scheduling constraints, training rhythm | What is realistically possible week to week |
| Physiological | Volume, intensity, progression, recovery | Controls load and adaptation; whether the plan is safe and effective |
| Goal alignment | Discipline, grade band, experience level | What the plan is trying to achieve; matches the training stimulus to the required performance outcome |
| Behavioural | Feasibility, adherence likelihood | Whether the plan can be completed consistently; a plan is only effective if it can, so behavioural constraints are central |

## Faults to check a plan for
- Unsafe progressions
- Unrealistic weekly structures
- Inconsistent load patterns
- Duplicated or missing training categories
- Illogical combinations of sessions
- Scheduling conflicts
- Anything that contradicts real coaching logic

## Three qualities of a good plan
| Quality | What it means | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Usability | How easily the athlete can understand and follow the plan | If a plan is unclear, adherence drops and the training stimulus becomes inconsistent; without consistent execution, adaptation cannot occur |
| Goal alignment | Whether the plan reflects the athlete's discipline, objectives and required performance qualities | Adaptations are specific to the stresses applied; if the plan targets the wrong qualities, progress slows |
| Physiological safety | Whether load and intensity progress sustainably | Excessive stress increases injury risk and disrupts long-term progress; too little stress produces stagnation; sustainable progress requires appropriate load management |

- Together these three qualities decide whether an athlete can complete the plan consistently, safely and with clear purpose. Consistency is one of the strongest predictors of long-term improvement in climbing.

## What shows up when real climbers follow a plan
- In real use, a plan is checked through adherence patterns, session completion, RPE drift, adaptation triggers and points where people disengage.
- Common problems found: stages where people commonly drop off, sessions that are technically correct but feel mismatched, misunderstood load expectations, and plan adaptations that need clearer explanation.

## How to measure a plan's effectiveness
- Judge the system behind the plan, its full logic: not the individual sessions and not isolated variables.
- A plan is effective when it:
  - behaves consistently across contexts
  - adapts only when behaviour justifies it
  - avoids unsafe or unrealistic patterns
  - supports adherence in real life
  - follows a validation framework built for subjective systems
