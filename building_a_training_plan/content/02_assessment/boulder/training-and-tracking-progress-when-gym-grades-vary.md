# Training and Tracking Progress When Gym Grades Vary

**Type:** Planning, Test · **Trains:** Technique, Power endurance · **For:** Traveling climbers

**TL;DR:** Bouldering grades vary between gyms by two, three or even four V-grades, so track progress with a grade-independent system that works in any gym. Log sessions by movement pattern instead of grade, run a 15-minute circuit test, and calibrate new gyms with style-based benchmark problems from your home gym. Use grades only to set warm-up and session intensity.

## The problem
- Bouldering grades vary between climbing gyms by two, three or even four V-grades, so a climber's "level" says more about the facility than about their ability.
- To climb consistently and track real progress, use a grade-independent training system that works in any gym, however soft or stiff the setting.
- Struggling on a new gym's lower grades (e.g. climbing V5 at home but barely V3 at a new gym, with unfamiliar holds and movement style) is not a decline in ability: it reflects how extreme grade inconsistency is.
- When the performance metric (grade) shifts unpredictably across locations, you can't accurately assess progress, identify weaknesses or structure periodization. "Just ignore grades" is unrealistic for most climbers; the answer is a grade-independent training system.

## Why grades vary, and by how much
Regional patterns (from 40 US gyms on the West Coast, Northeast, South and Midwest; a representative snapshot, not a census):

| Region | Grading |
|---|---|
| West Coast | Split between soft, circuit-based grading at many bouldering-focused facilities and notably stiff technical gyms with old-school setting |
| Northeast | Skews harder: some NYC training facilities are extremely stiff; traditional East Coast gyms keep old-school grading |
| South | Trends softer, especially in tourist-heavy markets where member satisfaction drives grading |
| Midwest | Most consistent, clustering around "medium" grades |

Wall style is the strongest predictor of grading:

| Wall style | Grading | Demands |
|---|---|---|
| Steep, overhang-dominated | Harder | More raw pulling power |
| Slab and vertical-heavy | Softer | Technical movement over strength |
| Volume-heavy, competition-style setting | More generous | Flow and coordination over raw difficulty |

- Gym type matters more than location: large commercial chains grade about one V-grade or more softer than training-focused facilities. This reflects different business models and audiences, but it makes a "V6 climber" identity meaningless without context.
- Practical impact: a solid V4 climber could reasonably climb anywhere from V2 to V6 across different gyms, depending on wall angle, hold style and setting philosophy. This disrupts standard training progressions.

## Principle 1: train movement patterns, not V-numbers
- Grades measure perceived difficulty; movement patterns measure actual competence.
- Log sessions by movement, not grades: instead of "I climbed three V5s today", write "Worked compression, tested crimp endurance, struggled with coordination-heavy dynamic moves."
- Categorize every session by its dominant movement demand, using five categories:

| Category | What it involves |
|---|---|
| Compression | Body tension on volumes/features |
| Crimp-dependent | Small edges, fingers |
| Sloper/open-hand | Shoulder-intensive |
| Dynamic/coordination | Momentum-based |
| Technical/balance | Footwork precision |

- Rate yourself 1–10 on each category every month.
- Film yourself at different gyms attempting the same movement patterns at varying grades: this shows which patterns are genuine strengths and which are inflated by your home gym's style.
- Different gyms expose different weaknesses. When grades shift, treat it as diagnostic data about movement limitations rather than feeling "weak":

| Gym style | What it exposes |
|---|---|
| Steep gym that grades stiff | Compression weaknesses |
| Slab gym | Footwork gaps |
| Comp-style gym | Coordination |

### Why it works
- The nervous system encodes climbing skills contextually, so training on only one wall style keeps neural adaptations narrow and gym-specific. Tracking movement competencies instead of grades builds strength that transfers across facilities.

## Principle 2: time-boxed performance testing
- The number of problems you can complete in 15 minutes is far more consistent than gym grades.

| Element | Protocol |
|---|---|
| Frequency | Every two weeks |
| Test | 15-minute circuit |
| Problems | Two grades below your perceived maximum |
| Rest | 90 seconds between attempts |
| Record | Successful sends; recovery quality (are you still breathing hard after 90 seconds?); where you failed (pump, finger strength, bad sequence read) |
| Logging | Across different gyms |
| Example of a strong benchmark | "12 problems in 15 minutes, minimal pump, failed only on coordination problems I couldn't read quickly" |

- Reading the result: completing 12 problems at Gym A but only 8 at Gym B (both gyms where you climb V5) doesn't mean you got weaker; Gym B's V3s are equivalent to Gym A's V4s. Your performance capacity is the same; only the labeling changed.
- The test also captures work capacity and recovery efficiency, trainable qualities that grades ignore. It measures the balance of the anaerobic and aerobic energy systems and shows whether your limitation is power, endurance or technique under fatigue.
- When traveling, run the test in your first week at a new gym. It calibrates how that gym's grading compares to your baseline, without failing "at your grade".

## Principle 3: benchmark across styles, not locations
- Comparing your V-grade across gyms is futile; comparing your performance on similar styles is valuable.
- Create benchmark problems: pick one problem at your home gym for each major style. These become e.g. your "home V4 steep overhang" or "home V5 crimp endurance".

| Style | Benchmark problem |
|---|---|
| Steep overhang | One problem at your home gym |
| Vertical crimpy | One problem at your home gym |
| Slab technical | One problem at your home gym |
| Dynamic coordination | One problem at your home gym |
| Compression | One problem at your home gym |

- At a new gym, don't hunt for "V5s"; look for problems that match your benchmark styles, attempt them and ask: did this feel easier, harder or equivalent to my home benchmark for that style?
- After 3–4 sessions at the new gym you'll know how it translates, e.g. "this gym's V5 steep problems feel like my home V4s" or "their crimp problems are much stiffer: their V3 = my home V4."
- Training application: in a strength phase emphasizing steep, powerful movement, you need to know which gyms give the right stimulus. A gym that grades soft on steep terrain might require climbing its V6s to match the training load of your home V4s. Without style-based benchmarking you would undertrain because the numbers looked right.

## Training dashboard

| Frequency | Metric | Target / how |
|---|---|---|
| Weekly | Flash rate | Percentage of problems sent first attempt; aim for 40–60% on warm-up grades |
| Weekly | Project conversion | Number of sessions needed to send your hardest problems; 1–3 sessions = appropriate difficulty |
| Weekly | Movement distribution | Hours spent on each pattern type: compression, crimps, slopers, dynamic, technical |
| Weekly | Subjective quality score | Rate technique, energy and confidence 1–10 each session |
| Monthly | 15-minute circuit test | Test results |
| Monthly | Movement-pattern self-ratings | 1–10 on each of the five categories |
| Monthly | Max strength metrics | Max hang time, campus rung progression, weighted pull-up max |

### Using gym grades correctly

| Session part | Grade |
|---|---|
| Warm-up | Always start 3–4 grades below your max, regardless of gym |
| Hard days | At-grade projects |
| Easy days | Two grades below max, for volume |

- Tracking: a simple spreadsheet works (apps can automate some of it). The key is consistency: log every session for at least 12 weeks to establish baseline patterns.
