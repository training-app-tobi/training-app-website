# No-Hang Finger Strength Testing

**Type:** Test, Injury & health · **Trains:** Finger strength · **For:** injured or returning

**TL;DR:** Finger strength can be tested without hanging from a hangboard, by measuring the pull of the finger flexors with the arm fixated (cannot move) or non-fixated (free to move). Use both: arm fixation isolates the finger flexors and is more reliable, while the non-fixated position relates better to climbing ability. Tension-based dynamometers (crane scale, Tindeq Progressor, K-Force Link) or a compression dynamometer can be used; be consistent with whichever method you choose. A finger-grip strength deficit of more than 41% (41.4%) in the one-finger crimp on the affected side compared with the other side may indicate a pulley rupture.

## Fixated vs non-fixated testing
| Form | What it is | How |
|---|---|---|
| Fixated strength testing | Dynamometry of the finger flexors with the upper limb "fixated", so it cannot move | Strap the arm down, place it in a specialty device, or simply rest the elbow on a table so it cannot move |
| Non-fixated strength testing | Dynamometry of the finger flexors, but the climber can adjust their arm while pulling | The arm is free to move |

- Both forms test strength without hanging from a hangboard.

### Research
Study of 22 male climbers, on a custom instrument with a climbing hold connected to an instrumented force plate; two maximal, all-out tests; reliability also tested.

| Position | Setup | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Arm fixated | Shoulder and elbow flexed at 90° | More reliable results |
| Arm not fixated | Shoulder flexed at 180°, elbow fully extended | Recommended when testing and relating results to climbing ability |

## Which to use in rehab
- Use both. Arm fixation isolates the finger flexors without contributions from the rest of the body, which improves reliability; but climbers don't climb with the arm fixated when they move on the wall, so also test with the arm non-fixated.
- Combining both gives a holistic picture of the performance of a climber's fingers.

## Tension-based dynamometry: setup
| Component | Options |
|---|---|
| Anchor | A lashing strap hooked to a ceiling anchor, or a chain, to make sure there is no attenuation of shock or force |
| Measuring device | A crane scale or dynamometer: anything that measures the amount of pull |
| Grip device | A tension block, grip pull or similar implement that lets you measure finger strength; many options |

The test can be done with a tension-based dynamometer or a crane scale, with one arm or with both arms at once; each has benefits and drawbacks.

| Option | Benefit | How |
|---|---|---|
| Two arms, two dynamometers | Much quicker; assessment goes with more ease and efficiency | Test both sides at once (see warnings below) |
| Single arm | Much easier to control variables | After the first reading, place the dynamometer on the opposite side and repeat exactly the same test and measure |

- **Warning:** With two arms, keep the climber's face directed forward during the test. Gripping two dynamometers at once, they may turn their gaze toward one side and neurologically bias that side.
- **Warning:** With two arms, the dynamometers and grips must be at exactly the same height (not only the portable hangboards, also the dynamometers and slings) to quantify strain side by side.
- Tension-based dynamometry can also be used with the arm fixated to test finger flexor strength, again with two arms at once or a single arm.
- You can choose which grip to test, e.g. a half crimp on a portable hangboard.
- Single-finger testing: arm fixated, portable hangboard and tension-based dynamometer. Repeat with each finger and note the readings, so you can compare each finger with the others, including the fingers of the opposite hand.
- **Warning:** Force is attenuated through the nylon strap above and through the quick link on the portable hangboard. To decrease this attenuation, use chain links or something similar with more metal that is generally more stable.
- A lashing strap on top makes it much easier to change tension, height and position when using the same system on one side and then the other; a quick link is easier and lighter to use.
- There are many ways to obtain the data and values; be consistent with each one.

## Tension-based dynamometers compared
| Device | Maximal load | Cost | Sampling frequency |
|---|---|---|---|
| Crane scale | About 272 kg | Around $50 (US) | Low |
| Tindeq Progressor | 150 kg | About $135 (US) | About 80 Hz |
| K-Force Link (higher end) | 300 kg | About $1,000 (US), give or take | 75–100 Hz |

- Study of 8 recreationally active males: 5 isometric mid-thigh pulls measured on a force plate and compared with a crane scale. Intra-class correlation coefficients and coefficients of variation showed strong reliability for both; the crane scale showed good construct validity against the force plate. So a crane scale is a valid, reliable and affordable alternative for measuring peak force when a force plate or dynamometer is not available.

## Compression-based dynamometry
| Step | What to do |
|---|---|
| Setup | A compression dynamometer (e.g. K-Force) placed below a portable hangboard; arm fixated |
| 1 | Stabilize the elbow on a table or surface |
| 2 | Place the fingers into the portable hangboard |
| 3 | Grip fully with maximal force for 5 seconds |

## Using the data for diagnosis
Study of 39 rock climbers with A2 or A4 pulley injuries of the third or fourth fingers (pain on palpation, ultrasound showing increased tendon–bone distance, decreased finger-grip strength).

| Item | Finding |
|---|---|
| Grips tested | One-finger crimp, open crimp, closed crimp |
| Threshold | A finger-grip strength deficit of more than 41% (41.4%) in the one-finger crimp on the affected side compared with the other side |
| Equivalent | A high-resolution ultrasound tendon–bone distance of more than 2 mm |
| Meaning | May indicate a pulley rupture. A decrease in finger-grip strength could be considered diagnostic for A2 and A4 pulley injuries of the third and fourth fingers, and can help determine clinically whether a climber has a pulley sprain |

- **Warning:** Correlate the strength deficit with other measures: clinical criteria, active range of motion, palpation, muscle tests and subjective reports, combined into one picture of the climber.
