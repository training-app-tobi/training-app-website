# Assessing Your Starting Point Before Writing a Training Plan

**Type:** Test, Planning · **Trains:** Finger strength, Endurance, Pulling strength, Mobility · **For:** self-coached climbers

**TL;DR:** Before building the roadmap of a training plan, find where you are now with an assessment: a series of tests that gives a snapshot of your current strengths and weaknesses. For a first assessment, start with just 4 or 5 tests, chosen from your weaknesses and the demands of 2–3 performance goals; a weakness that blocks your goal takes the highest priority. Four tests useful for most climbers: a 7-second max hang on a 20 mm edge, 7:3 repeaters to failure at 60% of the max hang load, a 2-rep-max pull-up, and a side-split distance.

## The three parts of plan writing

| Part | What it is |
|---|---|
| 1 | Building a roadmap and a timeline of your training |
| 2 | Building progressive overload into that plan |
| 3 | Making time for recovery, which is where adaptation happens |

- Each of the three parts is crucial to effective training.
- Building the roadmap means planning the journey to where you want to go with your training, but first you need to know where you are now (point A). Only then can you start moving in the right direction. Finding point A is the job of the assessment.

## What an assessment is
- An assessment is a series of tests that shows how well (or how poorly) you perform in them. It gives a clear snapshot in time of your current strengths and weaknesses, and it is used to inform your training, i.e. to build the roadmap.

## How many tests

| Situation | Number of tests |
|---|---|
| Advanced and elite athletes | May get a large number of tests, to see the broad picture and also the nuances and specific details of their training |
| Doing an assessment for the first time | Start simply with just 4 or 5 tests and go from there: this helps you narrow your training focus, which is an important part of planning |

- Fewer tests doesn't mean a lot of training goes unmeasured: you can still measure training metrics along the way, and these can inform pretty much anything you want to train.

## Choosing the tests
- Choose tests from what you want to achieve in your climbing (a goal, a grade or a specific style): analyse it and write down the list of demands that style or goal needs. Example: an endurance repeater test on a hangboard is not very specific if the goal is a really hard four-move boulder problem; a maximal finger-strength test is much more useful for that goal.

### Two-step process for a first assessment

| Step | What to do | Example or detail |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Ask three or more friends to write down your three biggest weaknesses and your three biggest strengths. Take the two of each that come up most often and devise a way to test them, keeping the test specific to the weakness | Particularly weak on wide shoulder moves → test pull-up strength, specifically in a wide grip |
| 2 | Write down two, or at most three, performance goals and list the demands of each | Keep it fairly simple (maximum strength, endurance, flexibility) or get more detailed (wrist strength for slopers, core tension): make it as detailed as you can, since this also helps you plan the tests |

- You might also write down some process goals, because they make training really simple.

### Setting priorities

| Result of the two steps | Priority in training |
|---|---|
| A weakness that would prevent you from meeting the demands of your goal | Highest priority |
| A strength that the goal doesn't need much | Lower priority |

- Don't only test and train your weaknesses: also exploit your strengths as much as possible. Even at the elite level, climbers show an ability to make the most of their strengths. "Train your weaknesses and perform in your strengths."

## Judging your scores
- One way to judge whether a score is good or bad: do the assessment battery with a group of friends climbing at a similar level, to see where you sit in terms of numbers.
- **Warning:** Don't get too caught up ranking strength or endurance measures within a group of friends: everyone is different, and anthropometrics and training history in particular make a big difference to the scores.
- The other way: get a professional to look at the scores and give their opinion.
- It helps, but you don't necessarily need to know whether a score is good or bad: you need to know where you are and how much you want to improve that score to achieve your goals. Be realistic about the time frame in which you want to change the scores, and respect your current training history and how fast you can make changes.

## Four tests useful for most climbers

| Test | Protocol | Score |
|---|---|---|
| Finger strength (max hang) | Fingerboard, 20 mm edge (roughly one pad deep), 7-second max hang protocol. Hang for the full 7 s time under tension, progressively increasing the load over roughly 8 sets until you reach the absolute maximum you can hold for the full 7 s. Rest 2–3 min between hangs, even though the hang is short, so you are fully recovered and can try your hardest | Maximum load held for the full 7 s |
| Forearm endurance (repeaters) | 7 s hang, 3 s rest, repeated continuously until failure, at about 60% of your maximum 7-second hang | Time to failure |
| Pulling strength | 2-rep max: the maximum weight you can pull for two full pull-up repetitions | Maximum weight for 2 reps |
| Lower-body flexibility | Side split (box split) | Distance you can get between your feet |

### Finger strength
- As you approach your maximum and the hangs feel close to failure, keep the weight increments small.
- Not used a hangboard before, or concerned about using one: a good place to start is testing finger strength on a pick-up edge, one hand at a time, keeping the weight really light and building up very gradually.
- Edge size doesn't matter much (a slightly larger or slightly smaller edge is fine); what matters is keeping it consistent between tests. Record exactly how you performed the test (hang time, rest between hangs, any details that might differ) so a later retest can be compared with your notes.

### Forearm endurance
- Endurance tests can be done at many intensities; a middle intensity of about 60% of your maximum 7-second hang gives an endurance score akin to climbing a route of somewhere between 10 and 20 m.
- If you can't comfortably hang bodyweight on the edge you plan to test on, this test isn't appropriate: use a pick-up device or a larger edge instead.
- Calculating 60%: take the total load held in the 7-second max hang (bodyweight plus any added weight) and multiply by 0.6.

| Step | Example |
|---|---|
| Bodyweight | 70 kg |
| Added weight in the 7-second max hang | +30 kg |
| Total load | 100 kg |
| 60% (total load × 0.6) | 60 kg |
| Repeater setup | 60 kg is less than bodyweight, so use a pulley setup to take off 10 kg (assistance) for the 7:3 repeaters |

### Pulling strength
- As with the fingerboard test, keep it consistent, especially the form: no kipping, chin fully above the bar.

### Lower-body flexibility
- Ways to measure: a measuring tape (handy if a friend can measure for you); put an object either side of your feet, push them apart, then measure the distance between them; or measure the angle with free apps on Android and iOS.
