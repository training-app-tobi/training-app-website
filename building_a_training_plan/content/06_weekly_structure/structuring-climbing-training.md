# Structuring Climbing Training: Session Length, Split Days and Weekly Workload

**Type:** Planning, Principles · **Trains:** Finger strength, Power, Power endurance, Endurance, General strength

**TL;DR:** The top priority is good physical and mental quality in every session. Two shorter sessions a day with different stimuli keep you fresh in a base phase; drop to one a day towards a peak. Long sessions build the ability to perform while fatigued, so mix short, sharp weekday sessions with one long weekend session. Plan the week around people, place, time and the forecast, put the hardest sessions at the start of the week, keep high-intensity elements to about five a week at most, and only train high intensity when recovered: an outdoor day or a competition counts as one of them.

## Quality first
- The top priority in training is being able to put really good quality into each session, both physical and mental quality. Many people are fatigued by heavy jobs and stressful lifestyles: they may have the physical ability but can't bring the mental capacity to a long session.

## Several short sessions a day
- Splitting training across the day into shorter sessions (e.g. 45 minutes in the morning and 45 minutes in the evening, instead of one 3–5 hour session where you get very fatigued but still do intense work at the end) lets you feel fresher, put high intensity in, and train in short, sharp hits with good, intentional efforts instead of exhausting yourself over three hours.

Ideal split day, when the middle of the day is relaxed:

| Part of the day | What to do |
|---|---|
| Morning | A session whenever you're ready (8:00 or 11:00) |
| Middle | A long break |
| Evening | Another quality session |

- **Warning:** three sessions a day is sometimes too much: there isn't a quality recovery period between the sessions.
- The two sessions of a day should generally be a different stimulus, e.g. strength in the morning and aerobic capacity in the evening. Strength twice is possible, but because both use climbing-specific movements and the same muscle groups, it tends not to work as well for the adaptations.
- Exception: a hard board session in the morning (very hard pulling, physical power and strength) and competition-style bouldering in the evening (coordination and technique). Both are high-intensity bouldering, but the two sessions have different aims.
- With a job, stress or a busy family life, the second session of the day is always more compromised by mental fatigue: reduce its intensity by quite a bit if you still split your sessions. Doing a split day around work and family is possible, but few people can manage it; think about the mental fatigue going into the second session.

| Phase | Sessions a day | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Base phase | Two | Building the adaptations |
| Going towards a peak | One | You want to be fresher: the peak phase is for fine-tuning what was built in the base phase and performing when conditions are good |

## One session a day: short or long
- With one session a day, the choice depends on what you are training and what you feel comfortable with. One option: train more often in the week (e.g. five days a week) with shorter, sharper sessions, because mental fatigue sets in 1.5–2 hours into a session and climbing quality drops. Many climbers prefer this.
- Long sessions are time-efficient: you can put all your training into one block (e.g. three hours in the day).
- The main advantage of long sessions, especially for route climbers: they build the ability to train and perform while fatigued, because you train in a fatigued state more often and the body adapts. Unlike a finely tuned "racehorse" that can only pull out maximum efforts when feeling amazing, very good route climbers can produce very good efforts when quite fatigued. Part of this is mental, but a lot is physical: getting the muscles to fire with a level of pre-fatigue.
- Long-session fatigue tolerance is valuable for route climbers, big-wall and multi-pitch climbers, people on long trips who want to climb everything, and competition climbers (competitions last one or two days and you climb a lot of blocks and moves by the end).
- Session fatigue tolerance can be built up over time. Climbers who can keep working routes or problems for a much longer period tend to do longer sessions.
- You can mix both, like runners (a couple of short runs in the evenings during the week, the long run at the weekend):

| When | Session |
|---|---|
| During the week | Short, sharp, high-quality sessions |
| Weekend | One really long session (indoors, outdoors, on a project or just training) to build work capacity and get used to the kind of session you will do when you go outside to perform |

## Managing workload across the week
- First decide where you will train and who with, and build the priority sessions around that: e.g. a regular training partner on a Wednesday with whom certain sessions work well, or a different climbing wall that makes you more enthusiastic, with more problems or harder things to try. Don't do the hard, fun sessions early in the week alone in a less ideal facility, and then a session you end up doing on your own anyway, bored, on the day you are with psyched people in a great place.
- At the start of each week, look at the time you have across the week and the 5–7 day weather forecast, and match your climbing and training to the time available and the conditions. E.g. don't plan all your time and energy for Saturday without checking the forecast, only to find the good conditions are on Sunday.
- There is no secret formula (one on, one off; two on, one off); everyone works slightly differently. Plan sessions well and put good quality into them.
- Once people, place, time and conditions are sorted, put the hardest sessions, the ones for the key adaptations you are after, at the start of the week or on the first day of a block (e.g. the first day of two on, one off), when you are most mentally and physically able: high-quality board or bouldering sessions, or aerobic power sessions. A pump session needs psych and freshness: not a second day on slightly sore skin with fingers a little creaky from the board the day before. It depends on what works for you.
- Skin: many people suffer with skin on consecutive days. On the second day, climb on holds and movements on which you can still try really hard.
- The order of the parts inside one session (finger-intensive work first) is in the note Order Within a Session: Finger-Intensive Work First, Conditioning Last.

## How often to do high-intensity work
- High-intensity elements are fingerboarding, campusing and board climbing: not whole sessions, but e.g. a 15-minute fingerboard session, a 30–45 minute board session or a set campus routine. Do these up to around five times a week at most: e.g. a couple of fingerboard sessions and one or two board sessions a week tends to be enough. Five is the maximum, seen in high-level athletes.
- Training five days a week doesn't mean doing high-intensity training in every session.
- A single framework for how many intense sessions a week is impossible because training history and grades vary. The rule: to do high intensity, you need to be recovered. Best feedback: when you arrive for your planned high-intensity session, do you feel recovered and able to go hard? If you feel cranky, tweaky, sore or low on energy, don't do it.
- **Warning:** don't copy someone else's plan (e.g. three or four hard sessions a week seen in a video) just because they did their first V8 with it.
- An outdoor session or a competition counts as one of the high-intensity sessions: you can't fit all your training in and still arrive fresh. Replace a training session with it, e.g. outdoor bouldering on a project counts as a board session, so remove a board session from that week. Learn what you can cope with and what an outdoor session feels like.
