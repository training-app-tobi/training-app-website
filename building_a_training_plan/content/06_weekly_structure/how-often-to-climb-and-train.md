# How Often to Climb and Train: Frequency, Repetition and Outdoor Days

**Type:** Planning, Principles · **Trains:** Finger strength, Power, Power endurance, Technique, Recovery · **For:** new to training, experienced, Older, outdoor climbing, route setters

**TL;DR:** Ideal frequency is not a number but a moving target set by age, training age, recovery, goals and phase: find your minimum effective frequency, then layer on, and let frequency reflect your capacity to recover. Cycle frequency by phase (about 5 lower-intensity days in base building, 3–4 targeted sessions in strength or power, 2–3 in a peak) and run a freshness test before each session. Repeat skill work often, max hangs 1–2 times a week at most, and space projecting days by 48–72 hours. Plan outdoor days into the week as high-load sessions, keep 1–2 short non-negotiable core sessions, and count stimulus, not sessions.

## Who should do what
| Situation / level | Do this |
|---|---|
| Young and new to training | May be able to train 5 or 6 days a week and still bounce back |
| New to climbing-specific training | Everything is a stimulus: a light hangboard session twice a week makes you stronger, and climbing at moderate intensity 4 times a week makes your technique improve fast |
| 3 to 5 years into structured programming | Give the body more stimulus to create adaptation and more recovery to absorb it: it gets pickier |
| Older, with a decade of hard climbing | With a job and young children too: don't copy a 5–6 days a week schedule: it ends in physical therapy (e.g. shoulder impingement) |
| Route setting | Count setting (e.g. 3 setting days a week) as load, not training, and recover after a full setting day |

## Ideal frequency is a moving target
- Ideal frequency is not a number: it is a moving target that shifts with your age, recovery capacity, training history, goals and phase of development. Train more and you risk injury and burnout; train less and you don't progress.
- Biology decides how often you can and should train. Chronological age matters, but training age (how long you have been seriously training) matters just as much.
- Recovery is not just rest days: it is sleep quality, stress levels, hormones and emotional load. Sleeping 5 hours a night, your recovery bank is already low before you touch a campus rung. Frequency should always reflect your capacity to recover, not your desire to train.
- Example: in the mid-20s, sleeping 11 hours a night with climbing, coaching and route setting as the whole day, training 6 days a week was no problem. Later, training about 4 to 5 days a week at most, with more days dedicated to specific outcomes and fewer "junk" sessions done just to feel like you did something, gave better gains with less total frequency, because the training was better targeted and recovery was respected.
- Your optimal frequency is shaped by your current life load, injury history, hormonal profile and how well you actually recover, not how well you think you do. Many climbers who say "I recover well" turn out, when looked at more closely, to barely recover back to baseline, and rarely move forward or adapt in the short term.
- Instead of copying a pro climber who lives in a van with all day to train and nap, find your own minimum effective frequency: the least amount of quality climbing or training with which you still progress. Then layer on from there.
- Check your metrics: are you sleeping better or worse (a sleep tracker and a strict sleep journal give data on whether you are meeting your requirements)? How do your fingers feel 24 and 48 hours after a session? Are you motivated or mentally fried? These feedback loops matter more than what your spreadsheet says.

## Cycling frequency by training phase
Cycle frequency just as you cycle intensity:

| Phase | Frequency |
|---|---|
| Base building block | Climb about 5 days a week, at lower intensity and with higher variety |
| Strength or power phase | 3 or 4 highly targeted sessions a week, with lots of recovery in between |
| Peak performance window | Frequency may dip further, to 2 to 3 sessions a week of super-specific efforts, with the rest of the week dedicated to recovery, mental rehearsal and movement prep |

- The key is not just cutting volume when you are tired, but strategically undulating frequency and load to match the type of adaptation you are aiming for.
- "Sessions" are not only climbing sessions: the count includes strength-training sessions (e.g. the 3–4 sessions of a power phase can blend climbing and strength, or include separate strength sessions).

## Minimum viable frequency
- The minimum viable frequency is a moving target: it depends on the goal and on what you have been doing. Your body is used to the chronic workload you have been training at; with infrequent sessions you can still improve certain qualities, but overall conditioning (capacity) drops. To build or keep capacity you want to train multiple days per week. It also depends on your life, stress and recovery ability.

| Phase | Minimum frequency |
|---|---|
| Capacity phase | 3 days a week is about the minimum; fewer won't expose the body to enough volume to build capacity |
| Max strength phase | 2 days a week can be fine; you can build max strength with 2 days a week |

## The freshness test
- Before each session ask: Am I mentally focused? Are my fingers a little tweaky or sluggish? Am I excited to try hard, or dreading my hardest attempts? Dreading is usually a sign you shouldn't do it. This check tells you more than a calendar.
- If you are not fresh, shift: swap in skill work, or move the intensity to tomorrow.
- **Warning:** Forcing high-intensity days when body or mind isn't ready is the biggest mistake: every time you force too high a frequency of these sessions, you get burned and set yourself back. On those days do the best light skill session you can (if you always push your limits, you probably haven't done one in a while); if you truly feel bad, focus on high-quality rest so you can do well in the next session.
- Physical tiredness is felt in the body: a little sore, slower, achy, weak when you pull, even when your brain feels good. Mental tiredness shows in mood and in your energy levels for the day (e.g. when thinking through the structure of the day or at work).
- Use the warm-up to check physical readiness: arm swings, about 3 minutes in, show whether there is physical fatigue. A readiness score from a sleep/recovery tracker is another indicator.

## Planning the week
- Three key factors affect weekly frequency: what to repeat, how to balance types of days, and how outdoor climbing fits in. Consistency is key, but plans must be flexible because life, recovery and adaptation are not linear.

## What to repeat, and how often
- Repetition is one of the most powerful tools, if used wisely: repeat too often and you plateau; not enough and you never build deep fluency. You don't need new climbs every session: getting better at the climbs you already have (a footwork drill, a coordination boulder, a redpoint project), repeated with intention, refines movement, deepens confidence and builds mastery.
- Skill work (drop knees, dead points, heel placements, precision footwork, tension, and more): repeating it throughout the week can be very effective. It is usually low intensity and can be slotted into warm-ups, cool-downs, or off days when the body needs recovery but the brain still wants reps. Example: about 10 to 15 minutes at the start of a session repeating a specific movement on a familiar climb, not to try hard but to move better; after a few weeks you don't just do the move, you own it.
- Do the skill drill or boulder repeat in place of the last three boulders of your warm-up, so it adds no excess volume. You are fresh and the brain is warming up. A small dose every session, repeated over a 9-week block, is a high frequency of high-value, skill-based repetition that makes you much more capable in those areas, and it is easy to fit in.
- **Warning:** Projects need more care. Trying a single outdoor project 3 or 4 days a week led, by the end of week two, to being physically and emotionally fried: attempt quality dropped, execution frayed, and walking up to the climb was dreaded. Repeating a project too often without recovery or movement variety doesn't give more progress; it gives burnout (physical, mental or emotional).
- Space projecting days with 48 to 72 hours of rest or lower-intensity movement in between, so you arrive fresh and ready to execute. Indoor gym projecting can feel easier to repeat, but it taxes the nervous system, skin and tendons just the same.
- Drills (fingerboard protocols, recruitment pulls, isometric work) fall in between; their frequency depends on intensity:

| Drill | How often |
|---|---|
| Heavy max-hang session at 90% or more effort | 1 to 2 times per week maximum |
| Low-intensity skill work or scapular activation drills | 3 to 4 times a week, assuming you are fresh and rotating stress across joints and muscle groups |

In short:

| What | How often to repeat |
|---|---|
| What builds movement fluency | Often |
| Strength-intensive work | Cautiously |
| Projects | Only when you are physically and mentally prepared to try with intention |

## Climbing days vs training days
- Climbing is not always training, but training can be climbing. Climbers who say they train 5 days a week are often just climbing 5 days a week; that is fine if those sessions drive the adaptations you want, but not all climbing creates adaptation: a fun circuit session with friends doesn't necessarily improve finger strength, and 3 hours of plastic pulling doesn't automatically develop power. The difference between a training session and a climbing session is not the tool (hangboard, campus board) but the intent, structure and stimulus.

| Aspect | Training day | Climbing day |
|---|---|---|
| About | Structure and purpose, not style: you target a specific attribute (max strength, power, movement quality, endurance, and others) | Expression, exploration or flow |
| Tools and forms | Whatever tool gets you there: hangboard, weighted pull-ups, limit bouldering, or low-rep micro-projecting on hard, skin-limited 2–4 move climbs | Projecting with less structure, or mileage sessions that build endurance or practise movement under fatigue |
| What makes it so | (1) Intensity high enough to stimulate adaptation, not just movement; (2) rest intervals planned, not incidental; (3) a narrow focus, not scattered (possibly the most important); (4) a measurable intention | Can be training-oriented but often serves a different purpose: applying the attributes you have developed, testing your skill in a more natural environment, or reinforcing joy and movement patterns |
| Example or role | A short session of 3 boulders, all near your limit, with full rest and deliberate analysis between attempts, is a training day even though you are just climbing | Not less important; it plays a different role |

- Climbing can be your training if you treat it like training. The alternative, as many top pros do: travel and get novel experiences, constantly pushing your limits or doing new things at submaximal intensity. Many have improved from climbing outside alone, but they get on new climbs every time (e.g. a climber who circuited constantly in a huge bouldering area got new stimulus every time because there were so many different boulders, pushing limits in multiple ways). Repeating the same boulders at your local crag is essentially the same as indoor climbing.
- Choose the stimulus by goal; the tool doesn't matter, the stimulus does:

| Goal | Stimulus |
|---|---|
| Finger strength | 45 minutes climbing on small edges in a half-crimp position, with maximal effort and long rests |
| Power | 3-move limit boulders that hit the central nervous system hard but don't pump you out |
| Technique | Climb easy, with hyper-focused footwork drills and precise hip timing |

- When planning the week, don't think "training days vs climbing days": ask what adaptation you want, how to stimulate it, and which medium (climbing or a training tool) best creates that stimulus. Some weeks that means a few focused gym sessions and some playful outdoor climbing; other weeks 2-hour power sessions on a board and recovery walks the rest of the time. In peak season the training gets lighter and the climbing sharper; in base building it is the reverse.
- Frequency is not how many days you climb or train but how many days actually matter: 5 unstructured sessions a week may make you feel busy, but 2 focused, high-intensity climbing days with clear intent can move the needle far more. Every day should serve a purpose within your larger plan: count stimulus, not sessions.

## Outdoor climbing
- Outdoor days are often higher impact than indoor days, even when the volume seems low: hiking, skin wear, conditions (e.g. cold), long rests and emotional fatigue.

| Outdoor days per week | Verdict |
|---|---|
| 1 or 2 | Usually the sweet spot: you can go hard and give quality attempts on those days and still train effectively midweek |
| 3 | Pushing it, unless it is a short trip |
| 4 | A full-blown peak phase, or a trip trying to redpoint, with full recovery planned before and after the trip |

### Fitting outdoor days into the plan
**Step 1: flip the framework.** Outdoor days are the plan, a pillar, not a disruption. If your goal is to get outside, build it into the plan from the start; outdoor days can be given a structure that makes them a contributor to the plan. For the next 4 weeks, pick your outdoor windows in advance (every weekend, just Saturdays, midweek if the weather lines up), block them out, and reverse-engineer your training week around them. Example week:

| Day | Session |
|---|---|
| Saturday | Outdoor projecting, high intensity |
| Sunday | Light movement, recovery |
| Tuesday | Fingerboard or power session, if fully recovered |
| Thursday | Technique-focused session |

**Step 2: give each outdoor day a micro-goal** aligned with your overall training goal:

| Training phase | Outdoor day focus |
|---|---|
| Strength phase | Limit-boulder attempts, harder single moves, longer rests |
| Building power endurance | Longer boulders or circuits, simulated redpoint burns |
| Tapering | Movement quality, lower volume, longer rests |

- This turns outdoor days from unstructured free-for-alls into planned application days: a test of your training, not an escape from it.

**Step 3: adjust load, not just frequency.**
- Outdoor sessions are less controlled but still cost your recovery bank, so treat outdoor days as high-load sessions and adjust the rest of the week.
- If Saturday wrecks your fingers and central nervous system, Sunday and Monday should be recovery or very low load; if the outdoor climbing was moderate or playful, you may keep Tuesday's hangboard session.
- Track how you feel with a simple green, yellow, orange, red system and be ruthless about adjusting.

**Step 4: choose 1 to 2 non-negotiable core training sessions each week.**
- These are anchors that serve long-term adaptation goals (finger strength, max power, etc.) and stay consistent even when the rest of the week flexes. Hit those and the week has succeeded.
- Make them short and potent: 30 to 45 minutes is enough (e.g. weighted hangs or isometrics, limit boulders).

**Step 5: accept imperfect weeks.**
- Consistency over time beats perfection in the moment. One week might be 3 sessions and a crag day, another just a send day and 1 training session; that is fine as long as the trend moves forward. Show up with intention, adjust instead of quitting, keep the long game in sight.

## Travel
- Travelling to climb feels like it should count as a rest day, but it often doesn't: sitting on a plane and sleeping in unfamiliar beds tax the nervous system even without climbing. On a climbing trip, build in a day of decompression before trying your hardest.
- **Warning:** Don't come back from a (weekend) trip and jump straight into a heavy training week: skin is trashed, tendons cooked, the nervous system still recovering, and often jet lag. Give the circadian rhythm time to get back on track and ease back in. Treat travel like a competition or a huge day out: preload with rest and reload with rest. Frequency that ignores the travel toll is a fast track to burnout.

## Route setting
- Route setting is extremely taxing and counts as load, not training: it is far from specific, no more useful for climbing strength than yard work (maybe slightly more specific because you feel holds), and a lot of stress on joints and fingers.
- How much it costs depends on what you set (ropes, from a boom, wall height, fixed lines and hauling a bucket), how many routes or boulders, and for how long.
- Set as efficiently as you can, so as much energy as possible goes to high-specificity stimulus with intention.
- Setting can improve route reading to an extent, a lot if you do much of it and are highly absorbed; the same can also come from watching lots of climbing videos and climbing.
- Training around setting can work: add a little finger work before setting sessions, and sometimes use forerunning as training if you can bleed it into climbing afterwards. After a full setting day you need to recover.
- Setting limits the best results, as any physical-labour job does, though great results are still possible and the activity is good for health.

## Two sessions in one day
- On days with an awkward schedule, two 1-hour sessions in a day are better than one, if the second hour can be high quality.
- Warm up again for the second session (don't go in cold); ideally leave a 4–6 hour break between them (not required).
- Do the higher-intensity training first (as long as you are awake and feel good) and the lower-intensity training second; some people prefer the opposite, everyone is different.
