# Repeating Climbs to Build Technique

**Type:** Principles · **Trains:** Technique

**TL;DR:** Do a climb once to scrape by, one more time to learn it, one more to master it. After a send, decide whether to repeat or move on in three steps: match the climb to your goal (or your weakest areas), check that it is not so physically intense that you only get one or two laps, and check that it gives a new, unique or highly applicable stimulus. Repeat a new set of movements 5–10 times every 7–10 days, include repetition in every session, and reduce how often you repeat a climb as time goes on.

## Who should do what
| Situation / level | Do this |
|---|---|
| Beginning climbing | Prioritize exposure: climb all forms of climbing to build your movement vocabulary by creating new movement patterns, mastering your body, gaining new types of strength and developing confidence across all styles |
| Switching climbs after a few attempts, or often on climbs that are too hard | Add more repetition to your sessions |

## Repeat or explore
- Repeating climbs improves technique, but gaining experience on new types of moves and techniques is also important.
- The usual cycle: project a climb, doing its moves over and over and improving each time until you stick them and send; then move on and repeat the process on a new climb.
- How learning a climb works: at the start the mind is challenged most, solving the puzzle and identifying the movements the body must perform; as moves stick and are confirmed, focus narrows to the crux; once the crux is solved and the climb is sent, the brain stops solving.
- After a send, decide whether to repeat the climb or move on. The three steps below identify which climbs or moves are most beneficial to repeat, so repetition is efficient and makes the best use of time and energy.

## Step 1: Identify your climbing goal
- Define the goal: what you are training for and the style of the climb (type of holds, moves, wall angle, length, intensity, techniques required).
- Repeating a climb develops muscle memory and specific strength.

| Purpose | Climbs to repeat |
|---|---|
| Prepare best for a specific goal | Climbs as similar to the goal as possible |
| Improve overall technique as much and as fast as possible | Climbs that represent and test your weakest areas: the styles you train least, which have the most ground to gain |

## Step 2: How physically intense is it?
- Aim for as many repetitions as needed to guarantee progression and mastery of new skills: repeat a new set of movements 5–10 times every 7–10 days, to learn moves fast enough to develop muscle memory.
- If a climb is so physically intense that you can only get one or two laps per session, it may be too hard to develop muscle memory on its movements. Repeating it still has value, but if the goal is to improve movement, repeat something slightly easier that still challenges your movement and allows more laps.

## Step 3: What is the quality of the climb?
- Check whether the climb gives a new and unique or highly applicable stimulus. If it is full of simple movements you are well versed in, or nothing like what you will meet on your goal, choose another climb.
- Instead of moving on, reduce the climb to only its high-value moves. This helps most on physically intense climbs with technical moves you have a lot of room to improve on. It is like projecting, but with the added repetition you keep improving the trickiest moves even after you execute them.

## Blend of repeated and new climbs
- Like repetitions in strength training, repetitions in skill learning should be a staple of climbing training. There is no hard rule on how much, but every session, week and cycle should be balanced with a guaranteed percentage of successful repetitions and improvements in specific areas.
- Include repetition training in every session, even if it is only a warm-up climb with a tricky technique you know you can progress.
- Repetition done correctly on the right type of climbs guarantees improvement. Getting on climbs that are too difficult too often means pulling only a handful of moves per session.
- Reduce how often you repeat a climb as time goes on: this maintains the improvements made, frees time for learning new movements and avoids accommodation and habituation.
