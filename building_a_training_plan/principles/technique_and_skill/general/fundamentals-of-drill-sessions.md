# Fundamentals of Drill Sessions

**Type:** Principles, Session · **Trains:** Technique

**TL;DR:** Drills are the tool for developing specific facets of technical skill; start with positioning-based drills. Spend 20–30 minutes in total on skill work within a climbing session and stop earlier as soon as movement breaks down. Make drills measurable, increase the number of weekly touches across a training cycle, regress a drill you can't manage and progress one that is too easy. Work on 1–2 skills per month.

## Who should do what

| Situation / level | Do this |
|---|---|
| Rebuilding fingers after recurring finger injury | Combine finger development with skill development: build skill work into the strengthening plan to stay on track and motivated to stay the course, and to make use of the time given to the fingers, instead of leaving the rest of the climbing to chance |
| Starting skill development | The logical first step is positioning-based drills |
| Can't do the baseline drill (e.g. for explosiveness, moving fast: can't link 5–6 powerful moves in a row) | Regress the drill |
| Drill is too easy | Progress it: always work against yourself and push a bit out of your comfort zone |

## Skill work alongside finger rehab

- The skill block chosen was slab climbing: rebuilding the ability to smear on granite and on non-footholds, and teaching the body to keep the hips and weight over the feet and to rely less on pulling with the hands. It ended with sends of hard, pure-friction slabs.

## Selecting skills

- Development has three areas: mental, physical and technical abilities. In each you can choose skills that build your ability to climb in a given style or terrain. Drills are the tool for developing specific facets of technical skill.
- Positioning drills teach you to use your body and move into the most efficient positions on the rock, for economy, to conserve energy and for safety. Examples: clipping stances, resting in specific positions. They work on footwork and hip position, body tension, balance and flexibility.
- Positioning, movement and momentum drills are not strict categories: they overlap and build on each other in a cycle.

## Session length and when to stop

| Element | Guideline |
|---|---|
| Total skill work within a climbing session | 20–30 minutes |
| Longer than that | Technical breakdown and fatigue set in; these are the signs to stop |

- **Warning:** stop signs are your eyes starting to dart around, or finding it harder to place hands and feet where they need to go. Then end the drill work and move on to the next part of the session. Cutting it short is better than completing the 20–30 minutes: you don't want to teach yourself poor movement; masterful movement is the focus.
- Cue: climb as if you were showing off to someone, for the whole drill session. If at some point it no longer feels that way, stop and move on.

## Measuring drills

- Make drill sessions measurable, so you can track progress or regression and adjust. Metrics can be the height of your high step (in cm), the number of good technical foot movements per session, or the number of moves done with good eye contact.
- Tracking these over a cycle tells you how many touches you have had on a drill over a given time, which gives confidence that you are putting in the time to improve a skill and applying overload.

## Managing skills in a climbing session

- Aim for an increasing number of touches on a drill across the whole week over a training cycle. Deliberate exposure to drill work is a good marker of progress.

- The drill session must be manageable. To make it manageable, cut the number of reps done in a row: instead of a whole boulder problem for power, do half of it, or link 1–2 moves at a time. Track it: being able to do 2–3 power moves in a row shows your capacity for the drill is improving.

## How to regress or progress a drill

| Regress (drill too hard) | Progress (drill too easy: more complexity or demand) |
|---|---|
| Slow the movement down | Steeper angle |
| Do fewer reps | Smaller handholds and footholds |
| Reduce the complexity of the movement, e.g. go from a steep angle to a less steep or vertical one | Greater distance between holds |
| Do the drill more often but for less time per session | More reps per drill session |
| | Lower frequency of that session across a week or cycle |

| Stage | Time per session | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Standard | 20–30 minutes | |
| Regressed (e.g. power development is hard) | 5–10 minutes | 3–4 times a week |
| As you progress | Increase the duration of each session | Pull back on the frequency |

## Focus and planning

- Don't focus on too much at once: spend one month on 1–2 skills at a time. As a habit, 1–2 skills per month develops a whole athlete and a better climber over time, and makes it easier to judge whether you are improving on those skills.
- Think masterfully: decide what you will pay attention to so you stay intent on the drill. For footwork, focus on specific things rather than footwork in general.
- Plan the drills within each session: schedule drill sessions within your training so you actually do them.
