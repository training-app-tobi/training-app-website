# Patterns Behind Very Strong and Very Weak Fingers

**Type:** Principles, Planning · **Trains:** Finger strength · **For:** Youth, stalled progress

**TL;DR:** Climbers with very strong fingers stay at a healthy athletic body composition, often trained fingers when young (with strictly controlled fingerboard work), and train high intensity with low volume. Climbers with very weak fingers tend to train fingers at the end of the session, switch methods too often, and have too much going on in work or other sports. Put finger training first after the warm-up, stick with one method for 6-12 weeks, and cut endurance-sport mileage by at least 50% to gain strength.

## Who should do what
| Situation / level | Do this |
|---|---|
| Juniors | Fingerboard training, with methods and guidance highly specific to the individual and under strict control of amount and methodology; don't rush into it. It can be a pillar of training and a way to build more robust athletes |
| Busy job | Split sessions across the day so all mental energy isn't saved for the evening; avoid heavy training cycles in the middle of big work projects or while setting up a new business |
| Doing other sports, especially endurance sports | Cut weekly mileage (or time) in those sports by at least 50% to gain strength |

## Before you start
- These patterns come from climbers with excessively strong or excessively weak fingers (the outliers). Matching one or two of the weak-finger traits is most likely not a disaster, but be aware they are associated with these observations.

## Strong-finger pattern: body composition
- Climbers who spend long periods with very low body fat percentages struggle to make significant strength gains and do not end up, long term, with very strong fingers or upper bodies.
- Lowering body composition can create the effect of a high strength-to-weight ratio, but in the long run it sets any athlete up for sub-optimal strength gains.
- Athletes whose absolute strength (exclusive of weight) keeps improving year after year stay at a healthy athletic body composition.
- **Warning:** BMI showed no significant correlation with performance, both in a group of coached athletes and across a large online logbook database (whose data granularity had issues). Climbing is a strength-to-weight sport, but getting increasingly light leads to big issues in the long run.

## Strong-finger pattern: train finger strength young
- Over 5 years of consistent methodology across both genders and a range of ages, juniors doing fingerboard training reached basic strength levels (in a range of grip positions) far beyond those of international-level juniors 5 years earlier, with lower injury incidence.
- **Warning:** No campus boarding for juniors: the intensity is too hard to control and the impact too high.
- **Warning:** All junior fingerboard training should be under the guidance of a professional with appropriate experience and qualification.
- Junior fingerboard training is not a silver bullet for success, just one part of the equation.
- Starting in your 30s or 40s: you may never reach your genetic potential, but it is not a disaster and no reason not to start.

## Strong-finger pattern: train strength with low volume
- The highest finger strength scores (especially in climbers over 30) correlate closely with periods of low training volume: short sessions, a lot of rest, and session quality always focused on high intensity; not 10 climbing sessions a week plus sport climbing all week plus a daily jog.
- Rule: train high intensity with low volume.
- Traits of climbers with extremely high finger strength: they love training at high intensity and work at their last few percent of strength and power; they aren't satisfied with moderate-intensity sessions and put quality above most other things; they often complain about lower-intensity, high-volume training even when it suits certain goals.

## Weak-finger pattern: training order
- **Warning:** To gain finger strength, put fingerboard training at the start of the session, right after the warm-up, even before a high-quality system board or onsight session. Don't train fingerboard, campus or limit bouldering at the end of the session.
- Why: to make the muscle (or nervous system) adapt you must work at an extremely high intensity; too low an intensity fails to trigger the physiological response.
- Fatigue builds through a session. High-intensity training for strength, power and aerobic power needs high intensity; when fatigued you can't keep the intensity high, so less stress reaches the structures you want to adapt.
- Fatigue also dampens coordination, which lowers movement economy and execution of the exercise.

## Weak-finger pattern: inconsistent methods
- Don't switch finger-strength methods too often (e.g. max hangs one week, heavy finger curls the next, campus boarding the week after). Be consistent, especially if you struggle with finger strength.

| Step | What to do |
|---|---|
| Choose | One method (two if you can handle the load); keep high intensity, a set movement and a consistent muscle group |
| Stick with it | 6-12 weeks (twice a week for 6 weeks is only 12 sessions) |
| Assess | Check whether you are getting the adaptations you want |
| Change | Then change it; don't stay on it too long |

- If you get bored easily, vary the movement but not the intensity or the muscle group.
- How to cycle max hangs and repeaters depends on the athlete and their aims. A mix of max hangs and repeaters through the weeks, also varying hold size, gives the best adaptations; try several combinations to find what works for you.

| Standard cycle options |
|---|
| Several 4-week cycles (plus 1 deload week) of max hangs |
| Alternate max hangs and repeaters every 4-5 weeks |

- Rule of thumb: keep the same protocols until you plateau, then change.

## Weak-finger pattern: too much going on in life
- Climbers with very hectic jobs, and climbers who do a lot of other sports (especially endurance sports such as running), struggle to gain strength.
