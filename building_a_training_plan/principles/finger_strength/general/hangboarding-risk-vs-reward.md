# Hangboarding: Risk vs. Reward

**Type:** Principles, Planning · **Trains:** Finger strength · **For:** Novice, Strong climbers

**TL;DR:** Strong climbers with extensive experience (6+ years) should hangboard as one component of their overall training program: finger strength will very likely increase and injury risk may drop. Novices should approach hangboarding cautiously, and strong climbers with under 6 years' experience should decide based on their goals and the risks. Hangboarding builds finger strength more than climbing alone, and finger strength is strongly linked to performance, but finger injuries often happen without warning.

## Who should do what
| Situation / level | Do this |
|---|---|
| Strong climbers with extensive experience (6+ years) | Hangboard, as one component of the overall training program: high likelihood of increased finger strength and potential for reduced injury risk |
| Novice climbers | Approach hangboarding cautiously: potential for increased injury risk, and some uncertainty on the best strategy for improving finger strength in this group |
| Strong climbers with low to moderate experience (under 6 years) | Make informed training decisions based on your goals and the risks involved |

- These recommendations won't apply to everyone: they are based on average training responses and some correlational data, and each individual will respond differently to hangboarding, regardless of experience level.
- They are for climbers whose goal is to improve bouldering and sport climbing ability, not other valid climbing goals (such as having fun and decompressing).

## Hangboarding and finger injury
Climbing-specific research on this question is limited. A 2023 survey of 434 Finnish climbers of various abilities and experience levels found:

| Group | Finger injury among those who had been hangboarding |
|---|---|
| At least 6 years' climbing experience | Less common |
| Male climbers with less than 6 years' experience but climbing at least 7a Font (V6) | More common |

- The survey says nothing about non-male climbers with less than 6 years' experience, or about anyone climbing below V6.
- Hangboarding can't be assumed to have caused the injuries: the hangboard may have been used as a rehab tool for past injuries, or strong ambition may have driven some climbers both to hangboard and to take too many attempts on their projects.
- **Warning:** The survey doesn't report how these climbers hangboarded: a higher injury rate among some hangboarders could have come from progressing too much, too fast.
- **Warning:** Finger injuries often happen without warning, which makes the right progression hard to anticipate. Decide to start hangboarding only with full acknowledgement of the risks.

### Why it might work this way
- Climbers with many years of experience may have adapted their fingers to the point where hangboarding is not only safe but preventive against injury.
- Strong but inexperienced climbers may not yet have strengthened the structures supporting their fingers enough to withstand the loads they can create on the hangboard.

## Finger strength and climbing performance
| Climbers studied | Finding |
|---|---|
| 205 sport climbers (from under 1 to over 9 years' experience) | Hang time on a 25 mm rung explained 70% of climbing performance |
| 78 male intermediate to elite climbers | Climbing performance correlated with peak force (73%), average force (77%) and rate of force development (64%) on a 23 mm rung |
| 30 advanced sport climbers, comparing physical, technical and mental factors | Finger strength was the single most important factor for performance |

- It is not clear whether finger strength is still the most important factor for novices: those studies didn't analyse novices separately from advanced climbers, so for novices another factor (such as technique) may matter more. But a novice may become an advanced climber, at which point finger strength could become crucial.

## Does hangboarding build finger strength and performance?
- Hangboarding builds finger strength more than climbing alone, at least for intermediate to advanced climbers and competitive boulderers (the groups studied): in at least 5 studies, a 4 to 10 week hangboarding protocol increased finger strength (maximum force, rate of force development, maximum hang time) more than just climbing.
- Open question: whether hangboarding improves actual climbing performance more than climbing alone. Finger strength isn't the only contributor; limited time and energy might be better spent on climbing itself, which arguably trains technical and mental components better. The available hangboarding studies measured only finger strength, not climbing performance.
- Some climbing styles may challenge finger strength more than others; it is unknown whether climbing on the Moon Board or Tension Board could build finger strength as effectively as hangboarding.
- Prioritize your training methods by what you judge to be the most valuable use of your time and energy.
