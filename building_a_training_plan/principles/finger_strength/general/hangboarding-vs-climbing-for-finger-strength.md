# Hangboarding vs Climbing for Finger Strength

**Type:** Principles, Planning · **Trains:** Finger strength, Technique · **For:** Beginner, Elite

**TL;DR:** Finger strength and technique are the two most important aspects to keep improving; the hangboard isolates and measures finger strength, while climbing trains technique, movement, grip variety and the mental side. Beginners use the hangboard as a small (2 to 5%) portion of the warm-up; elite climbers limited by finger strength may take 5–50% of their finger load from it. No one has reached the highest level with the hangboard alone or as the majority of their training, so plan how much to hangboard, structure it and track your data.

## Who should do what
| Situation / level | Do this |
|---|---|
| Beginner | Hangboarding as a small (2 to 5%) portion of the warm-up, to warm up the fingers safely in a calculated, repeatable way, leaving the rest of the session for climbing |
| Elite (many years of movement experience, often limited by finger strength rather than technique) | A higher portion of finger load from the hangboard: 5–50%, depending on the focus of the training cycle, as strength training protocols |
| Short on time or no climbing wall | Use the hangboard: a convenient, simple and time-efficient way to train finger strength |

## Why fingers and technique
- Finger strength is the greatest determinant of climbing ability, and the hangboard is one of the only tools in climbing that targets it alone.
- Finger strength and technique are by far the two most important aspects to keep improving to raise your level. The main focus of the vast majority of training periods (if not all) should be developing one or both of them.

## What hangboarding and climbing have in common
- The hangboard and climbing both load the fingers. To be beneficial, both must stay above the Minimum Effective Volume (MEV) and at or below the Maximum Adaptive Volume (MAV) and the Maximum Recoverable Volume (MRV).
- Both must be done at the intensity and volume that is right for you, and progressed at the proper rate to keep improving.
- Hangboard and climbing sessions only lead to gains if they are followed by adequate rest before you load the fingers again.
- **Warning:** Too much intensity or volume, in climbing or on the hangboard, gives negative results or injury.
- For best results: learn as much as you can, build a plan designed for you that fits your schedule, and track data as you go through the program.

## Hangboarding: pros and cons
| | Point | Detail |
|---|---|---|
| Pro | Easy to measure | The total load of each session per grip type is easy to calculate from the exact weight, hold size, total time under tension and set/rep count |
| Pro | Reliable and consistent | The hangboard doesn't get reset and stays the same over the long term, so results are valid and you can judge whether your methods work |
| Pro | Low cost | A one-time investment of about $75–175 for a home set-up, much cheaper than a home climbing wall |
| Pro | Fingers isolated | No other variables; the fingers and forearm flexors are what is targeted. For this reason it is arguably the most reliable test of finger strength |
| Pro | Lower injury risk | Most hangboard protocols are static, so the injury risk at high intensities is lower than with many dynamic training methods |
| Pro | Useful during injury | Because it isolates the finger flexors, it is often the way to maintain and improve finger strength when another body part is injured |
| Con | No movement or skill | None of the body positions, movements and technical skills that climbing demands |
| Con | High opportunity cost | The more time spent hangboarding, the less time to practise and learn the skills and techniques needed to use finger strength for top performance |
| Con | Limited grip positions | Hangboards typically have 4–5 hold types; climbing has an endless variety of grip positions that the hangboard can't train |
| Con | No mental component | Apart from trying hard, the hangboard lacks the mental aspects of climbing, which are the third most important aspect for progressing in climbing |

- **Warning:** High form dependency: with poor form or an anatomical/muscular imbalance, the repetitive nature of hangboarding can overload soft tissue more quickly, leading to injury or a growing imbalance over time.

## Climbing: pros and cons
| | Point | Detail |
|---|---|---|
| Pro | Technical diversity | Trains all the techniques and skills used in climbing |
| Pro | Full spectrum | Trains all the muscles of the body, coordination, mobility and movement patterns at the same time |
| Pro | Complexity and problem solving | Every climb is different and forces mind and body to adapt to new stimuli, which is crucial for developing and reinforcing skill and strength |
| Pro | Wide variety of grip positions | The endless number of grips makes hands smarter and stronger |
| Pro | More solutions | Climbing offers the greatest number of highly relevant training opportunities and covers a wider range of a climber's strength and skill profile |
| Pro | Dynamic | Climbing requires moving at high speed, so it trains coordination, power (strength x speed) and movement skill |
| Con | Validity issues | Improved performance may come from several variables, or one you don't expect (e.g. better movement efficiency makes climbs feel easier and fingers feel stronger, rather than a real finger-strength gain) |
| Con | Hard to measure | You can't measure the load through the fingers and forearm flexors for each move or climb |
| Con | Moves not guaranteed | More failed repetitions, because difficulty varies between moves and because of technical shortcomings (foot slips, poor technique) |
| Con | Higher cost, time and space | A decent home wall costs much more than a hangboard (about $300–3,000 depending on many factors), takes longer to build and takes much more space |

## How much hangboarding
- How much of your program goes to hangboarding depends on your needs: the share of time for any form of training should follow from what you can benefit from most at this stage of your climbing.
- The more time and energy you put into hangboarding, the less you can safely and effectively put into other forms of training that stress the fingers.
- Beginners need to develop every aspect of their strength and skill profile, which is why the hangboard is only a small part of their sessions.
- Climbing alone can take you to your maximum potential; hangboarding can be used tactfully to speed up the process and avoid setbacks along the way.

## Using climbing like a hangboard
- Climbing can be used the same way as hangboard training: sets, reps and time under tension at intensities you can measure yourself (less accurately than on a hangboard). Carefully selecting or creating your own climbs, setting rules and structuring the parameters and execution style of sessions can develop finger strength as effectively as hangboarding.
- This is very hard for the average climber, because of the time it takes in sessions, learning sports science, and recording and analysing data.

## Using the hangboard well
- The hangboard can help a climber prepare, train and recover throughout a climbing career.
- Climbers have reached the highest level with and without a hangboard, but no one has got there with the hangboard alone, or with it as the majority of their training.
- Use the hangboard safely and responsibly, and decide how much time and energy this one-dimensional training deserves at this point in your career: every second of hangboarding has a cost, so it must be worth it.
- Organize the time spent: lay out a well-structured program, record your data and make decisions from it.
- Every training tool has a place; learn each tool and your goals so you use it in the right way, at the right time and in the right amount.
