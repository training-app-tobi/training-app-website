# Finger Training Questions: Grip Specificity, Light Hangs for Recovery, Weak Fingers and When to Start Hangboarding

**Type:** Principles, Planning · **Trains:** Finger strength, Recovery · **For:** Beginner, weak fingers

**TL;DR:** You only get stronger within plus or minus 15° of the joint angle you train, so one grip position does little for another. Light hangboarding can promote blood flow and recovery, but with sore, fatigued fingers use just light movement instead of adding hangboard load. With weak fingers keep at least 75% climbing to 25% finger training, and six months into climbing don't hangboard at all: many of the adaptations you need only come from the wall.

## Who should do what
| Situation / level | Do this |
|---|---|
| Six months into climbing | Don't hangboard, even if you feel you lack power and your technique is good: spend a lot of time on the wall |
| Weak fingers | Keep a ratio of 75% climbing to 25% finger training, or even more climbing |

## Grip specificity
- You only get stronger at the joint angle you train, within plus or minus 15° of flexion at that joint. This is generally true of all joints (some work slightly differently, but it is a good rule to know).
- So there is not much crossover between grip positions: training the three-finger drag does little for the half crimp.

## Light hangboarding for blood flow and recovery
- Loading tissue brings blood flow and the nutrients the body transports for tissue repair. Tissue benefits from light blood flow and light loading; depending on the intensity and the intention, light loading can bring about many different adaptations. So yes, light hangboarding can promote healthy blood flow and boost recovery.
- Everything, however healthy or useful done routinely, has a dose and an intensity at which it helps, and times when it helps and times when it doesn't.
- **Warning:** With a lot of fatigue in your training and sore fingers, don't add hangboard load "for recovery": your normal load may be too intense for fatigued tissue or tissue with micro-tears. Use light blood flow instead: just moving the fingers promotes good health, and many things done every day support healthy tendon gliding, blood flow and nutrient delivery to the tissue and tendons.

## Finger training with weak fingers
- If your fingers are weak, they are probably not your only issue: you need many other things too. So a ratio of 75% climbing to 25% finger training, or even more climbing, is always best.
- You may not need that ratio all year: some cycles can have a bit more finger training. But climbing is a skill sport, a lot of finger training happens on the wall, and there are many other aspects of climbing to work on, so a bigger split than 75:25 doesn't make sense.
- There are many forms of finger training. Some adaptations can't be had from a hangboard or from lifting weights off the ground: they only happen on the wall. Different protocols can replicate many of them, but climbing gives the best return.

## Hangboarding six months into climbing
- Six months into climbing, don't hangboard, even if you feel you lack power and your technique is good. You need a lot of time on the wall, and many adaptations you need come from the wall and won't be trained by the hangboard.
