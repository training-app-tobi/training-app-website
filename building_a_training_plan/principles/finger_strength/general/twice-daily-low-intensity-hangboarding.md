# Twice-Daily Low-Intensity Hangboarding: What It Does and Who It Suits

**Type:** Principles, Exercise/protocol · **Trains:** Finger strength, Recovery · **For:** Beginner, injured or returning

**TL;DR:** Low-intensity hangboarding twice a day (up to 10 minutes, at least 6 hours apart, for 30 days) is too light to build muscle, thicken or meaningfully stiffen tendons and ligaments, or improve high-threshold recruitment in healthy, trained climbers. Its main effect is probably reducing finger pain and discomfort, which lets you climb and train harder with confidence, plus some connective tissue remodeling. It does not replace high-intensity finger training; use it to build confidence on the hangboard, as a gentle warm-up, or when fingers feel tweaky.

## Who should do what
| Situation / level | Do this |
|---|---|
| Replacing high-intensity hangboarding | Think twice: the mechanisms that make high-intensity finger training effective are not at play in low-intensity hangs |
| Aiming for feats of strength | This protocol is not the way for feats like 1-5-9 on the campus board or hanging 6 mm edges: those need specific strength training that low-intensity hangs don't provide. For long-term strength, high- and low-intensity exercise are not interchangeable |
| New to climbing or intimidated by hangboarding | Use it as a virtually risk-free way to build confidence with fingerboard positions: start at as low an intensity as you want, no extra gear required |
| Healthy fingers, finger training progressing well | No strong need to add this protocol. You can, but the time could be better spent on e.g. improving mobility or addressing a nagging shoulder issue |
| Tweaky at the start of sessions | Use it as a gentle warm-up: more time spent loading the fingers safely is worthwhile |
| Tweaky fingers | The protocol is worth considering. Other methods may be preferable for these issues, e.g. farmer carries or no-hangs, which give a much more consistent, trackable load |
| In any kind of pain | Figure out the actual cause if possible: are you ignoring an injury that needs rest and rehab, how is your sleep, are you managing your overall training load well? |

## The method and where it comes from
- The method: low-intensity hangboarding two times per day for 30 days.
- It is based on a 2017 research paper ("Minimizing injury and maximizing return to play: lessons from engineered ligaments"): in an experiment on lab-grown, tendon-like tissue (engineered "sinew"), even low-level loads increased tenocyte activity and, ultimately, collagen concentration.
- From this, the suggestion for humans was: up to 10 minutes of low-intensity exercise, with at least 6 hours between sessions. The hangboard version loosely follows these guidelines and uses a much shorter duration than 10 minutes, to play it conservative.
- Reported results with the method (two highly trained climbers, 30 days, later continued): within the first month the fingers felt healthier than ever before; max hangs up about 10 kg; while continuing it, a first 8C (V15) boulder and 1-5-9 on the campus board for the first time. Over the same period other training routines were added as well.
- This low-intensity routine goes against every high-intensity protocol, so the question is how such light hangs could give such large benefits.

## Why the research paper doesn't explain the gains
- The tissue-loading recommendations come from an in vitro study: young, lab-grown tissue in a nutrient gel attached to cement anchors is very far from a real tendon in an adult hand hanging on a board.
- In vitro studies are not useless, but in vitro results are frequently found incompatible with real-world findings, so the study's results can't be assumed to happen in a climber's fingers.
- The recommendations are open-ended, which leaves room for many other variables to affect the results. The only specific part of the study a climber could follow is the time under load (10 minutes), and the hangboard version used a much shorter duration.
- So the study shouldn't be used to draw conclusions about the results; other mechanisms have to be examined.

## Mechanism 1: muscle hypertrophy
- Muscle hypertrophy (a muscle increasing in size) matters for climbers because a muscle's cross-sectional area is the biggest determining factor of its strength.
- Under the right conditions, the isometric contraction of hangboarding can make muscles grow; this is a huge, often overlooked way hangboarding helps climbers progress.
- The muscle won't grow without enough stimulus over the workout. You don't have to go all the way to failure, but with low-intensity reps you need to get in the vicinity of it.
- The twice-daily protocol is designed to stay far away from fatigue, so even two sessions per day are extremely unlikely to cause hypertrophy, especially in a highly experienced athlete rather than an untrained person.
- Verdict: muscle growth can be ruled out as a direct result of the protocol.
- To force hypertrophy with this protocol, add blood flow restriction: it can drastically increase muscle fatigue even with low loads.

## Mechanism 2: tendon and ligament thickening
- Research on tendon growth protocols is hard to come by in general and non-existent for climbers, but connective tissue does grow: climbers can have significantly thicker pulleys than non-climbers.
- Achilles tendon evidence: the tendon increased in cross-sectional area with high-intensity exercise (about 90% of maximum voluntary contraction); it did not grow with lower-intensity exercise.
- Current evidence suggests connective tissue grows under conditions similar to muscle: relatively high fatigue or high load is required in trained individuals. This may not apply to damaged tissue or untrained individuals.
- For a healthy, well-trained climber, the extremely low fatigue of the protocol makes meaningful thickening of tendons and ligaments unlikely.

## Mechanism 3: tendon and ligament stiffening
- Isometric exercise can increase tendon stiffness, and stiffer flexor tendons could in theory increase finger strength.
- But most research shows tendon stiffness increases only with moderate- to high-intensity exercise, around 70–100% of 1RM, though smaller effects could still happen at milder intensities. The research is not perfect nor perfectly applicable to climbers.
- Verdict on tendons: the protocol was probably not intense enough to meaningfully stiffen the flexor tendons. Some small stiffening is possible, but it is highly unlikely to have caused large improvements or an increased risk of injury.
- Ligaments (pulleys): stiffer pulleys could improve crimping, but research suggests ligament stiffening happens as a by-product of thickening, which seems to need high-intensity stimulus, so it probably doesn't apply to this protocol.

## Mechanism 4: recruitment
- Strength is not limited only by muscle tissue: the brain and central nervous system often limit peak force output so you don't hurt yourself, and sometimes the nervous system hasn't learned to produce high forces efficiently (there is noise in the signal).
- Training can partly remove these barriers, by teaching the nervous system you can handle high forces without injury and by practising producing those forces on command. This raises a muscle's total force output and how quickly it produces force, and lowers the stimulus needed, without changing the muscle tissue.
- Because the benefits of recruitment are about high force and/or high speed, recruitment protocols are high intensity.
- Low-intensity exercise doesn't activate the high-threshold motor units, so it won't improve high-threshold recruitment; it may only make low-threshold motor units fire more easily and quickly. Low-intensity recruitment training could improve low-intensity performance, but almost certainly not high-intensity performance in a meaningful way.
- Very low-intensity finger strength recruitment doesn't seem relevant to climbers, especially trained boulderers, whose performance centres on short bursts of high to maximal strength output.
- Verdict: the protocol appears too low intensity to cause useful recruitment.

## Mechanism 5: connective tissue remodeling
- Remodeling (not the same as thickening or stiffening) is when collagen fibres change their alignment or orientation. Tendon and ligament fibres are not always perfectly aligned, especially where injuries have occurred.
- Force or stretch on the tissue increases activity of tenocytes and uncrimps the collagen fibres, both of which can improve the tissue's orientation and strength.
- Remodeling can occur in damaged Achilles tendons even with something as simple as massage, so the protocol could plausibly cause some remodeling in the fingers.
- But animal studies indicate high-volume or hypertrophy-like training has a much higher impact on remodeling than low-fatigue training, and remodeling is much more pronounced in untrained and/or injured tissue than in trained and/or healthy tissue.
- Verdict: for a highly trained climber doing a low-intensity, low-fatigue protocol, remodeling probably contributes but is not hugely significant, unless the connective tissue was significantly damaged, in which case the effect would probably be greater. In the reported case there were no serious finger injuries, only generic tweakiness.

## Mechanism 6: pain science
- When there is pain, the brain naturally reduces force production in that area, so fingers can literally be weaker when they feel painful, regardless of the tissue's actual strength.
- This can become a feedback loop: being conditioned to expect pain in certain situations causes more strength loss, a fear response and even exaggerated pain, and builds a set of rules in the brain about what you will and will not allow yourself to do, consciously and subconsciously.
- Isometric exercise can alter the acute pain response through a complex mechanism: pain receptors become less active or excitable, the central nervous system dampens the pain response better (inhibitory interneurons), and your perception of the stimulus changes. This is related to recruitment but not strength recruitment in the traditional sense.
- When the low-intensity hangs remove pain or discomfort, the body's rules are rewritten: fingers feel and literally are stronger, you focus on trying hard instead of worrying about injury, feel psyched, perform better and in a relatively short time make significant gains in climbing and training metrics.

## Other explanations
- Other, less interesting variables could have caused some or all of the results: sleep, personal life, mindset, etc. These boring variables often have much larger effects than we like to admit.
- The placebo effect can, does and will continue to happen.

## Summary of mechanisms
| Mechanism | Verdict |
|---|---|
| Muscle hypertrophy | Unlikely: the hangs are too low intensity |
| Connective tissue thickening | Significant amounts unlikely, for the same reason |
| Tendon stiffening | May occur in small amounts; generally seems to need higher intensities |
| Strength recruitment | Might happen at some level, but probably not a significant or useful amount, as intensity is so low |
| Connective tissue remodeling | Probably contributes; the amount depends on how unhealthy the tissue was |
| Pain science | Almost certainly at play: less discomfort leads to psychological and physiological improvements, more and better training and improved performance |
| Other factors (sleep, mindset, placebo…) | Almost certainly had a large effect, though impossible to quantify |

- Overall: the protocol probably caused some direct strength improvement, but mainly it allowed harder climbing and training with new confidence, and that made the climbers stronger.
- The fingerboarding opened the door, the later climbing and training pushed through it, and the prior experience and fitness base were needed to find the door at all. Long-term gains come from smart training, hard work and a healthy mindset.

## Other uses
- More experienced climbers can also use it to experiment with grip positions and find useful insights about grip preferences (finger morphology, specific weaknesses, etc.).
- As a gentle warm-up the protocol can be useful, though it is not a complete finger warm-up. When used as a warm-up, ignore the 6 hours between sessions and go straight on to climbing.
- Increasing the frequency of light loads on the fingers can be quite useful overall; it is prescribed to some patients as a way to promote healing.
