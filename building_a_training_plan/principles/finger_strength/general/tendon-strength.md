# Tendon Strength

**Type:** Principles · **Trains:** Finger strength, Pulling strength · **For:** Advanced, Older

**TL;DR:** Stiffer, stronger tendons transfer muscle force to the bone better, reduce injury risk and improve maximal force and rate of force development, the two key factors of finger strength. Train them with high-load, short-duration, long-muscle-length isometrics: maximal open-hand hangs on an edge of around 20 mm, plus heavy weighted pull-ups. Ramp the load up slowly and expect tendon changes only after about 2 months.

## Who should do what
| Situation / level | Do this |
|---|---|
| Experienced and older athletes | Only these athletes should target their tendon strength: their tendons are a greater limiting factor, they will see greater results, and they already have the other necessary adaptations. |
| Want to improve other grip types too | Train those grip types as well: the greatest results come around the joint angle you actively train. |
| Speed climbing | The benefits of tendon elasticity should potentially be taken into consideration. |

## Why tendon strength matters
- Tendons, like muscles, are crucial components of the movement system. Training strong muscles is wasted if the force cannot be properly transferred to the skeleton because the tendons were neglected.
- Tendon strength is a different concept from muscle strength. Muscle strength is about exerting force on an external resistance (and differs from muscular endurance or explosiveness). Tendon strength is about having a robust, healthy tendon that eases force transfer from muscles onto bones.
- With tendon strength you improve muscular strength, endurance and explosiveness at once. For climbing, stiffer tendons are crucial, so your strength is better translated onto the wall.
- Analogy for tendon stiffness: you are the muscle, a sled is the bone. Pulling the sled uphill with a rope is easy because your force transfers properly onto the sled; pulling it with a resistance band is much harder because a lot of force is lost between you and the sled.

## When stiff tendons can hurt performance
- Tendon stiffness can be detrimental to athletes who benefit from short-term energy storage, e.g. sprinters: when tendons lose elasticity they don't spring back as well after elongation, which would otherwise aid the muscle contraction.
- Elasticity matters in sports where the stretch-shortening cycle plays an important role (start-stops, jumping, sprinting).
- For most climbing this elasticity is not very important, except maybe for moves like large triple dynos. The benefits of tendon stiffness far outweigh the negatives, especially in sport climbing and bouldering.

## Three reasons to work on tendon stiffness
- **Lower injury risk.** Tendon strength reduces injury risk (contrary to popular belief). In the sled analogy, the resistance band ruptures before the rope does; a compliant tendon is more likely to overstretch, overload and tear. Several studies suggest stiffness is a factor for injury, but a large part of that "stiffness" is caused by the muscle, not the tendon.
- **More maximal force and rate of force development.** Tendon strength improves maximal force and rate of force development (i.e. contact strength and explosive strength), the two key factors of finger strength in climbing. Higher force transfer from muscle to bone lets you exert greater force more quickly.
- **No loss of mobility.** Tendon strength does not directly correlate with decreased mobility/flexibility. Flexibility is mostly determined by the muscles, not the tendons. Regular stretching compensates for stiffer tendons.

## How to train tendon strength
- There is a lack of research on which training variables matter most for tendon strength. Some studies show isometric training gives the greatest results, but they do not focus on the upper body. Assuming forearm tendons act similarly, the fingerboard is an ideal tool for climbing.
- High-load, short-duration, long-muscle-length isometrics have proven especially effective: in practice, maximal hangs with an open-hand grip.
- An open hand also puts less strain on the finger tendons, reducing injury risk.
- Hang setup: always warmed up and from a rested state; you should already know your way around the fingerboard; use an edge of around 20 mm; adjust the weight so the hang feels maximal but you could hold on for a couple more seconds than required.
- Isometrics are not the only way: dynamic exercises with high loads (heavy strength training) also increase tendon stiffness. Adding heavy strength training to the plan can be a good idea, as it is generally very useful for climbing.
- **Warning:** Be patient: improvements in tendon stiffness only occur after about 2 months of training. Impatience leads to hasty overloading and a higher injury risk, which is exactly what tendon training is meant to avoid.
- A 9-week program (8 weeks of ramp-up, then a deload) is in the note Tendon Strength Program: Open-Hand Max Hangs and Weighted Pull-ups.

## Density hangs
- **Warning:** "Density hangs" do not necessarily improve tendon stiffness, although they may show some of the same results (e.g. greater rate of force development). This has been attributed to stiffness increases in the muscle (potentially leading to higher injury risk and decreased flexibility/mobility) and in the muscle-tendon junction, while tendon stiffness might even be reduced.
