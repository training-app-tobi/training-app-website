# Finger Strength Training: Methods, Grip Types and Progressive Overload

**Type:** Principles · **Trains:** Finger strength

**TL;DR:** Finger strength can be trained by climbing, campusing, hangboarding or lifting; each trades precision against specificity to climbing. Train the grip that matches the demands of your project or area (the 4-finger half crimp is the best all-rounder for most people), and keep full crimp for climbing, not the fingerboard. Injuries come from tasks you aren't prepared for, so progressively overload intensity, volume or frequency, one at a time. Justify every finger strength session by its grip, arm position and hang time.

## Methods of training finger strength
- There are 4 methods of training finger strength: climbing (bouldering or climbing on a board), campusing (on a campus board or a boulder wall), hangboarding (isometric hangs) and lifting (taken from grip strength sports).
- Each method differs in precision (how easy it is to control and measure) versus specificity to climbing, in how well it lets you control training load, speed and form, and in injury risk.

| Method | Pros | Cons | When and how to use it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Climbing | The most specific exercise for finger strength | Harder to manage the load, the grip types and the speed of contractions | The primary way to build sport-specific strength; can be used all year round, but higher-intensity sessions should be carefully monitored |
| Campusing | Good for specific catch and release and for stable grip types | Load isn't easily measured | Specifically targets contact strength; use it in short periods of training before a peak or in a power phase |
| Hangboarding | Extremely measurable, grip-specific hanging | Little to no movement specificity | Excellent for specific grip type training; large protocol variety makes it easy to use all year round |
| Lifting | Extremely accurate and controllable | No other muscles in the chain are used | Very accessible, easy to do anywhere; favoured by physiotherapists for injury rehab and prevention; handy for working weaker individual fingers |

## Grip types
- No single grip is "best". High-level climbers differ in their strongest grip: one is strongest on high-angle crimps (back 3 dominant), one on front 3 drag (open grip dominant), one on half crimp (front 3 dominant). Each moves slightly differently on the rock.
- The most appropriate grip position to train is usually the one that matches the demands of your project or of the area you want to climb in.
- Another reason to train a grip is to build resilience / load robustness in that position. What doesn't get your attention probably won't get changed.

### What grip positions to train

| Grip | Good for | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Half crimp | The best all-rounder for strength training, arguably more transferable than other positions | — |
| Front 3 drag | Catching holds and using pockets | Usually a slightly lower-risk position to train |
| Back 3 half crimp | Those with weaker pinky fingers | Higher risk than drag positions |
| Front 3 half crimp | Big moves (the pinky doesn't catch) and smaller-width edges | Higher risk than drag positions |
| Full crimp | Small edges | Higher forces through the joints and pulleys |

- **Warning:** Because of the higher forces through the joints and pulleys, train full crimp through climbing, not on a fingerboard, unless you are very experienced with a suitable training history.

### Open or closed positions

| Position | Benefit | Drawback |
|---|---|---|
| Open | Thought to be lower risk | Can limit some movement patterns and reduce maximal strength potential across the whole grip range |
| Closed | Transfers well to many movement styles and rock types for most climbers | Trains at a greater joint angle, which puts more stress on soft tissues |

- With closed positions, weigh risk against reward, together with load, frequency and the other training variables.

## What happens when you train your grip

| Adaptation | What changes | Speed |
|---|---|---|
| Neurological | Greater magnitude, rate of firing and synchronisation of motor units, plus coordination (skill) and antagonist co-activation changes | Usually quick changes, specific to a new protocol |
| Muscular | Whole-chain changes from the fingers up to the core/trunk: muscle size, muscle architecture (e.g. pennation angle, fascicle length) and blood supply network | — |
| Connective tissue (capsule, collateral ligaments, tendons, pulleys, etc.) | — | Many connective tissues adapt more slowly than muscles; keep this in mind when training your grip |

## Avoiding and managing injuries
- Usually the exercise or protocol itself isn't dangerous. Injuries come from tasks you aren't prepared for: a duration you don't have capacity for, an intensity you aren't ready to hold, push or pull, or a frequency of loading.
- To be prepared for the demands of the task, use the progressive overload principle.
- Progressive overload: adding training load as time passes (days, weeks, months) by changing intensity, volume and frequency, with the aim of continuous progress in performance.
- If you get injured despite best practice, get tailored advice from a climbing-specific physiotherapist or specialist.

### How to progressively overload finger training
Change one variable at a time.

| Variable | How to increase it | How it affects injury risk |
|---|---|---|
| Intensity | Increase the weight held on the fingerboard while keeping sets, reps and time under tension / volume the same | Prepares you for the maximal and supra-maximal loads your fingers are likely to meet while projecting |
| Volume | Increase the number of reps or total time under tension while keeping the intensity the same | Improves your ability to stay in your chosen grip position, without sliding into a non-preferred one, over many moves or long holds |
| Frequency (the often "forgotten factor") | Increase the number of sessions while keeping intensity and volume the same | Recovery and adaptation happen during rest; if frequency is too high, inadequate recovery and repair raise injury risk |

- An example 12-week protocol built this way is in the note A 12-Week Progressive Overload Protocol for Finger Strength.

## Planning a finger strength session
- Be able to justify every finger strength session in your plan, e.g. "I train the 4-finger half crimp because I want to get better at using incut crimps on overhanging terrain". If you can't justify it, reconsider including it.
- To justify a fingerboard session, decide three things: grip choice (which grip to strengthen), arm position (2 arms, 1 arm, different arm angles, or a lifting pinch and edge) and hang time.

### Grip choice
The 4-finger half crimp is generally the best option for most people. Other factors:

| Factor | Question | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Frequency of grip type | Is a grip type common on your project or at your local gym/crag? | Train it to be ready for those demands |
| Stopper grip type | Does a grip type often stop you progressing up a route? | Train it so you can pass the stopper moves |
| Chink in the armour | Is one grip type clearly weaker than the others? | Train it to bring it in line |

### Arm position and tool

| Option | Best for |
|---|---|
| Hangboard, 2 arms | The usual go-to; great for intermediate to elite athletes |
| Hangboard, 1 arm | Usually ideal for elite athletes, or for those who find the weight hanging from their hips uncomfortable |
| Lifting edge | Great for beginner finger strength training and ideal for rehabilitating a finger injury, because it allows accurate incremental loading |

- Be consistent: once you have chosen and tested an arm position and tool, stick with it for the rest of the plan.

### Hang time

| Protocol type | Contraction | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Max strength focused | 10 s or less | Max strength |
| Health focused | 20–45 s | Health |
| Repeaters | Back-to-back reps with short rests; traditionally 7 s on, 3 s off, aiming for 2+ repetitions | Power endurance / endurance |
