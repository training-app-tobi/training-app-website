# Building Finger Strength in Your First Year of Climbing

**Type:** Principles, Exercise/protocol · **Trains:** Finger strength · **For:** Beginner

**TL;DR:** Build finger strength on the wall first, then learn to use every grip type, and only then add hangboarding as a light supplement: longer, lighter hangs (e.g. 20 s with one foot on the floor), never maxing out. Tendons and ligaments may take 5 times longer to adapt than muscles, so go slow and steady. After more than 6 months of climbing, if you can hang bodyweight on a 20 mm edge for 7 seconds, consider a 7-second max hang test.

## Finger strength
- Finger strength is very important for climbing, and for a beginner there is more than one way to build it.
- Step 1, training on the wall: climbing more and climbing harder boulders or routes builds finger strength naturally. This is the first approach because it doubles up with skill development and full-body strength training.
- **Warning:** starting hangboard training too soon is likely to increase training volume and initially reduce recovery capacity, which limits the quality of your skill sessions (the priority).
- Step 2, vary grip types: a beginner may favour one grip; good climbers can use all grip types. This is a skill just like footwork: try different grips on different holds and be patient if some take longer to feel natural.
- Step 3, start hangboarding: it builds the specific finger strength climbing needs. Check your ego: hangboarding supplements your climbing and is not a source of accomplishment; don't chase strength gains over everything else.
- **Warning:** the hangboard is arguably safer than the climbing wall because it can be very controlled, but keep it light: maxing out on the fingerboard at this stage tips the risk-versus-reward balance.
- Slow and steady: use the hangboard to develop training capacity and longevity rather than maximum strength. Longer but lighter hangs, for example 20 s with one foot left on the floor, help develop strong tendons.
- **Warning:** tendon and ligament injuries are very common in climbing. These structures may take 5 times longer to develop than muscular strength, and a beginner will likely adapt quickly on the strength side, so you can be too strong for your own good. Slow and steady wins every time.
- Specific hangboard sessions and protocols are too individual to prescribe for a general audience. If you think hangboarding is needed to keep progressing but you need support, find a coach or an experienced trainer.
- If you can do a 7-second hang at bodyweight on a 20 mm edge and have been climbing for more than 6 months, consider testing your finger strength (7-second max hang).
