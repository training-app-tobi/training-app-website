# Conditioning and Mobility

**Type:** Principles · **Trains:** Antagonists & core, Mobility

**TL;DR:** Conditioning (physical fitness training that is non-specific to climbing) and mobility (range of movement around a joint) underpin long-term progression and sporting health, improve performance and reduce injury risk. Train both on the upper and lower body, roughly 2–3 sessions per week (more if doing mobility only), with relatively more volume in base periods and more specificity and less volume approaching peak periods. Keep form excellent in every conditioning session and stop anything that causes pain.

## What conditioning and mobility are
- Conditioning and mobility are fundamental pillars of athletic performance: foundational aspects of climbing and training that underpin long-term progression and sporting health.
- Conditioning, strictly, is "physical fitness": anything from maximal strength to endurance capacity. Its benefits include increased confidence, improved recovery rates, cognitive improvements, more refined skills and better health. Here it is defined as the physical fitness training that is non-specific to climbing.
- Mobility (flexibility) is the range of movement a limb has around a joint. It is one of the most neglected areas of training among climbers, but could have huge impacts on performance.
- Mobility is made up of three parts: muscle elasticity/length, joint structure and the nervous system.
- More mobility helps prevent injuries through unimpeded, fluid movement, and can decrease soreness and stiffness, particularly in athletes who train at much higher frequencies and intensities.
- To reach the highest levels of performance, the full length of the muscle must be used to exhibit power and strength.

## Why climbers should train them
- Conditioning: improves the body's exercise capacity; improves adaptation rates to training stimulus; decreases injury incidence; lets climbers work climbing-specific muscle groups without some of the risks of dynamic loading.
- Mobility: increases performance in training and on trips/comps; decreases pain/discomfort (mainly in the muscles exercised); improves habits around relaxation and stress relief; decreases injury risk.

## Boulderers and route climbers
- Conditioning and mobility are hugely beneficial to both boulderers and route climbers, are used throughout the climber's year, and underpin much of the sport-specific work done on the wall.

| Period | Conditioning and mobility |
|---|---|
| Base | Spend relatively more time on conditioning and mobility; use both regularly and with relatively high volume |
| Approaching peak | Increase the specificity of the exercise protocols and reduce overall volume: this training is less specific and may use up precious time that is needed elsewhere in peak periods |

- Vary the exercise methods over the months; don't get fixated on one protocol or intensity.
- Use both conditioning and mobility on the upper and the lower body; climbers easily forget the lower limbs.
- Rough guide for boulderers and route climbers: 2–3 sessions per week (depending on training history), more if doing mobility only.
- **Warning:** In all conditioning sessions, form must be excellent throughout and session quality high. If either is lost, stop for the day and adjust the next session.
- **Warning:** Don't continue either form of exercise if it causes pain.
- A simple core session is in the note A Simple Core Session: Twisting Crunches, Dish Tucks and Supermans.

## Long-term benefits
- Regular conditioning and mobility training increases sport performance and general athletic health; long-term improvements in both lead to a longer climbing career and lower injury rates.
- Training should take into account your training history, past/present injuries, physical profile and goals.
