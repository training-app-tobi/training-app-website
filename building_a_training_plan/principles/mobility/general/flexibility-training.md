# Flexibility Training: Active vs Passive, Stretch Types and Scheduling

**Type:** Principles, Planning · **Trains:** Mobility

**TL;DR:** Flexibility plays an important role in climbing performance. Active flexibility (range you reach with your own muscles) depends on strength at the end ranges, so active static stretching is essentially strength training and the fastest method; passive stretching mainly teaches the body to relax. Do active dynamic stretching daily and in every warm-up, active static once or twice a week (around 72 hours to recover), and passive static daily as low-intensity maintenance.

## Why flexibility
- Collected data show that flexibility plays an important role in climbing performance.

## Defining flexibility
- Flexibility is the extent of range of motion (ROM) that the limbs can achieve. People reach different ranges depending on whether they move the limb with the muscles surrounding the joint or with assistance (another limb, weights, or a friend pushing them into the split position).
- Terminology: this difference is often described as "flexibility vs mobility", but in the sport performance literature range of motion is described only as "flexibility"; "mobility" refers to the ability to move, to be mobile. Knowing this matters for reading the literature and building an evidence-based approach.

| Type | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Passive flexibility | The range of motion a joint can be pushed into | A friend pushing you down into the splits |
| Active flexibility | The range a joint can achieve using only the muscles surrounding and supporting it | A ballerina holding her leg high to the side |

- Muscles are weaker when elongated or shortened and strong in the mid-range, so the body protects itself from going into the weaker end ranges, where injury risk is higher. Increasing strength at the end ranges teaches the body it is safe to go there; this is why strength training is an important component of flexibility training.

## Myth: "Don't stretch, it's bad for performance"
- Maximal power has been shown to decrease immediately after long-duration, passive, static stretching, but power quickly returns if the rest between stretching and the power performance is increased. Rarely does anyone stretch and then immediately do an all-out power effort on the wall.
- In climbing we may not extensively stretch the muscles we want to be most powerful before performance or training. Lower-body muscles are rarely used in a powerful manner, so even losing some power in the hip adductors from stretching is unlikely to hurt climbing performance.

## Myth: flexibility doesn't reduce injury risk
- Many papers conclude that improving flexibility doesn't reduce injury risk, but a conclusion is only as good as the method. Example: hockey players did a sit-and-reach test and their season was tracked for injuries; flexibility in the test didn't reduce injury risk, but the sit-and-reach test is likely not a meaningful measure in a sport where most injuries come from change of direction or dynamic movement. So "being flexible doesn't reduce injury risk" is not that simple.
- To judge the link between flexibility and injury risk, assess flexibility through sport-specific movement patterns. A climber's shoulder tweak often comes from a more extreme position (e.g. high gastons); increasing range of motion so the position is no longer as extreme may reduce injury risk in these moves, especially if you develop **active** flexibility there, i.e. strength at the end ranges.

## Types of stretching
- Four main types. The first word says whether you use the muscles surrounding the joint to do the stretch (active) or not (passive); the second whether it moves (dynamic) or not (static). The active forms are what people often call "mobility".

| | Dynamic (moving) | Static (not moving) |
|---|---|---|
| Active (muscles around the joint do the stretch) | Active dynamic | Active static |
| Passive (no muscles around the joint) | Passive dynamic | Passive static |

- Dynamic and static flexibility can be trained separately, and improvement is most pronounced in the method you train, so static-active and dynamic-active range can differ in the same person. E.g. you may swing and kick your leg much higher than you can statically hold it; in climbing, you may kick a leg up onto a foothold but not place it there in a static, controlled way, so foot placement is sloppy. Train both if your sport demands both.

## Active dynamic stretching
- Example: leg swings, commonly used in a warm-up. Many climbers use it to raise arousal and feel ready to perform.
- Why it is worth doing: muscle spindles contain two fibres that sense the magnitude of stretch; one mainly picks up the velocity of the stretch, the other mainly the force. Without stretching with velocity you neglect training this component of the muscle.

## Active static stretching
- Active static stretching = using your muscles to hold static positions near your end range. Two main types, both useful with their own benefits:

| Type | What it does | How it works / example |
|---|---|---|
| Agonist isometric | Strengthens the muscle you are stretching | Increasing its strength at the end range makes it safe to go into that range, so the muscle lets you access it uninhibited |
| Antagonist isometric | Contracts the antagonist of the muscle you want to stretch | Reciprocal inhibition: when a muscle tenses, its antagonist relaxes to allow the joint to move (bringing the hand to the shoulder, the biceps tenses and the triceps relaxes, otherwise the muscles would fight each other). Example: a high V-sit, where the hip flexors contract and strengthen and the glutes relax and stretch |

- PNF (proprioceptive neuromuscular facilitation) stretching is agonist isometric stretching with assistance, often from a partner: intermittent contractions and relaxations. During the contraction the partner counters the force you exert; during the relaxation the partner pushes you into a deeper stretch. It works because there is a short window of post-contraction relaxation that allows increased range of motion.

## Passive static stretching
- The best-known form of stretching. It is effective but likely gives the slowest results. The active methods are essentially strength training (working on physiological components, by strength and conditioning principles); passive static stretching essentially teaches the body to relax.
- Passive static stretching should therefore be much lower intensity and used more as maintenance; do the higher-intensity stretching with the active forms.

## Passive dynamic stretching
- Very rarely used, because it usually needs a partner to move the joints in and out of their end ranges. Goal: teach the body to relax at the end ranges. The golgi tendon organ receives signals telling it to create stiffness in the muscles; as the body realises these ranges are safe, it relaxes and activates less often, allowing better range of movement.

## Aims of each type

| Type | Aims |
|---|---|
| Active dynamic | Increase active dynamic range, including "cold flexibility"; active dynamic flexibility depends on the "range in reserve" (maximum warm range minus cold range) |
| Active static | Essentially strength training: increases strength in a range; the fastest method for flexibility; increases the maximal static range and therefore the range in reserve |
| Passive (static and dynamic) | Relaxes the muscles and assists the other methods; increases warm range of motion; speeds up flexibility gains through increased frequency of flexibility training; mainly neurological, so it must be maintained regularly to feel the benefit |

- Treat the strength (active static) sessions like any other strength training: they need rest and recovery.

## How to fit it into your plan

| Type | How often | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Active dynamic | Daily and in every warm-up | Can be done very frequently without fear of overtraining or overloading. Make it as sport-specific as possible: which moves do you want to complete? E.g. for high feet, forward leg kicks (legs straight or bent) |
| Active static | As often as any other strength training, e.g. once or twice a week | Essentially strength training, so it needs around 72 hours for full recovery |
| Passive static | Daily, or during warm-up and cool-down | Make it a routine, e.g. on rest days or while watching TV |

- **Warning:** Active static training can cause quite severe DOMS, especially when you are new to the exercise.

## Individual differences: current flexibility
- Bodies are not anatomically identical and people are at different stages of flexibility training, so the same stretch can feel different to you than to a friend. Choose stretches, or variations, that suit your current flexibility.
- Example, pancake stretch:

| Situation | Variation |
|---|---|
| In the gravity-neutral variation (on the floor) you can't get your lower back past 90 degrees | Use the gravity-assisted variation (standing) first |
| The gravity-neutral position is too comfortable | Move to the gravity-assisted variation to push the stimulus |

## Individual differences: bone structure
- Flexibility training changes the structure of the tissues, but the bones are part of the joint too; pelvis position matters in lower-body stretching. Side split example: how you position your pelvis changes the range you can reach, and testing your hip limit in a few hip positions will likely give different ranges. With a very neutral pelvis you may feel "compression" at the end of the range, where the pelvis blocks further movement. To avoid it, tilt the pelvis anteriorly: the front of the pelvis moves down (the bum sticks out).
- Pelvis and femur shape vary. For most people the head of the femur won't limit flexibility, but for a small proportion there is a genetic limit, due to the size or the angle of the femur head. Sign: a hard finish at the end of the range without feeling a stretch (the point where the bones meet). For most people pelvic tilt lets the femur go past the hip bone for a deeper stretch; for some it doesn't. Experiment to see whether this applies to you.
