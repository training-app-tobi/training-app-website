# Flexibility Training Is Individual: Loaded Methods and How Long It Takes

**Type:** Principles · **Trains:** Mobility

**TL;DR:** Flexibility training is as individual as any other training: some people gain from 60 s passive-static stretches a few times a week, others need other methods. People with a heavy lifting background may need holds of up to 5 minutes; men tend to respond best to strength work and isometric holds under tension, women may respond better to passive-static stretching. Loaded progressive stretching (slow tempo weighted exercise, isometric holds, CR and CRAC) builds strength through range: add only enough load to push you a little deeper. Plan flexibility as seriously as strength and fitness, and expect big gains in 3 to 6 months that are then easy to maintain.

## Flexibility training is individual
- Flexibility training is as individual as any other form of training. With passive-static stretching, for example, some people gain from 60 s stretches a few times a week; for others this simply won't work.

| Who | What tends to work |
|---|---|
| Heavy weightlifting background | May have a lot of intrinsic muscle tension and may need to hold passive-static stretches for up to 5 minutes before seeing a significant increase in range of motion |
| Men | Tend to respond best to strength training and isometric holds under tension |
| Women | May respond better to passive-static stretching |

- If a method doesn't work well for you, there are many other viable methods to try.

## Active and loaded methods

| Method | Example | How | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Slow tempo weighted exercise | Lowering in and out of a deep horse squat | Load may be added, but only enough to pull or push you deeper into the stretch; the intention is always to increase range of motion. Use a slow tempo, even a pause of several seconds in the stretch | Emphasises the stretch and reduces reflex or stretch-shortening contractions |
| Isometric holds | Holding a long lunge | Hold a stretched position under your own strength for a time | Builds strength in or near your current end range; as strength increases the muscle becomes more "comfortable" in that range, so you can progressively increase the depth of the position |
| Contract-Relax (CR), a form of PNF (proprioceptive neuromuscular facilitation) stretching | — | Contract the muscle being stretched (the agonist), then relax it | Lets the muscle relax more, increasing range of motion and the stretch; the contraction also builds strength at or near your current end range |
| Contract-Relax-Antagonist-Contract (CRAC) | — | As CR, plus a forced contraction of the opposing (antagonist) muscle to "pull" yourself further into the stretch | Through reciprocal inhibition the stretched muscle should be forced to relax further |

- Many of these methods are "loaded progressive stretching": loaded (bodyweight or added load), active, and progressed in either weight or range of motion. The principle is always the same: develop strength through range.
- **Warning:** Don't let the load become the task: too much weight can limit flexibility because the muscles can't relax. Moving under your own strength is the goal; this is where sport-specific skill transfer comes from.

## Where to start
- If you don't already plan flexibility training as seriously as your strength and fitness training, start doing so.

## How long it takes
- Everyone progresses at a different rate: experiment to find what gives you the quickest gains, and change things when you hit a plateau.
- In 3 to 6 months people see big improvements in their flexibility and climbing. It is unlikely to be a quick process.
- Once you have built flexibility and strength in the new range, it is very easy to maintain.
