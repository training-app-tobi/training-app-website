# Stretching for Flexibility: Types, Timing and Dose

**Type:** Principles · **Trains:** Mobility

**TL;DR:** Warm up before stretching; if time or space is short, foam roll the area first. To increase tissue mobility and joint range of motion, warm up with a light activity and then do static stretches, at any time except immediately before exercise; before a workout, use dynamic and then ballistic stretches. Stretch hard enough to reach the plastic zone of the tissue, but not into pain. Static stretching at least 5 days a week for 5-10 minutes per muscle group per week is enough: for example 3 sets of 30-second stretches per muscle group, 5 days a week (90 seconds a day, 7.5 minutes a week).

## Who should do what
| Situation / level | Do this |
|---|---|
| New to stretching | Do not stretch into pain: pain may trigger a protective response from the golgi tendon organ (GTO) or muscle spindles, or mean you are approaching the tissue's failure point |
| Acute inflammation from training | You can stretch; research shows stretching can reduce inflammation. Warm the tissue up appropriately first, then stretch at adequate intensity; you may feel better afterwards. Listen to your body |

## Warm up first
- Warm up before stretching whenever possible. A warm-up is a necessary part of stretching: it warms the tissue, increases blood flow to the area and reduces some built-in protective mechanisms, so you stretch more efficiently.
- If time or space is an issue, foam roll the area before stretching it. Research supports that foam rolling can improve mobility; it moves fluids around and reduces motorneuron excitability, so you reach a better stretch.

## Types of stretches
| Type | How | Dose |
|---|---|---|
| Foam rolling | An external device (the foam roller) helps stretch and elongate tissue. Also often used for joint cavitations of the thoracic spine. Can be used on many body parts | 3-5 minutes |
| Static | A sustained stretch, on large or small muscle groups. Active static: engaging one muscle group to stretch another (e.g. engaging the quads to straighten and stretch the hamstrings). Passive static: an external force applies the stretch (gravity, a tool or a partner); passive is slightly more common, since gravity is often the assisting tool | Hold 30-60 seconds |
| Dynamic | Performed in a controlled manner like active or passive stretching, but without a long hold | Hold about 2-3 seconds, then release |
| Ballistic | Performed in a bouncing manner, using quick movement to stretch the tissue (e.g. swinging arm hugs, small bounces to stretch and warm up the calf muscles). Differs from dynamic because there is no hold | No hold |
| Proprioceptive Neuromuscular Facilitation (PNF) | Contract, then relax the muscle group. Each time the tissue relaxes, take it deeper into the stretch if possible | Contraction typically 7-15 seconds, then relaxation for about the same time |

## When to do each type
| Purpose | What to do |
|---|---|
| Increase tissue mobility and joint range of motion (ROM) | Warm up with a light activity, then do static stretches. A meta-analysis showed static stretching has the greatest effect on ROM; all types of stretching improved ROM, so other forms still work, but static is best |
| Loosen up and/or get ready for activity | Do dynamic stretches (same setup as your static stretches but no long hold, held for as little as one second), then move on to ballistic stretches |

- Static stretches can be done essentially any time except immediately before exercise. Doing them after exercise is a great idea because the tissue is already warmed up.
- **Warning:** Before a workout, prefer dynamic and ballistic stretches over static: studies have shown static stretching can reduce muscle power because it reduces motorneuron excitability.
- An example routine for the wrist flexors, showing where each type fits in a session, is in the note A Wrist Flexor Stretching Routine: Where Each Stretch Type Fits.

## How intense stretching should be
- Intensity is subjective.
- Rubber-band model: stretch a little and the band quickly returns to its shape (elasticity); stretch further and hold it there and it begins to deform and doesn't return to its original shape (plastic region); pull hard enough and it snaps (failure point). To create a physiological change you must enter the plastic region.
- Aim for enough subjective intensity to reach the plastic zone, without pain, since pain may mean you are nearing the failure point.
- With more experience, stretches may be more uncomfortable as you push the limits, and you learn the difference between pain and discomfort.
- **Warning:** Golgi tendon organs and muscle spindles control muscle length. Stretch too quickly or too hard and the muscle contracts to resist the stretch, reducing or eliminating the stretch's effect.

## Frequency, reps and duration
| Finding or target | Frequency | Time per muscle group |
|---|---|---|
| Static stretching that may promote ROM gains (weekly frequency is positively associated with ROM) | At least 5 days a week | At least 5 minutes per week |
| Best result in one study | 6 days a week | 5-10 minutes per week |
| Main body of research | At least 5 days a week | 5-10 minutes per week (per week, not per day) |
| Practical target: 3 sets of 30-second stretches | 5 days a week | 90 seconds a day = 7.5 minutes a week |

- Consistency is ideal.

## How stretching improves mobility
- Two basic processes: sarcomeregenesis and decreased motorneuron excitability.
- Sarcomeregenesis: the creation and serial deposition of new sarcomeres (the contractile units), which lengthens the tissue and improves mobility.
- Reduced motorneuron excitability: stretching reduces the excitability of the GTOs and muscle spindles, allowing greater lengthening of the muscle and easier access to the plastic zone, where change (sarcomeregenesis) happens. This is part of why frequency theoretically matters: with frequent stretching the body reduces motorneuron activity more quickly, so you reach a proper stretch.

## Stretching acutely injured tissue
- **Warning:** If you tore something, do not stretch it.
