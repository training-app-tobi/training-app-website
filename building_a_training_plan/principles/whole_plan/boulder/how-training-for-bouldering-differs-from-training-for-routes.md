# How Training for Bouldering Differs from Training for Routes

**Type:** Principles, Planning · **Trains:** Power, Finger strength, General strength, Technique · **For:** Performance bouldering, advanced boulderers

**TL;DR:** Route-climbing training programs don't quite work for bouldering: boulder problems are short, intense, anaerobic sprints separated by long relative rests, so the plan needs more intense training with greater rest, and you stop when power declines. For performance bouldering, virtually eliminate endurance and power-endurance training, build strength and power, and keep sessions to about an hour.

## Who should do what

| Situation / level | Do this |
|---|---|
| Training for performance bouldering | Virtually eliminate all endurance and power-endurance training and focus on building high levels of strength and power. Each climber has limited physiological resources and adaptation potential: the more facets of performance you try to change at once, the smaller those changes will be |
| Advanced boulderer | Add special preparatory exercises (partial movements, similar to climbing in movement pattern or metabolic demand, done to increase specific strengths): bouldering alone doesn't give enough overload to improve |
| Developing maximum power and strength | Keep sessions shorter than a route climber's: a long session is about an hour |

## Bouldering needs its own approach

- Route-climbing training programs are often used for bouldering, but they don't quite work: bouldering needs its own approach.

## Boulder problems are shorter than routes

| | Effort | Duration of each effort | Rest between efforts |
|---|---|---|---|
| Typical sport-climbing day | 5-8 pitches | About 10 minutes each | 20 minutes or more |
| Bouldering | Problems | Usually less than a minute | Just 3-5 minutes |

- Routes involve much more low-intensity aerobic metabolism and much more total volume per day. Boulder problems are sprints: they depend heavily on alactic and lactic metabolism and are separated by greater relative rest periods.

## Boulder problems are more intense than routes

- Bouldering is anaerobic: a 200 m sprint compared with the 5 km run of a sport route. Each attempt on a boulder problem needs more energy per unit time than a route.
- So the plan needs more intense training with greater rest.
- Back this up with a nutrition plan that supports the different style of training.
- **Warning:** stop when it's time to stop: training past the point of power decline is counterproductive, invites injury, and you might actually be getting weaker.

## Body tension and core

- Bouldering requires much more body tension, core strength and focus than most routes; build these into every training plan.
- **Warning:** on artificial walls the footholds are often far too good to force real-world movements. Routesetting should reflect real-world movement as much as possible, and training should include tension sets.

## Bouldering days: less volume, more intensity

- You climb far less on a bouldering day, but much harder. A 12a-level climber might do 5-8 V3 or V4 moves in an entire day at the crag; the same climber might do five times that many in a bouldering session.
- **Warning:** recognise this difference to avoid injury when moving into a power phase.
- Strong boulderers make strong route climbers: the transition from high intensity to high volume is not very difficult.
- Building power takes a long time, which is why route-focused climbers have such a hard time progressing.

## Work-to-rest ratios

- Rest between problems is short in absolute terms but long relative to how long the problems take. Longer recoveries give much better power and strength gains in every session.
- **Warning:** without enough rest, sessions turn into endurance "short-route" sessions that no longer improve bouldering. Rule of thumb: if you're out of breath and sweating, you aren't training power.

## Bouldering is faster than route climbing

- Contact time (how long you grip a given hold) on a typical boulder problem is at most about 2/3 of that on a redpoint route and about 1/6 of that on an onsight. Take this into account when converting a bouldering season into routes.

## Bouldering is repetitive

- You try a given problem more times in a session than any section of a route. This speeds up learning, overloads the specific movements (faster strength gains) and teaches the mind to relax on hard moves.

## What a boulderer's training consists of

- Most of a boulderer's training is bouldering on an indoor wall, which closely simulates actual climbing.
- The tools for special preparatory exercises: system wall training, campus boards and targeted weight training. Used correctly and repetitively they make you a better climber; used wrongly they just make you tired.
