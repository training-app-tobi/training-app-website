# Mistakes That Cause a Climbing Plateau

**Type:** Principles, Technique · **Trains:** Technique · **For:** Beginner, stalled progress

**TL;DR:** A plateau is likely in the first couple of years of climbing, often somewhere between V3 and V6. Don't over-focus on strength; use varied technique drills that make you solve problems yourself; try hard climbs and less preferred styles; adjust a session's intensity or intention on bad days instead of skipping it; and treat movement inefficiencies as mistakes, repeating climbs until you've climbed them the best you can.

## When the plateau happens
- In the first couple of years of climbing, a plateau is likely. For many people it happens somewhere between V3 and V6.

## Mistake 1: too much focus on strength
- **Warning:** Strength is really important in climbing, but newer climbers often put too much emphasis on training strength in the early days.
- If weak fingers or upper body strength seem to hold you back, don't stress: weaker fingers or arms force you to find efficient ways to move, which can improve technique. Naturally stronger climbers often find good technique harder to learn, because they can pull hard through difficult movements.

## Mistake 2: not doing (the right) technique drills
- **Warning:** Isolated practice (repeating the same movement over and over until it's perfect) isn't necessarily the best way to learn a new skill: technique learnt this way often doesn't transfer well to the wall.
- Drills should encourage perception-action coupling with multiple affordances (options): a high amount of variation and multiple opportunities to solve the problem yourself, so you work out how to solve it for future application.
- Example drill: climb with only one hand. It forces you to move dynamically and makes you think about how you shift your weight.

## Mistake 3: not trying hard climbs
- Don't avoid climbs you find challenging, whether because of their style or because they are at the very limit of your ability. Mix less preferred styles into your climbing to stay well-rounded and to transfer the skills and strength used on them to the rest of the wall.

## Mistake 4: not listening to your body
- Going into a session with an intention (e.g. mileage or limit bouldering) helps.
- On days you feel rubbish (stress, a hard day at work, your period), you don't need to skip the session: changing its intensity or intention is often enough.

## Mistake 5: not learning from your mistakes
- A mistake isn't necessarily a fall; it can be a movement inefficiency. Example: a board session where the feet must stay on the wall at all times; if the feet cut loose, that counts as a mistake and the climb must be repeated.
- Repeat a climb several times until you're satisfied you've climbed it the best you can. Then try alternative methods: watch other climbers on it and give their beta a go.
