# Where Training Begins: Small Steps, One Focus

**Type:** Principles, Planning · **Trains:** Finger strength · **For:** stalled progress

**TL;DR:** Training is simple: take something you don't do well, break it into a small but achievable step, repeat that step until you can do it, then advance the difficulty again and again until you reach your goal. In practice it fails for two reasons: steps that are too big and trying to progress in too many things at once. Pick one thing, address it with your whole focus, and add load or duration over time to force a change. For finger strength, start with something so simple it seems it could never work, such as 5 sets of 10-second hangs on the minute on a 20 mm edge, and progress slowly.

## Who should do what
| Situation / level | Do this |
|---|---|
| Gone flat: months or a year with no advance in grades | Choose between accepting this as your level, with ups and downs, or training. Recommendation: train |
| Climbing and training for a few years, pretty good and strong, now no progress | Don't look for more motivation or throttle yourself in the gym even harder: pick one thing and address it with your whole focus |

## The plateau
- After a couple of months off, you start from what feels like zero: weak skin, sore feet, sloppy movement. It comes back, and within a few weeks you can be climbing your hardest ever.
- Going flat looks like this: months or a year with no advance in grades, feeling weak for a month and then back in shape without knowing why, still unable to climb a given problem.

## What training is
- **Warning:** Training that is fun and comfortable is probably not effective. You can still do a lot of bouldering in the gym, but when adaptation becomes too narrow, that is a red flag. Committing to training means more than moving your climbing performance indoors.
- **Warning:** If the desired outcome of a program is a specific grade, you can chase the wrong outcome: the number becomes more important than the ability. Strength example: lifting programs built on bench press and back squat with load progression; as load rises, squat depth creeps shallower until a reasonably strong kid "squats" 102 kg at half-depth. Climbing example: asking for the easiest 13a (a route most people call 12d) means wanting the grade, not the ability to climb at the grade.
- Training is simple: take something you don't do well, break it down into a small but achievable step, repeat that step until you can do it, then advance the difficulty again and again until you reach your goal.
- **Warning:** Two errors make it hard in practice: making the steps too big, and trying to make progress in too many things.
- Change one thing at a time: people change a habit around 70% of the time when making just that one change; add a second change and the success rate drops to about half that; a third and it drops to the teens.

## The interference effect
- Some studies find a conflict between strength adaptations and conditioning adaptations, e.g. running and lifting on the same day may negatively affect results in one or the other. How much you lose is debated, but the concept holds: even if you could do both on the same day, it may not be the best choice.
- Some people can hold onto a lot of power while building endurance, but adding focused isometric strength work and overall cardiovascular conditioning on top is too much: something has to give, and it gives sooner than we'd like. Being a general outdoor athlete generally results in mediocre performance across all activities; even elite athletes performing in multiple disciplines are subject to the same rule.
- To send a hard goal route (example: mid-5.14), even an elite all-rounder had to focus heavily on bouldering power and sport climbing. A move away from generalism is essential: being a universal athlete is amateurism. More focus, better results.
- Training is not about dying in the gym: set a goal, progress it over time, and make sure you add load or duration to force a change.

## Finger strength example
- **Warning:** Finger strength is consistently overdone: many holds and grips (open hand, full crimp), bent elbow, one arm at a time, added load, offset positions, and many people try to fit all these variants into one super-mega session.
- Start with something so simple and easy it seems it could never work, and progress slowly: tendons take time to adapt, and slow, steady progress over months is the way.

| Week | Protocol |
|---|---|
| 1 | 5 sets of 10-second hangs on the minute on a 20 mm edge (or a simple "Ten Minutes of Hangs" routine) |
| 2 | 18 mm edge, add one set |
| And so on | Keep progressing slowly |

- If hangboarding is already your thing, do trap bar jumps instead.

## How to progress
- An upward tick is all you need. Example, pull-ups: no need to double your strength, or to keep pushing once you've seen a jump in strength. Accept the gain, then move on to the next thing.
- The gain/maintain cycle also needs to be mastered, but it comes after you begin to train.
