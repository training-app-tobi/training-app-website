# Rest or Climb: When Tired, Back Off

**Type:** Principles, Injury & health · **Trains:** Recovery

**TL;DR:** Energy is a position: it is better to be healthy and resting, wishing you were climbing, than climbing and wrecked or injured, wishing you had rested. When something is tired or hurting, back off: training in that state wastes the effort, adds fatigue and delays your next useful session. One overdone session can cost weeks of progress, so decide with a plan, not emotions.

## Rest as a position
- "Energy is a position": it is better to be healthy and resting, wishing you were climbing, than climbing and wrecked or injured, wishing you had rested.
- Rest is one of the ultimate determinants of the rate of progression, arguably the most important one.

## What happens if you don't rest enough
- **Warning:** Training or climbing too much, or when the body says "I'm tired/hurting", risks overreaching, overtraining or injury.
- **Warning:** The body must be at a proper level of energy and health to adapt to training or climbing and make gains. If it is not, the time and energy spent pursuing gains is wasted in nearly all ways: training in that state further fatigues body and mind and lengthens the time until you can benefit from training again. This applies to everything from the central nervous system down to the smallest soft-tissue fibre. When something is tired or hurting, the body is sending a clear sign to back off.
- There are rare exceptions, especially in a rehabilitative setting, but the vast majority of climbers are far better off playing it safe. Climbers often use the rare exception to talk themselves into training or climbing when they shouldn't, and regret it later.

## Small setbacks
- The best case when you train but should have rested: a small step back in strength and energy. Too much volume builds too much fatigue, and the body then needs longer to recover than is ideal for optimal progression.
- **Warning:** This most often happens when you have been feeling your best, are excited about recent progress and feel invincible: after doing the right thing for long enough, you forget why not to do the wrong thing, and stay at the crag or gym for a couple more tries even though you know you're tired. The more attempts you give past peak strength and energy, the further back you are next session and the longer it takes to return to that level of preparedness.

## Why overdoing it sets you back
- Muscles get stronger because training stresses them: it creates growth responses and small microscopic tears in the fibres, which heal and come back stronger and bigger. This healing takes time, even for the smallest tears. Tears have a maximum size: beyond it they become an "injury" and give no strength gain.
- **Warning:** Injuries are not only the severe kind that hurt and need long time off and full rehab. If after a training, climbing or even stretching session where you know you went too hard your next session(s) feel full of energy (or not) but your muscles can't produce as much force or stretch as far, you have likely picked up a small "injury".

### A one-session setback
Finger flexors injured by doing too much; nothing hurt, fingers just felt a bit more tired than normal.

| Stage | What happened |
|---|---|
| Build-up | 8 weeks of dedicated training: one-arm hang on a 22 mm middle edge for 5 s, from bodyweight to +9 kg |
| The mistake | Next session: tried to hang 10 s, added 3 extra sets to failure (not done before), then normal climbing training despite tired fingers |
| After usual 2 days' rest | Could not hang bodyweight for 5 s |
| Next 2 weeks | Continued rest, deloaded volume and intensity; back to +4.5 kg |
| Another 4 weeks | Back to +9 kg for 5 s |
| Total | A six-week setback from one poorly executed session |

## Deciding with a plan, not emotions
- Stay patient and don't let emotions guide the decision. Have a plan and an exit strategy, and stick to it unless new, highly influential information arises; decide only on evidence and facts. Do the research, invest your energy wisely, timing is everything, and know when to get out.
- With a history of pushing yourself too far: write a list of all the mistakes you have made and, immediately below it, a list of all the right things you will do in the future. Refer back to it every session (e.g. as the first page of your training journal) and never make those mistakes again. Why: you will meet the situations you fumbled before again, and without reflecting and setting up guard rails they are much harder to avoid.
