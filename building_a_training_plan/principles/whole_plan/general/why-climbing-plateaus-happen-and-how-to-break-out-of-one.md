# Why Climbing Plateaus Happen and How to Break Out of One

**Type:** Principles, Planning · **Trains:** Technique, General strength · **For:** stalled progress

**TL;DR:** Plateaus come from the law of accommodation and habituation: a constant stimulus gives less and less improvement, and repeated failures stop being noticed. Keep a routine for 4–8 weeks so you adapt, but change at least one variable every cycle (intensity, complexity or volume), and target each key attribute with 2–4 methods. Don't change too many variables or change the stimulus too drastically at once.

## What a plateau is
- After more than 4 to 6 months of climbing, most climbers hit a period with no noticeable progress in climbing strength, skill or performance, even though they keep training/climbing in their normal routine: a plateau. It is better than regression, but frustrating, because the activities that once brought improvement no longer work.

## Why plateaus happen

| Cause | What it is |
|---|---|
| Law of accommodation (also called the principle of diminishing returns) | The body's response to a given stimulus decreases over time when that stimulus stays constant. In skill learning, performing the same task repeatedly leads to reduced improvement as body and mind adapt to the task. |
| Habituation | A psychological and behavioural process in which a person's response to a repeated, benign stimulus decreases over time; a simple form of learning that filters out irrelevant or non-threatening stimuli. |

- Both phenomena, if not actively monitored, can creep in and stall training progress.

## Why consistency matters
- Keep a consistent training routine for multiple weeks (typically 4–8) rather than constantly changing exercises, so body and mind have enough time to adapt and develop ("consistency for proficiency").
- Why consistency works: repeating the same exercises/styles of climbing lets the neuromuscular system adapt, improving coordination and technique; muscles and tendons need consistent, progressively increasing resistance to get stronger, and changing exercises too often prevents them from adapting to the load (hangboarding, pull-up training, power endurance, etc.); a regular routine makes the program easier to stick to; familiarity with exercises reduces cognitive load, so you can focus on intensity and quality instead of learning new movements (as when you reach flow state on a climb after many sessions of working the moves).
- **Warning:** These are the tenets of periodization: training split into cycles/phases that each focus on a single adaptation (endurance, hypertrophy, strength, power). Trouble starts when you stay in one phase too long or don't change the variables.

## How a plateau forms
Without a carefully written program, stagnation comes from two opposite mistakes:

| Mistake | Result | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Not following a routine long enough to get its benefits (e.g. a new routine from social media every other week) | The benefits of the routine are never reached | Follow through an entire training cycle |
| Sticking to the same routine too long (e.g. climbing every day for a year, only board climbing and doing the same pull-up routine) | Neither skill nor strength increases, because of accommodation and habituation, even though the routine brought success in the past | Change the routine (see below) |

- **Warning:** Habituation severely reduces attention to detail: climbers become complacent with the outcomes of their attempts and tolerate failure because they are used to the same shortcomings. Example: short climbers who fall on bigger moves say "I'm just not tall enough" instead of recognising the technical errors that are the real reason they didn't stick the move.
- Climbers can become so habituated that they overlook errors completely and ignore, or stop noticing, valuable feedback that would make the next attempt more effective. Use video analysis to counter this.
- Climbing the same wall angle, routine or style causes boredom (sometimes without you noticing). The lack of novelty decreases motivation and engagement, making climbing/training far less effective and productive.
- Accommodation limits skill transfer: the ability to transfer technique to different contexts (new moves, styles of climbing, terrain, drills, exercises). Training with only a limited set of stimuli dramatically decreases the ability to generalise and apply skills in new and varied situations.
- Diminishing returns: the effectiveness of a constant training regimen decreases over time, so gains in skill or performance slow down. The mind adapts fastest to new stimuli, then slows as it becomes familiar and sees no need for big neurological changes (the reason "newbie gains" exist). To keep improving, increase the intensity, complexity or variation of the training tasks; this applies to both skill and strength training.

## How to avoid a plateau and break out of one
- The key is change: vary training with different exercises, drills or tasks to prevent accommodation and keep learning dynamic. Gradually increase difficulty or change the conditions of practice for consistent progression; the more intentional and calculated you are, the more reliably your abilities and strength increase.
- Target each of your most influential attributes for your goals with 2–4 methods and unique stimuli. Deliver multiple forms of training to the muscles or skill set, and change the intensity, complexity or volume every cycle to stay in a state of continuous learning.
- Some drills/stimuli don't need to change in form (e.g. hangboarding for many cycles in a row), but always change at least one variable to avoid a plateau.
- **Warning:** Don't change too many variables or change the stimulus too drastically: you want to be able to recreate the positive adaptations from the previous cycle. Switching too many drills at once can be overwhelming and cause a stall at the start of a new cycle, because there are too many new things to learn at once, which hurts momentum. Momentum depends on how appropriately you adjust the program and how well you stay within the parameters of your goal.
