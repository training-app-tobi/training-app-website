# From Exercising to Training

**Type:** Principles, Planning · **Trains:** General strength · **For:** stalled progress

**TL;DR:** Doing exercise is not the same as training: training requires systematic and progressed overload through a series of sequential sessions, aimed at a relatively permanent change in physical potential. Pick a couple of exercises to improve, plan them twice a week for about 6 weeks, start a couple of clicks easier than you can currently do and progress session by session. When sessions go flat, lean into the plateau for a few sessions, then cycle back and start a new training phase.

## Exercise is not training

- Doing exercise is not the same as training. You can go hard all week (boulder twice, do a couple of CrossFit classes, run hill intervals, do three hangboard repeater sessions) and still not be training, because training requires systematic and progressed overload through a series of sequential sessions.
- Committing to a series of sessions that progress over time (whether or not each one needs high motivation) matters more than getting motivated for one hard session.
- **Warning:** crushing one very hard session and then not doing anything like it again for weeks: the body tends to respond to such a session as a trauma rather than as a stimulus for growth.
- **Warning:** doing the exact same workout several days a week (same exercises, same order, same load) gives no progress.
- Both of these can have a place, but they miss the key component of training, which is what is needed to improve. Training is a series of progressive sessions aimed at creating a relatively permanent change in physical potential. Chase the adaptation, not your response to it.

## Step one: start too easy

| Element | Rule |
|---|---|
| Plan | Build a session calendar and add a couple of exercises you want to improve (lifts in the weight room, stretches, finger hangs or a type of boulder) |
| Frequency and length | These exercises twice a week for about 6 weeks |
| First session | A couple of clicks easier than you can currently do |
| Goal of the first session | Do the exercises well, let the body know work is coming, and leave room to improve |

| You can currently | Start with |
|---|---|
| 5 pull-ups with 9 kg extra on your waist | Bodyweight for 5 reps |
| Average 20 problems at V5 after warm-up | Back off to an average of V4 |

## Step two: do the training

- The first session should feel simple, as if you have not done enough. Note how each exercise felt, what went well and anything else relevant in the session.
- Chase only a couple of primary changes. Other exercises can be done alongside them, but don't get fixated on progressing everything.

| Session | Progression |
|---|---|
| 1 | Simple, as if you have not done enough |
| 2 | Small increases in the main exercises |
| 3 | Probably working at about the loads where you think you should have started the cycle |
| 4 and 5 | Push harder |

- Rest plenty before your focus sets or problems.
- When chasing higher average grades, your volume of attempts might drop off. When chasing higher numbers, stop when the sets are not clean.
- When you start to hit your limit, add sets:

| Stage | Sets × reps |
|---|---|
| First few sessions | 3 × 5 |
| Then | 4 × 4 (more rest, fewer reps, about the same total volume) |
| Then | 5 × 3 |

## Step three: be flexible

- Sessions 6, 7 and beyond might be really tough. Be willing to take extra rest between sessions and more rest between sets, and accept that sessions may be "flat", with little discernible progress from the previous one. This is OK and becomes part of life as an athlete. The essential part is pushing against the ceiling: you might go up in one lift or get one hard boulder but feel flat otherwise.
- If the intensity is right, almost every exercise you are chasing will eventually go flat for a time. Lean into the plateau for a few sessions, then cycle back and start a new training phase. A plateau means you have started making complex adaptations, not that you are doing something wrong.
- Worthwhile adaptations have to be approached from several angles. Example: a better ability to lock off on a boulder may require building general grip strength, back strength and core strength, then bringing them together. Each must be trained, not just done once.

## What training is

- Training is building a plan to address specific needs, addressing those needs, then stepping back to check whether they are still the critical limiters or whether something else needs attention.
- Some essential things for learning or progress are not easy: they require discipline, stall out or don't feel right, yet knowledge, strength or wisdom are on the other side. Difficulty is no reason to bail out; it is a reason to assess your limiters and decide whether you like where you are or whether what you want is on the other side of training.
