# Energy Systems in Climbing

**Type:** Principles · **Trains:** Endurance, Power endurance

**TL;DR:** The anaerobic and aerobic energy systems work together, not in sequence, but high intensity makes the anaerobic systems dominate and low intensity lets the aerobic system contribute more, so intensity and duration guide energy system training. Near-limit climbing relies on the anaerobic systems and is "on a timer". A well-developed aerobic system, with dense capillaries, replenishes the anaerobic systems during the short relaxations and rests of climbing, so you recover more quickly at rests and recover at poorer rests.

## Why energy systems matter
- Energy system training, along with finger strength development, forms the backbone of forearm physiology.
- In climbing the limiting muscle is often, though not always, the forearms, so energy system profiling and training for climbing usually focus on the forearms (e.g. using a fingerboard).

## ATP: the building block
- Whatever the hold (a crux crimp or a resting jug), a molecule called ATP provides the energy to contract the muscles. Only a small amount of ATP can be stored in the muscle, so cells produce ATP on a supply-and-demand basis to meet the demand of increased activity.
- There are 3 different pathways that produce ATP, at different rates. This explains the relationship between intensity and duration: extremely intense exercise can't be sustained for long, while moderate or low intensity exercise can be done for much longer.

## The energy systems
- Two pathways make up the energy systems: the anaerobic and aerobic pathways. The anaerobic system is made up of two pathways: the alactic and lactic pathways. The anaerobic systems produce ATP without the use of oxygen.

| | Anaerobic (alactic and lactic) | Aerobic |
|---|---|---|
| Steps | Fewer steps/reactions | Many steps |
| Rate of ATP production | Faster | Slower |
| Capacity | No capacity to produce very large amounts of ATP in a sustainable way | Capacity to produce large amounts of ATP in a sustainable way |
| Where | Cytoplasm | Mitochondria |

- The energy systems don't work in isolation or in sequence for set amounts of time with a "switch" between them. They work together in a coordinated response to an increased energy demand in the muscle. This matters especially in climbing, an intermittent, varied-intensity sport (not an all-out sprint).
- This does **not** mean they contribute equally at different intensities and durations. Different systems dominate at certain intensities, so intensity and duration are used to guide energy system training.

| Training intensity | Dominant system | Why |
|---|---|---|
| High | Anaerobic systems | Forces the muscles to produce ATP quickly |
| Low | Aerobic system contributes more | A slower rate of ATP production still meets the demand. Aerobic ATP production is more sustainable (no build-up of metabolites that reduce muscle function), so the exercise can be sustained for longer |

## How energy systems play out in climbing
- When exercise starts, the muscles produce ATP to maintain homeostasis. If ATP can't be supplied at the rate the intensity needs, performance drops. In cycling or running this means slowing down; in climbing it generally means falling off, because the challenge level is fixed and you can't "slow down" (like running on a treadmill set at a fast speed until you can't keep up and fly off).
- Much climbing is near your limit, so the anaerobic systems play a large role in ATP production. That is why in most climbing you are "on a timer" and can't sustain the level for long. At an intensity that needs fast anaerobic ATP production, failure (inability to sustain the force) is inevitable.

## What limits the anaerobic pathways
- Two limits: "not enough fuel" and "too many metabolites" (byproducts of the reactions).
- Not enough fuel: as PCr or glucose/glycogen declines, the rate of anaerobic ATP production drops.
- Too many metabolites: lactate was historically blamed for muscle fatigue in high-intensity exercise. The current view is that inorganic phosphate (the high-energy phosphate cleaved from ATP to form ADP and energy) is responsible for the reduced force over time when using the anaerobic systems.
- Energy system contribution is a spectrum across intensities and durations, with different "feelings". "Power out" and "pump" generally indicate that the anaerobic systems are contributing to ATP production (to varying extents).

## The intermittent nature of climbing
- Forearm contractions in climbing are intermittent: you relax to release the grip and move between holds. During relaxation, full blood flow reaches the forearm muscles, delivering oxygen and removing the metabolites from anaerobic ATP production that would impede muscle function if they accumulated.
- Metabolites can be shifted into the mitochondria, and aerobic ATP is used to replenish the anaerobic systems (resynthesis of PCr). This is how a well-developed aerobic system complements the anaerobic systems: they can be replenished more readily during rest.
- Oxygen delivery and metabolite removal rely on a dense system of capillaries. Training the aerobic system is not only about the processes inside the cell; what happens outside the cell (capillarisation) is also very important for using the energy systems in climbing. A higher degree of capillarisation improves the ability to deliver oxygen to the muscle and remove metabolites.
- The recovery intervals of climbing are mirrored in repeater-style training protocols. These rest periods may seem short but can strongly influence performance, partly because PCr resynthesis during rest is nonlinear: very fast at first, slowing over time.

## Water tank analogy
- "Anaerobic capacity" = the amount of work you can do above your aerobic level. "Aerobic capacity" = the ability to produce ATP and buffer anaerobic byproducts.

| Part of the tank | Represents | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Water flowing out of the bottom | Force produced | More flow = higher force = harder climbing |
| Water level in the tank | Anaerobic capacity | Dictates how much work you can do above a certain intensity; a high outflow rapidly lowers it; it can be replenished at rest or relative rest |
| Water flowing in | Aerobic capacity | The greater the inflow, the better developed the aerobic system and the greater the force (level of climbing) you can sustain without depleting anaerobic capacity |

| Scenario | What happens | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Water out = water in | Climbing at a level your aerobic capacity can sustain. Some anaerobic capacity may still be used, but the aerobic system replenishes it, so there is no **net** accumulation of metabolites. Lower intensity puts you well within aerobic ability; higher intensity means using anaerobic capacity. It still gets tiring after a long duration, but there is no imminent "pump" or "power out". You walk away from training at this intensity feeling relatively fresh | Vertical climbing around 5a for an 8a climber; 30% repeaters where no pump builds during the session and the final reps feel about as hard as the first |
| Water out > water in | The intensity requires relying on anaerobic capacity. The tank drops at a rate matching the intensity: the harder the climbing, the faster the outflow, the faster the depletion and the less time you can sustain the force | This broad category spans the "strength", "anaerobic capacity" and "aerobic power" training scenarios seen in climbing, because all have a significant anaerobic contribution |
| Reaching a rest where water out < water in, after depleting the tank | After a sequence that used anaerobic capacity, there is less water in the tank. At a rest that lets you exert a low enough force, the outflow slows; once it is slower than the inflow, you recover and replenish anaerobic capacity | — |

- Further developing the aerobic system lets you recover more quickly at rests, and recover at poorer rests.
