# Only Green Days: Train Only When You Can Adapt

**Type:** Principles, Planning · **Trains:** Recovery · **For:** stalled progress

**TL;DR:** Progress comes from adaptation, not from recovering back to baseline: after full recovery, the body needs extra time to adapt and rise above baseline. Aim for "green days": sessions started without significant fatigue that leave you better by the next session. When you are under-recovered, don't train, add recovery time or change the stimulus; the athlete with the best ratio of green days to other days improves the most.

## Goal of training
- The goal is to improve steadily over time. The best athletes keep a steady, steep upward trajectory, avoid setbacks, and have training days where they see progress in ability and strength.
- Progress comes from adaptation. Factors behind it:
  - appropriate volume of training across all time periods (daily, weekly, etc.)
  - appropriate scheduling
  - appropriate mindsets around training
  - discipline and/or natural tendencies to make better decisions

## What a green day is
- A green day is a completed session that results in adaptation and improvement of skills and strength, setting the athlete up to reach new peaks by the next training day(s).
- On a green day the athlete starts with no significant fatigue, at a current peak of performance or trainability, and trains effectively, giving the body a stimulus that yields the desired adaptations. The green day is confirmed only when the next climbing session is done.
- **Warning:** Choosing what and how much to train, and when to stop, is the hard part: the perfect amount of the wrong type of training gives no improvement; too much of the right training gives no improvement and most likely regression.

## Recovery vs adaptation
- During a session, as energy is spent and fatigue sets in, maximal performance (strength, power, endurance, coordination) drops. The greater the fatigue, damage or load on body, brain and energy systems, the longer the recovery needed to get back to baseline.
- You don't train to get back to baseline; you train to improve, and improvement only happens when you adapt to a new stimulus. The body needs more time than recovery alone to rise above baseline: after full recovery, allow extra time to adapt.

| | What happens |
|---|---|
| Recovery | Biological resources depleted by training return to resting values |
| Adaptation | Recovery beyond repair or refuelling: growth or supercompensation |

## Skin as a model
- Skin behaves like muscles and other soft tissue.
- **Warning:** Climb until the skin rips, splits or bleeds, and it only recovers back to baseline; it does not come back stronger, because the damage was too great (an "injury").
- Climb until the skin is slightly irritated, then stop: it recovers and adapts. Keep irritating it slightly with adequate recovery and adaptation time, and it keeps coming back stronger.
- **Warning:** Stronger skin can still be ripped by an even more intense move; the split then heals back to the original fragile skin, which has to be trained up again.

## Lifestyle after the session
- **Warning:** A well-executed green day can be spoiled by lifestyle choices during the recovery/adaptation window: a bad night's sleep or alcoholic drinks after an excellent session can turn it into a yellow, orange or even red day. Many other things can also hurt recovery.

## Day colours

| Day | Session outcome |
|---|---|
| Green | Increase in performance or potential |
| Yellow | Maintained: no increase or decrease |
| Orange | Minor decrease in potential, carrying slight fatigue; can possibly become yellow or even green by lengthening the recovery/adaptation period |
| Red | Significant decrease in potential, carrying significant fatigue; needs a long break and/or deload over several days or weeks to rebalance fatigue |
| Black | Injury during a session, followed by a long period of lowered performance or potential, with specific desired training protocols contraindicated |

- Purpose of the colour scheme: to visualise performance and potential trajectory, help planning, and build awareness and autonomy in regulating inputs to get consistent positive outputs.

## Long-term training
- Long-term improvement or regression is the sum of short-term outcomes, so make every session as productive and "green" as possible. First rule: avoid losing ground.
- If you know you are under-recovered and expect the next session won't improve performance: don't train or climb, add recovery time, or change the stimulus. Green days are only possible when fully recovered and adapted.
- Strict training schedules with fixed recovery windows (12 hours, 24 hours, 48 hours, etc.) are an overly simplified way to manage the body's many variables.
- If on a repeating day-on, day-off schedule your session performance feels unpredictable or has many lower-performance days, you most likely would benefit from extra rest days. Don't go to the gym regardless of what your body says just because frequent climbing is "how you improve".
- Over time, the athlete with the best ratio of green days to other days will on average improve the most.

## Day scores

| Day | Score |
|---|---|
| Green | +1 to +5 (by quality) |
| Yellow | 0 |
| Orange | 0 to −2 (by quality) |
| Red | −3 to −5 (by quality) |
| Black | −10 to −100 (by injury severity) |

- The scores are not based on any mathematical evidence or model; they are a simple reference for the sum of day types over the short and long term.
- Example: an athlete who trains once per week but gains 0.01 points each week improves more than one who trains four times per week with a mix of positive and negative days that sums to a negative number over the training period.
- When tired, don't train anyway hoping for a miraculously good session against your instincts. Go for a walk and sleep well instead, avoid the setback, and have a green day tomorrow.
- If you repeatedly let the desire for greatness override good training decisions, reach out to a coach or set up an accountability system.
- Applying the concept has helped climbers break out of multi-year plateaus, avoid injuries and reach their goals; for a constant over-trainer it was the biggest reason for a jump in performance.
