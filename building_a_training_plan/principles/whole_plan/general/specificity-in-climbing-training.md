# Specificity in Climbing Training

**Type:** Principles, Planning · **Trains:** Technique, Power, Endurance

**TL;DR:** Specificity means training that simulates your goal: similar movement (motor specificity) and the same energy systems (metabolic specificity). Train on terrain that resembles your goal in wall angle and hold size, and mimic its loads, durations and rest periods. What counts is the adaptation, not the exercise: identify the adaptations you need, then choose exercises, intensity, duration and frequency. Start general and gradually increase specificity over time.

## Who should do what
| Situation / level | Do this |
|---|---|
| Project near home with a long season | Climbing on the project as often as possible might (only might) be a reasonable strategy |

- **Warning:** otherwise, avoid the endless siege approach to projects: it ill-serves most climbers.

## What specificity is
- Climbing is very diverse: a three-move boulder problem demands something very different from a 20-hour alpine ridge traverse, so specificity should be a pillar of training.
- At its most basic, specificity means training that simulates the sport.

| Category | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Motor specificity | Training movement similar to the performance event |
| Metabolic specificity | Training the same energy systems as the performance event |

## Motor specificity
- Example: if your project has static moves on a horizontal roof, slabby coordination problems in the bouldering gym are not very specific.
- The biggest factors for motor specificity are hold size and type and wall angle, because they determine the nature of the movement.
- Make training more specific by training on terrain (indoors or out) that resembles your goal in wall angle and hold size.
- This is surprisingly hard to do: plastic and wooden footholds rarely resemble real rock; many hold types, such as sharp crimps, are rarely found in modern gyms; a training board can be far steeper than your actual projects.

## Metabolic specificity
- Metabolic specificity is harder to grasp: nearly every climb uses a combination of energy systems, the differences are subtle (did you fall because you were powered down or pumped?), and the terminology is confusing (power endurance, intensive endurance, anaerobic alactic).
- Standard approach: mimic in training the loads, durations and rest periods of your actual performance goal.

## Adaptations, not exercises
- "Exercises are not specific, adaptations are": what matters is the changes your body makes in response to training, not the training itself.
- Doing random exercises and workouts, even ones that seem highly specific, is not the best path to higher performance over time.

| Step | What to do |
|---|---|
| 1 | Identify the adaptations you need |
| 2 | Choose the right exercises and the proper intensity and duration |
| 3 | Get the frequency right, because the adaptations don't happen immediately |

## When to get specific
- **Warning:** Sieging a project has risks: periods without progress lead to frustration and mental hurdles; for others, the repetitive stresses are a quick path to injury or burnout.
- A better approach may be to start general and gradually increase the specificity of training over time.
- Example: a project with long pulls between monos. Adaptations needed: pulling muscle recruitment, plus coordination and tendon stiffness in the middle fingers.

| Stage | Training |
|---|---|
| General | One-arm rows and hangboarding on monos |
| Specific application | Spray-wall boulder problems with pulls between monos |
| Most specific | Time on the project itself, or a replica set by the gym's setters |

## Keep it simple
- Most of specificity comes down to mimicking the characteristics of your climbing goal in training as closely as possible.
- Start with angle, hold type and number of moves, the biggest factors; then don't limit yourself to those, and get creative.
