# General, Specific and Simulation Training

**Type:** Principles, Planning · **Trains:** General strength, Technique

**TL;DR:** General training is the fastest way to develop overall energy systems and strength, and the quality of all later training depends on it. Specific training uses the sport's movement patterns or muscle groups (movement specificity) and its loads and durations (metabolic specificity); simulation exactly mimics a route or competition setup. Build the general base first: simulation needs specific fitness, is ramped up over 3–4 weeks and is used sparingly.

## Who should do what
| Situation / level | Do this |
|---|---|
| Fit enough to simulate safely and effectively | Simulation can be added |
| Not yet that fit | No simulation yet: build the general and specific base first |

## General vs specific training
- General training makes us better as athletes and is the fastest way to develop overall energy systems and strength. Step back from only bouldering and develop back, arm and leg strength in the gym; climbers resist this because they have seen the value of specific training.
- Moving from general to specific training is a transition, not a switch. Some general exercises are somewhat specific to climbing (e.g. inverted rows), so they can appear in both general and specific training.

## What specific training is (and isn't)
| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Performance | Doing the sport itself |
| Simulation | Exactly mimicking a route or a competition setup |
| Specific training | Using the same movement patterns or muscle groups as the sport (movement specificity) and the same loads and durations (metabolic specificity) |

- Specific training is neither performance nor simulation.

| Example | Movement-specific to route climbing | Metabolically specific to route climbing |
|---|---|---|
| Bouldering | Yes | No: durations too short |
| Treadwall | Falls short: easy footwork, fixed plane of movement | Can be close |

- You can do decent specific training in the gym even when you can't actually climb: make sure each exercise checks the box on movement or on metabolism.

## Ten facets of specificity
| Facet | What it means for climbing |
|---|---|
| Type of muscular contraction | Climbing is not lifting's concentric-eccentric rhythm at a roughly 2-second tempo. Climbing is mostly a concentric movement, then a few seconds of isometric hold, then a totally unloaded, quick eccentric as you reach for the next hold. |
| Movement pattern | Indoor bouldering is likely not like route climbing or outdoor bouldering. Almost all indoor climbing defocuses core tension and footwork and does a poor job of simulating subtleties in wall angle. Ranking for movement specificity: bouldering wall > system wall > campus board > hangboard. |
| Region and range of movement | Are your reaches as long indoors as outdoors; are you using the same muscle groups; are you doing full range on exercises where you need full range; are you holding isometrics in the right positions? |
| Velocity of movement | Campusing to build power is markedly slower than a dynamic bouldering move: the unloaded arm moves very quickly to the next rung, but the loaded movement is slow. Likewise, indoor bouldering between big holds is fast, but that speed doesn't carry over to slopers on real rock (e.g. Fontainebleau). |
| Force of contraction | Pull as hard in training as in the sport, and use as much tension as your goal problems require. People are usually less fired up for training than for performance, and it shows in the lower training loads they choose. |
| Muscle fiber recruitment | If intensity, duration and load in training are not right, you use different muscle fibers than in performance. This is essential to get right in the gym, and one of the main reasons indoor bouldering sessions don't translate to real rock. |
| Metabolism | Metabolism is very specific to the intensity and duration of the event. |
| Biochemical adaptation | Enzymatic adaptation, related to metabolism: repetitive cyclic leg endurance (running, cycling) produces a profoundly different "endurance" adaptation than the stop-and-go, high local muscular endurance that climbing requires. |
| Flexibility | Getting more flexible in one movement doesn't translate to function in the performance environment. Stretching done only on a 13 mm mat on a heated floor may not apply directly to moving across a roof boulder problem. |
| Fatigue | The effectiveness of a training program and the type of fitness it produces depend on the specificity of the fatigue it causes. Being exhausted after a gym session means little when you're trying to be good on pitch fifteen. Training must account for how fatigue is produced in the performance environment; this facet matters a great deal. |

- **Warning:** excessive development of one type of fitness may have a profoundly detrimental effect on another.

## General, specific and simulation as stages
| Stage | Sword-making analogy | Role |
|---|---|---|
| General training | Forging the sword | The bulk of the work and where most development happens |
| Specific training | Honing and sharpening, once the blade has its shape | Done over and over, better each time |
| Simulation | The finest sharpening step | What makes a great sword better than a good one |

- The quality of specific and simulation training depends fully on how well the general base was built.
- How to lay the three stages out across a year is in the note A Year of General, Specific and Simulation Training.

## Simulation
- Simulation works so well it can almost be considered cheating: the body learns to use a particular hold type, adapt to a climbing angle or use a given technique. But the learning is limited to what you simulate.
- The better bet for most climbers most of the time is to get better at lots of hard bouldering movements, or get all fingers stronger.
- **Warning:** do simulation only once you're pretty fit and have the specific fitness needed to avoid injury and to execute an effective simulation session.
- When ramping up to simulation, think through all the facets of specificity, then get micro-detailed.
- Replicating a crux: take aluminum foil impressions of the pockets and edges to build molds of the holds, measure the wall angle and the distances between holds, build the cruxes at home, train on them, then return to the route (in the case described, the climber sent).
- Match the crux as closely as possible: wall angle, hold size, the shoes you will wear, how fatigued you are on reaching the crux, how long since you last could chalk, the same clothes, tape or kneepads.

| Step | What to do |
|---|---|
| Sessions | Do simulations in sets, rest a lot, take notes |
| Between sessions | Adjust and test |
| Ramp-up | 3–4 weeks of progressively more focused efforts |
| After the ramp-up | Give it a rest, whether you send or not |
| Frequency | Use this tactic sparingly |
