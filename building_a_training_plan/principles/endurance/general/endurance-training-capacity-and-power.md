# Endurance Training: Capacity and Power

**Type:** Principles, Planning · **Trains:** Endurance, Power endurance, Power

**TL;DR:** Endurance is the ability to display power or strength over time, and it is extremely specific: train the endurance your climbing needs, not "good endurance" across the board. It has two dimensions, capacity (handling a given volume of work) and power (output: how intense an effort you can sustain for a given duration), giving four qualities: aerobic capacity, aerobic power, anaerobic capacity and anaerobic power. Build endurance by focusing hard on your capacities for 6–8 weeks or on your powers for 3–4 weeks, planned as a progressive series of 5–7 sessions rather than one hard session.

## Who should do what

| Situation / goal | Do this |
|---|---|
| You pump out on juggy steep walls | Spending time there is more useful than a spinning class |
| To build endurance through capacity | Focus hard on building your capacities for 6–8 weeks |
| To build endurance through power | Focus on building your powers for 3–4 weeks |

## Key lessons

- Endurance is the ability to display power or strength over time. It can be continuous and high intensity, or intermittent. It shows up in many ways in climbing; simply working up a sweat is not the same as being able to beat the pump.
- Endurance must be understood in terms of specific needs. Overall "good endurance" across the board doesn't exist and should not be pursued. Endurance is extremely specific, so start from the information your body gives you.
- **Warning:** endurance is "expensive" to build and maintain and can compete with power, strength, or simply the ability to recover. These abilities can't all be maximized at once, so don't pursue that goal.
- Endurance has two big dimensions that most people ignore: capacity and power. Power here means output, not explosiveness.
  - Capacity = being able to handle a given volume of work (e.g. climbing 45 boulder problems in one hour). Capacity lets you replenish energy stores quickly.
  - Power (anaerobic or aerobic) = how intense an effort you can sustain for a given duration. Power lets you use energy stores effectively.
- General capacity training ("getting some exercise") is generally more useful than non-climbing high-intensity endurance training, such as sprint intervals with the legs or the SkiErg. Intense endurance training takes a lot of energy and should be specific.
- All endurance training is really speed or power training: the aim is not to go forever, but to go as fast as possible (e.g. a speed record on El Cap) or to do slightly harder moves over the same time or distance (e.g. the 13a next to the 12d you just sent). Just going to the end of your possible stamina is not very useful for route climbers and boulderers.
- Most people need better "cardio", but for general fitness and avoiding cardiorespiratory disease, not for climbing performance. Athletes who need high muscular endurance, such as short-distance speed skaters, show only small increases in VO2 max (5–10%) with high levels of training, but massive increases in local blood supply (50–250%). In some cases, better endurance performance comes with a decrease in VO2.

## Strength versus endurance

- Going from no pull-up to one proper pull-up relies on strength: the body's ability to create force, doing hard things for a short time. Going from one pull-up to multiple pull-ups is endurance: the ability to keep doing a task over and over.
- Endurance can have different goals, e.g. with pull-ups: several in a row; a set high number (such as 20) with extra weight on a weight belt; many within a day but not all in one set; or pull-ups several days in a row. All of these can be trained; one workout may improve more than one goal, and people with enough natural endurance might improve at all of them.
- **Warning:** the common mistake is thinking all parts of endurance are the same. Working on one part of endurance can make a different part harder.

## System power and system capacity

- The body supplies energy through three systems, like the gears of a car: System 1 (anaerobic alactic, first gear) for a few seconds, System 2 (anaerobic lactic, second gear) for about a minute, and System 3 (aerobic, cruising) for a long time; see the note The Three Energy Systems as the Gears of a Car.
- **System Power:** going as hard as you can for a given time depends on the amount of energy you can produce and use for one effort (e.g. how much weight you lift in one set, how fast you run one lap of the track, how hard a climb you do for one pitch). In single efforts (one climb, a 5k) the aim is the perfect amount of effort, often as close to your hardest as possible; going slow is easy, going fast shows what you're capable of. Each system has a power limit and each can be improved by training. This power is output, not explosiveness. In the car picture: the horsepower of the motor.
- **System Capacity:** how long you can hold top speed before the engine blows, how much gas is in the tank. You can build a more powerful engine (System Power) or a bigger gas tank (System Capacity).
- Each system has its own power and capacity, but System 1 has so little potential for capacity that it is hard to improve, and its power is best seen as explosiveness (sprinting, jumping). So Systems 1 and 2 are grouped together as "anaerobic", giving four qualities:

| | Capacity | Power |
|---|---|---|
| Aerobic (System 3) | Aerobic capacity | Aerobic power |
| Anaerobic (Systems 1 and 2) | Anaerobic capacity | Anaerobic power |

## Four ways to progress a boulder session

- Looking only at power output (the energy used to make movement happen), there are 4 ways to change the training effect of a boulder session that starts with one climb.

| Change | Quality trained | How to train it | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Do more problems | Capacity | Simply do more climbing, hiking or similar at an easy level, counting moves, minutes or pitches without worrying about difficulty. The main goal is just more | You get tired toward the end of a session; capacity is going from 20 to 25 problems per session. More fun, more chances to practise and progress skills, and eventually more good tries per day at the crag |
| Add more moves to a problem (same difficulty throughout, e.g. from 10-move to 12-move problems) | Aerobic Power (what climbers often call power endurance) | Hard to train: do more efforts or reps at the same intensity. Unlike capacity (where easier moves or exercises are fine), the difficulty must stay the same | Example: bodyweight pull-ups going from 10 to 12 to 15 reps need more system power (aerobic and anaerobic) but neither more strength (bodyweight doesn't change) nor more capacity (it's one set) |
| Do harder moves | Anaerobic Power (first gear: more energy for hard moves for a few seconds) | Do harder moves | Doesn't directly affect endurance, but being able to do harder moves means working less on submaximal ones: going from climbing V4 moves first try to V8 moves first try makes V4 feel easy, so you can relax on those moves, which indirectly increases endurance. Over a climbing career, this is the single most important factor in changing the grades you can climb |
| Decrease rest between problems | Anaerobic Capacity: the ability to switch on and off between medium-hard efforts | One boulder per minute for 20–30 min (best shows how this quality feels). Another option: 4 boulders in a row with no rest, rest 5 min, repeat | Can increase short-route or long-boulder endurance quite a bit |

- **Warning:** anaerobic capacity workouts are hard and hard to recover from, and the fitness doesn't last as long as you'd like.

## How to build endurance

- The most important step is committing to the process of endurance training and following through. One workout is a trauma; eight done progressively are training. Keeping focused on one aspect gives far more progress.

## The energy equation

- Endurance is power output over time. Improving it involves more than making the muscles better: skills and technical ability, movement quality, motor control, autonomic function, and the energy systems.
- Endurance improves both by improving energy production (eating better, training better, improving the quality of individual muscles) and by reducing energy expenditure. For many climbers available energy isn't the limiter; using it efficiently is. To be more efficient ("thrifty"), keep working on movement capacity, skills and the mental game. Training is only part of the equation.

## Planning progressions

- Whatever you choose, plan endurance work as a series of sessions, not one hard session: plan 5–7 sessions in advance, perhaps 2 sessions per week. Decide how each workout will get harder with respect to the four ways to progress (more problems, more moves, harder moves, less rest), and do just a bit more each time.
- **Warning:** don't build a whole month of training on a spreadsheet and then get frustrated when your body or your schedule can't keep up. Don't worry if sessions don't happen on the planned days: what matters is staying with endurance work long enough for it to change your body.
