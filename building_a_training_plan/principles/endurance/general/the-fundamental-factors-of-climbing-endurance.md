# The Fundamental Factors of Climbing Endurance

**Type:** Principles · **Trains:** Endurance, Power endurance, Finger strength, General strength

**TL;DR:** Climbing endurance depends on physiological factors (local endurance, general endurance, thermoregulation, nervous-system and muscle efficiency) and psychological ones (motivation and pain tolerance). Strength is the foundation: maintain finger strength and power year-round, test strength once per month, and build capillary density in the higher-strength fibres with progressively higher-volume efforts over 12+ weeks. Anaerobic training tops out quickly (4–6 hard sessions of a type, no "power endurance" program longer than a month), and the biggest gain is being better adapted to avoid getting pumped, while still accepting the pain needed to peak.

## Who should do what

| Situation / level | Do this |
|---|---|
| Endurance phase | Strength numbers (tested once per month) should be maintained; keep minimal levels of finger strength and power maintenance |
| Strength phase | Strength numbers (tested once per month) should go up |
| Exclusively indoor boulderer | Consider long hikes 1–2x per week |
| Alpinist | Consider heavy resistance training |
| Big mountains or speed climbing on big walls | Developing the heart's stroke volume is nice to have |
| As you age | Try to take on and master one new sports movement per year: not to quit climbing, but to keep learning new movements that keep you in the thoughtful/exciting/frustrating zone and keep motor-learning potential high |
| Not feeling it (drive doesn't match the training) | Don't start: come back tomorrow, next week or next phase when your drive matches the training |

## What endurance means in climbing

| Objective | What it means |
|---|---|
| 1 | Sustaining a given level of power output while moving up the wall |
| 2 | Recovering at stances along the route |
| 3 | Recovering between efforts, such as resting between pitches |
| 4 | Doing a lot of climbing in a single day or several days in a row |

- Key questions for training: can all four goals be trained concurrently, are they improved by the same types of exercise, and, most importantly, can endurance be improved without diminishing power or strength?
- Endurance is the ability to sustain a given power output for a prescribed duration.

| Form | Power output | Duration |
|---|---|---|
| Power endurance | High | Relatively short periods |
| Pure endurance or stamina | Fairly low | Greater durations |

- Aerobic and anaerobic endurance are two markedly different types of endurance, and each can work on a global (whole-body) or local level.
- **Warning:** developing one energy system can cause a decline in the other, and better local endurance won't necessarily mean better whole-body endurance, and vice versa.
- **Warning:** a training plan based on replicating how you feel when tired is almost never the most useful program.

## Local endurance: strength of a muscle group

- Local endurance is the ability of a muscle or group of muscles to perform a given level of work under fatigue, usually for a fixed amount of time. It depends on three factors: strength, energy stores and capillary density of that muscle group.
- The stronger a muscle group (for climbers, the forearms and fingers), the less muscle is needed to generate a given force, so the move-for-move load on the muscle is lower. A very strong-fingered climber uses only a small percentage of grip capability on any given move, so the muscles stay fresh, easily supplied with blood and ready for the next hold. Train for stronger fingers as a basis for better endurance.
- **Warning:** finger strength is foundational to endurance and a necessary part of every climber's endurance training. Don't build strength in the off-season and then let it drift away during endurance work: maintain strength year-round with minimal levels of finger strength and power maintenance, even in an endurance phase.

## Local endurance: capillary density

- Climbers who have climbed for any length of time probably already have quite high forearm capillary density. ARC (aerobic restoration and capillarity) training was adopted to increase bloodflow to the forearms.
- **Warning:** capillarity is specific to the muscle fibres those capillaries supply: low-intensity climbing increases bloodflow to the low-strength muscle fibres, and there is a good possibility this doesn't help when efforts get difficult.
- A safer bet: increase bloodflow to the higher-strength muscle fibres by using them in progressively higher-volume efforts, i.e. interval-style efforts or longer sessions, adding demand over a period of 12+ weeks. In strength athletes, capillary density has been shown to increase with added sets at a fixed load, but only after 3–4 months of training.

| Option | Session for raising capillary density |
|---|---|
| 1 | Density bouldering sessions |
| 2 | High-volume alactic intervals |
| 3 | On-the-minute bouldering sessions |

## General endurance: strength of all muscles

- "If you're not strong enough to hold on, there is nothing to endure." Gaining strength means recruiting a smaller percentage of muscle mass at any given hold size. Climbers with very strong fingers don't grip as hard, so blood keeps flowing and they don't get pumped. This applies to all muscle groups, so any climbing-specific strength training lays a foundation for endurance.
- Test strength once per month. In endurance phases the numbers should be maintained; in strength phases they should go up. If strength drops, so does the high-threshold endurance potential of that muscle. Strength training is easy to implement and has many (especially long-term) benefits, so do it all the time.

## General endurance: energy stores

- Global energy storage: besides muscle glycogen, the liver stores the next "layer" of energy, and the body stores large amounts of energy as fat. Both liver glycogen and body fat can fuel movement, but the process takes slightly longer and needs full-body metabolic transport. The goal is not to increase body fat but to maximise the availability of liver-stored fuel and fat.
- Science is conflicted on the best ways to increase fuel mobilisation, but maintaining a steady, low heart rate all day is a bad idea: if you don't move and work through different effort zones, metabolism becomes restricted and quick access to liver glycogen can decline. To use fuel well you must use fuel: exercise daily, with weekly variation in the duration and intensity of that exercise.
- Exclusively indoor boulderers: consider long hikes 1–2x per week. Alpinists: consider heavy resistance training. In general, look at the duration and intensity of what you do and add what's missing; this helps fuel storage and other areas of endurance.

## General endurance: cardiorespiratory factors (aerobic)

- **Warning:** endurance training can be a trap: the fallacy is thinking that reaching the feelings of the limit of endurance (breathing hard, sweating, a little nauseous) by any means will improve your ability to handle any endurance situation.

| Factor | What it is | How it is trained | Value for climbers |
|---|---|---|---|
| Respiratory function | How much air you take in and how well oxygen transfers to the blood | Trainable by general means | Of limited value for rock climbers and boulderers. About 10% above average respiratory function is probably more than enough for hard climbing |
| Stroke volume of the heart | How much blood the heart pumps per beat | Well trained by general means | Not very important for rock climbing, but nice to develop for big mountains or speed climbing on big walls |
| Ability of the blood to transport oxygen | Raising heart rate and body temperature increases oxygen transport and binding to haemoglobin | Improved by everything from walking to sprinting to weight training; some research suggests athletes who train at multiple intensities and volumes (as climbers do) are best adapted | Improved by the mix of intensities and volumes climbers already train |
| Capillarisation of muscles | The more blood reaches the forearm, the longer you hang on | Lots of pitches at low intensity, lots of mileage on boulders, and slow, steady climbing all work; nonspecific means such as big system board or weight training sessions also add up | Probably the most important endurance factor for climbers. Avoid "dumb climbing" with only the clock in mind; if possible, build mileage on real rock at low grades |

## General endurance: homeostatic factors (anaerobic)

- Anaerobic work involves essential pain and is also a trap to some degree. The biggest endurance gain for climbers is probably not tolerating anaerobic situations better but being better adapted to avoid them. Anaerobic function still needs to be optimised.

| Factor | How to train it | How long |
|---|---|---|
| Buffering capacity of the blood | Fill the forearms with as much blood as possible, hold it as long as you can, rest and repeat (intervals, 4x4s, etc.) | Training tops out quickly: 4–6 hard sessions of any given type probably give 95% of what you need |
| Ability to tolerate high acidity | Closely related to buffering; best trained at slightly lower intensity and slightly longer duration, e.g. the minute-on, minute-off foot-on campus ladder workout | Do it for 3–4 weeks, then go back to focusing on more power |

- **Warning:** beware any "power endurance" program that lasts longer than a month.

## Efficiency of thermoregulation

| Process | How it works |
|---|---|
| Evaporation | Sweat pushing heat out as warm liquid |
| Radiation | Heat transfer by electromagnetic waves |
| Conduction | Physical contact, e.g. lying on cool concrete after an intense interval session |
| Convection | Air movement, faster with movement like walking or light jogging |

- The better you control body temperature, the better your endurance: the same routes feel much easier in cool conditions than in hot, humid ones.
- To thermoregulate on endurance routes: dress "too light" for the pitch so you are at the right temperature higher up; drink plenty of fluids (water is essential for internal cooling and sweating); wear shorts rather than long pants to get cool air on the skin.
- Thermoregulation is hard to change over time; manage it intelligently day to day.

## Efficiency of the nervous system

- Humans are wired for both gross and fine motor control and have become better at fine motor tasks (painting, writing) while slipping in gross motor ability: you are probably better adapted to typing than to having very strong fingers; pound for pound humans are the weakest of the mammals.
- Improve nervous-system efficiency by training specific to the task: climbing is the ultimate expression of efficient movement, but learning other tasks (weight room, gymnastic rings, parkour, new-style bouldering) also helps nervous-system development.
- As you age, try to take on and master one new sports movement per year. Not to quit climbing, but to keep learning new movements that keep you in the thoughtful/exciting/frustrating zone and keep motor-learning potential high.

## Efficiency of the muscles

- **Warning:** possibly the simplest way to semi-permanent endurance is to improve muscle efficiency by training muscular endurance capacity over a prolonged cycle while backing off all other training. The downside: you lose strength and power (you can hang on forever but may lack the finger strength for the next moves).
- Muscular efficiency comprises the ability to bring in and push out energy via the blood, convert oxygen and glycogen into usable fuel, and process waste quickly. All are trained the same way: by creating an environment that stresses the system.

## Psychological factors

- Motivation is key: have clear goals when you start a training program. Endurance comes steadily, piece by piece, and has to be worked on every session. Endurance training is by definition fatiguing and makes you suffer (strength training can leave you fresh, power training can be fun), so your heart must be in it. If you're not feeling it, don't start: come back tomorrow, next week or next phase when your drive matches the training.
- Pain tolerance: endurance is better developed by avoiding getting pumped than by the classic method of getting pumped silly. But even with an aerobic-alactic program you still have to peak your endurance, and that means pain. Accept that it will hurt and that dealing with pain is a controllable skill (how pumped can you be and still hang on, how much suffering will you take to stay on the rock). Training pain tolerance is essential for long-term success.
- Understand the whole picture of endurance, not just "input x gives result y": a purely black-box approach leaves you lost when your schedule or training environment changes.
