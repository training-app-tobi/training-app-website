# Fundamentals of Endurance

**Type:** Principles · **Trains:** Endurance, Power endurance, Power, General strength, Technique

**TL;DR:** Endurance depends above all on muscle economy: the easier the moves are for you, the greater your ability to endure them, so build strength, confidence and calm before chasing the pump. Seek the ability to avoid getting pumped in the first place, not the ability to handle being pumped. Train three intensities of session (short intense sets under 10 s, medium sets of 10–40 s, long easy sets up to about 10 min), aim for quality movement under slight fatigue, and climb more total moves per week and per month.

## Who should do what

| Situation / level | Do this |
|---|---|
| Average climber | Aim for 20 pitches per week before worrying about any other specific training |
| Beyond 20 pitches per week | Add more long, easy climbing to the week, aiming to get better rather than more fit |

## What endurance is

- Endurance is being able to display strength or power over a fixed period of time. In climbing it has a time limit: ropes are only so long, cliffs have rests (ledges, hand jams, knee bars), the day ends, and the anchors are a finish line where you can stop.
- Everyone has a force-generating capacity: a tiny crimp can be held for a few seconds, the next bigger hold for longer, a jug or pull-up bar for a few minutes. At any level these numbers can be trained higher.
- On a fixed mark (e.g. a favourite steep jug haul at your local crag) you slowly build the ability to link the moves more easily. Part of this is improved physiological function, which is what training targets. The rest comes from trying it over and over: knowing what's coming next so you can relax, confidence from a reduced feeling of fatigue, and the body learning the move pattern and becoming slightly more efficient.
- More important than building endurance capacity for a given route is increasing muscle economy long before project time; above all other factors, this determines endurance over the long term. If you can't hold the holds, there's nothing to endure; if the climbing is easy for you, nothing will tire you out.

## Where endurance training missed the mark

- Training endurance by quickly simulating the searing pump and whole-body fatigue of a deep pumpy route, and just doing more of that tiring stuff, is useful but best as a last step: a honing of the blade.
- The real work comes earlier: forging the capacity to endure by making all climbing movement easier so you don't try so hard move for move, developing more efficient systems in the body, and building a base of strength and power from which to launch endurance.
- Training is not performance. Sending is a clear, black-and-white achievement, so at the cliff climbers want to perform on every route. See incremental gains as success: link small sections of routes over and over and treat them as building blocks.
- Take a long view instead of constantly measuring yourself against lifetime bests and desperately seeking fatigue in training: ask what you really want a year from now and what your lifetime goal climbs ask of the body.
- Instead of seeking the ability to handle being pumped, seek the ability to avoid getting pumped in the first place.

## Factors affecting endurance

- You can climb continuously at an intensity that is very low compared to your maximum. Climbing combines two demands: performing repeatedly at high power (like sprinters who can be fast over and over at just sub-maximal speed) and simply being able to keep climbing. The take-home from muscle economy, and from boulderers who turn into endurance climbers: the easier the moves are for you, the greater your ability to endure them.
- Primary factor: pure strength. Long-term development of finger, upper-body and core strength lets you exert less force move for move, so your ability to output power at any level increases.
- To get strong, do things that require strength: try hard boulders and routes, and do a few heavy pulls a few times a week. Don't worry about being too organised at first; just work hard.
- **Warning:** don't get injured on the hangboard.
- Second factor: confidence. Strength and endurance help, but the biggest factor is experience: the more climbing you do on fatiguing routes, the more you believe you can climb more of them. No shortcut: the quickest way to be able to do lots of pitches is to do lots of pitches. The more you climb and the stronger you stay, the more confident you are.
- Third factor: fear. Climbers who fall off almost every time they go up the wall (e.g. youth team climbers) aren't afraid of falling but of failing. Few adults get to fearing failure because they are truly afraid of falling; many are so afraid that it drives all their decisions: where to climb, who to climb with, whether to lead. This fear turns up fight-or-flight, increases breathing rate, tightens the muscles and prevents seeing the whole picture while climbing.
- If you're afraid, no amount of physical conditioning will overcome what's going on in your head. Step one in endurance training is to get to the point where failing to do the climbing (not fear of moving above a bolt) is your greatest concern. Calm confidence is the foundation for endurance.

## Is the goal really endurance?

- The need for pure muscular endurance varies from climber to climber; getting to the top doesn't require a fixed level of power output. What's needed is muscular endurance AND efficiency: you can improve the end result by getting better at moving, not only by chasing the limited force output of your muscles.
- You still need time hanging from your fingertips and moving up overhanging terrain, relaxing as best you can and holding on as loosely as possible. The biggest improvement comes from returning to how well you are climbing. Each of these costs efficiency: both feet swinging off the wall, bearing down on a hold with maximum force, climbing with tense shoulders and a slack midsection.
- Total output for a climbing day is somewhat fixed. A sneaky way to get more endurance is to keep pushing to do a bit more climbing each day, building the capacity for more tries per day. Having just one golden try at your limit per day severely limits your potential for hard routes over the long run.
- Endurance is an amalgamation of: more practice to improve skills, more hours moving at the crag, calmness when fatigued (understanding you're not imperilled by it), and improved muscular endurance.
- You don't need (or necessarily want) burnout, sloppy laps on a circuit. Aim for quality movement under slight fatigue. Start every set as fresh as possible, as at the crag. Review each effort in the workout: did you do it right, or get sloppy and "power through"? Save powering through for the end of the session or the last few bolts of a pitch, not every set. Quality counts.
- You don't have to chase fatigue to build good fatigue resistance: big chunks of submaximal work build fitness without killing you.

## Three intensities of endurance session

- **Warning:** a common way to train endurance is to find climbs or circuits that make you super pumped, climb until super pumped, rest until a little recovered, and repeat a couple of times. It feels right, but it is too hard to build the ability for repeated efforts (capacity), and by nature too long for doing difficult moves over and over, so too easy to build the max power of the aerobic or anaerobic energy systems.
- The simplest way to train overall endurance: three intensities of session, placing the body under three types of stress.

| Session | Work time | What to do | Target |
|---|---|---|---|
| Short | Under 10 s; intense sets with long rests | Hard stuff such as campus ladders, short hard problems, jumps or pull-ups in an interval-style workout | End the session fatigued but not pumped: powered down but able to do another set if asked. If you get pumped, add time to the rests |
| Medium | 10–40 s | Linked problems, rest-to-problem, short interval links like 4x4s | You get more and more pumped and fight for composure; the goal is not to get sloppy in your climbing. Do this type of session the least often |
| Long | Up to about 10 min, not a lot longer; low intensity; rest can equal climbing time | Skilled climbing, not mindless traversing on the most convenient holds: route laps, big circuits, or repeating a couple of boulder problems and traversing between them | Never reach a pumped state. This is psychologically hard for strong climbers: most need to drop 10 or more number grades below their level |

- You can carefully plan and progress the specific climbing, or aim for the "spirit" of the session; both seem to produce positive results.
- Almost any session aimed at climbing longer or doing hard moves under fatigue stimulates overall endurance, so even with less frequent training than hoped you still stimulate it.
- Hard and fast rules for building a specific type of endurance are hard to give, and it's harder still to know whether you're in that zone (e.g. a workout of 20–40 min continuous climbing with a "light pump": do rest stances and chalking count?). The target is big and the tools varied: get into fatigued hard climbing occasionally and you'll get better at it.
- Most climbers don't do enough overall volume: they focus on one project at a time (often for weeks per route) and climb very few total moves per day. Climb more moves on more days and get used to it, and capacity goes up.
- Stay with the pump. At the first tightness in hands and forearms climbers often panic: hang on the rope, step off the bouldering wall, or desperately shake out at a rest. Climbing deep into the pump with composure is a skill in itself. After a few sessions of climbing into fatigue and pushing against it, you learn there may be one more level and a few more metres in you even when all your senses say no.
- As long as you keep working across all three zones and keep pushing each in the right direction, it's hard to go too wrong; every climber has built stamina a different way.
- A workout for each intensity (a short circuit, two-problem links, long intervals), plus add-on easy laps for capacity, is in the note Endurance Workouts: Add-On Laps, Short Circuits, Two-Problem Links and Long Intervals.

## Adaptations to endurance sessions

- A lack of fitness shows up in the same ways it is trained: lacking capacity for day-long or multi-day efforts, lacking the endurance to reach the anchors on a long pitch, and feeling the power drain away on a series of hard moves.
- General endurance activity could possibly help, but skiing, riding or running instead of climbing in order to get better at climbing makes little sense.
- Lots of time on rock or walls increases blood flow to the arm muscles, improves the muscles' ability to use energy and their efficiency at using it. More important are the mental adaptations: believing in yourself and your training, no longer panicking at the first sign of pump, and feeling fatigue as a return to a comfortable place rather than a terrifying one.
- The zone of improvement is less specific than you might think: do hard efforts over and over, some painful, energy-draining intervals, and hours of easier-than-you-think repeats. Aim for more total moves per week and per month, and the fitness will come.
