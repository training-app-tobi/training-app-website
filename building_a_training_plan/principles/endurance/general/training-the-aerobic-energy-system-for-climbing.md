# Training the Aerobic Energy System for Climbing

**Type:** Principles, Planning · **Trains:** Endurance, Recovery

**TL;DR:** The aerobic system drives recovery between efforts and raises the grade at which the pump starts. Build it in three steps: general capacity, climbing-specific capacity, then aerobic threshold, building volume at one intensity for a month before raising the intensity, never both at once. Every interval in a set should feel about the same as the first.

## Who should do what
| Situation / level | Do this |
|---|---|
| Most sport climbers | Build specific capacity to 10–15 pitches in a day |
| Wall link-ups or big-wall endurance objectives | Build capacity higher than that |
| Looking to really improve endurance | Up to two aerobic phases of 2–3 months per year (not one month each) |

## Energy systems by duration
| System | Time horizon | In climbing |
|---|---|---|
| Alactic | 8–15 s | Very short, hard boulder problems (e.g. a Moonboard problem) |
| Glycolytic | 30–90 s | Longer boulder problems or short sport routes: power output lasting a minute or two (maybe 20 moves) |
| Aerobic | Anything longer | Long pitches, all-day efforts, anything submaximal you could continue for 3 min, 5 min or an hour |

- The alactic system lasts 8–12 s; the aerobic system, always running, recovers it afterwards. The glycolytic system fuels efforts harder than the aerobic system can fuel but not maximal, and 1–2 min is about as long as it lasts.

## Why the aerobic system matters
- The pump starts when the climbing is above your aerobic ability: a well-trained, still-advancing aerobic system lets you climb harder grades without the pump.
- Recovery between efforts, whether 20 s or 20 min long, is driven by the aerobic system. If it is underdeveloped, you recover more slowly and may not recover completely.
- One-minute efforts of steep climbing get over 50% of their energy from the aerobic system.
- It allows longer sessions, and so more time on the wall to learn skills, which get more nuanced as grades rise (5.12 movement differs from 5.14 movement). A climber with a better aerobic system progresses far more per session than a similarly strong climber with a poorer one.

## Three-step progression
| Step | What it is | How |
|---|---|---|
| 1. General capacity | General aerobic base and work capacity: the prerequisite | More movement: walks, gardening, a hike, a bike ride, pickup basketball |
| 2. Climbing-specific capacity | Climbing multiple pitches at a submaximal level, all day | For most people a good threshold is 10 pitches in a day |
| 3. Aerobic power (aerobic threshold) | Keep the capacity but raise the grade at which you express it, with less and less pump | Gradually increase intensity |

- Extending capacity beyond 10–15 pitches may not be necessary for the average sport climber, a boulderer, or someone pursuing performance climbing.
- Volume for capacity eventually hits the limit of your weekly time or your skin. Ways to advance endurance training: increase difficulty, increase volume, or change the rest intervals.

## Aerobic threshold cycle
- Build volume at a given intensity for a month, then restart at a slightly higher intensity and build volume again.
- **Warning:** Programs fail when they try to build volume and intensity at the same time.

| Stage | Example: climber who climbs 5.11a aerobically |
|---|---|
| Start | 3 sets of 2 routes at 5.11a |
| Following sessions | 4, then 5, then 6 sets of 2 (12 pitches, which might be enough volume) |
| Next cycle | Reduce the volume, raise the intensity to 5.11b, start again |

## Finding the right intensity
| Work | Pump |
|---|---|
| Aerobic capacity | Very low (a little is okay) |
| Aerobic threshold | Medium: a little pump, but totally sustainable |

- Main cue: movement quality. If you couldn't do a technique drill or pay attention to footwork while climbing, it is probably not aerobic anymore.
- In a set of intervals (aerobic or power endurance), the first should feel about like the last. If the first 5.11a feels like 3 out of 10 and the last like 9 out of 10, or you fail at the same difficulty, the intensity is too high. A little increase in fatigue is okay.
- **Warning:** Focusing only on power endurance is a short-term solution (sometimes the best one for this season's project), with a hard limit set by the aerobic system. Projecting a pumpy 5.13a year after year without raising the grade you can climb without getting (overly) pumped misses the long-term aerobic adaptations.
- Two aerobic workouts for short walls, or for when skin or time are short, are in the note Aerobic Workouts for Short Walls: Boulders on the Minute and Hangboard Rowing.

## Place in the training year
- The aerobic system and strength are the two persistent pillars of climbing performance. Endurance is slow to build but takes relatively little work to maintain, so aerobic training should be a large part of preparation every year, whatever the discipline.
