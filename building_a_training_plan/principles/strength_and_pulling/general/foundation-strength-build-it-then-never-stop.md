# Foundation Strength: Build It, Then Never Stop

**Type:** Principles, Planning · **Trains:** General strength, Finger strength, Pulling strength, Antagonists & core

**TL;DR:** Strength is the basis for power and endurance, slow to build but easy to keep, so it is the hard-earned foundation you don't want to pay for twice. Foundation strength means applying high force in six ways: squatting, hinging, pressing, pulling, midsection and finger strength. Build it once or twice a year for 4–8 weeks and maintain it with low-volume sessions the rest of the time instead of letting it dissipate.

## Train year-round, ramp up for goals

- People who train generally fall into two groups: those who get in shape a month or two before "the season", and those who "train" all the time at about the same intensity. The most effective program is likely in a sweet spot in between: certain things every climber should do year-round, no matter the season (a basic level of athleticism is key to continued improvement and health), and on top of that, assess the needs of upcoming trips and goal routes and ramp up for them during certain parts of the year.
- Avoid dropping too far away from your highest levels of fitness: it makes those levels much easier to re-attain.
- Foundation is the hard-earned strength you don't want to pay for twice. Getting strong is a real chore and can take years and years; staying strong is the key to being able to do all the specific work. If you don't have to worry about strength, conditioning is easy.
- Foundation strength is what you do every week. It is not forced, it is coaxed. It is the kind of strength you can't fake, and the kind to prioritize in every phase of training.

## Why strength, not power or endurance, is the foundation

- Strength is the basis for all facets of fitness. Power is strength displayed explosively and is fully dependent on how strong you are. Endurance is strength (or power) displayed over time. Strength is a "persistent" factor of fitness: slow to build, but it tends to stay once built.
- Being able to stand from a squatting position, maintain an erect posture, pull your body up to an overhead bar or maintain torso stiffness sets the basis for doing those things faster or for longer. The easier a movement is for your body, the more you can repeat it.
- **Warning:** explosiveness is the basis of movement in climbing, but it can be dangerous without the strength to keep the body intact. Without the ability to create massive amounts of tension, efficiency in explosive movement suffers.
- **Warning:** if you maintain or build your base by doing lots of easy pitches, you will have a very hard time when the moves get hard.
- **Warning:** without a high level of finger strength, you risk injury and you work at a high percentage of your maximal strength when you shouldn't be.

## What foundation strength looks like

- Like a house: the deeper the footings, the wider the slab and the thicker the sub-floor, the bigger the house you can build on it. The more force you can apply and the more strain your connective tissue can take, the further you'll go in any sport.
- Train movement patterns, not muscles: take fundamental human movements and design ways to overload them in the gym. Tiring out individual muscles on machines (pecs, biceps, delts…) doesn't make them function correctly in a real-world setting, nor does it necessarily make them stronger. Letting muscles work together in natural movement patterns, with ground-based exercises, better mimics what happens in sport.
- For climbing, foundation strength is the ability to apply high levels of force in six ways: five standard movements (squatting, hinging, pressing, pulling, midsection) plus finger strength, which is fundamental to climbing but almost unnecessary in most other activities.
- A standard for each of the six is in the note Foundation Strength Standards: Six Movement Benchmarks.

## How often: build and maintain

- **Warning:** work to improve foundation strength all the time. One of the biggest mistakes is to build strength in the off-season and then let it dissipate through the performance seasons. Strength is easy to keep and very useful when maintained.

| Phase | How often | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Build (build strength quickly) | Once or twice a year, for 4–8 weeks | Most athletes train strength 4–5 days per week, usually combining resistance exercise and hangboarding/grip training. Keep climbing, but the mindset shifts from performing on the rock to getting stronger |
| Maintenance (build strength slowly) | The rest of the time | Low-volume sessions done together with climbing or specific training days. Sessions should leave you able to climb hard the same day: no tapping out in the gym |

A year of thirteen 4-week cycles:

| Cycles | Use |
|---|---|
| 3 | Strength builds |
| 9 | Maintenance phases |
| 1 | Rest, vacation, illness etc. |

- Training strength before performance can enhance performance ("strength potentiation").
- Even in a maintenance phase, strength can still increase in some exercises.

## Consistency

- "Getting in shape" with an on-again-off-again program, however hard, never measures up to a simple punch-the-clock program. No amount of work done today can offset the progress you could have made in a properly structured week; the same goes for a month, a year and a career.
- Design training around what you can manage long-term: what you could do if you weren't feeling great, had low motivation and were training alone. Whatever you can make yourself do on those days is where foundation training should start.
- Low day, not feeling it: show up anyway and do nothing more than a few minutes of jogging and some movement prep. Great day: train for 2 hours or more.
- The habit of showing up is the key to long-term success.
