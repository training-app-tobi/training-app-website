# Should Climbers Weight Train? Reasons For and Against

**Type:** Principles · **Trains:** General strength, Pulling strength, Antagonists & core

**TL;DR:** Weight training helps climbing only when it is specific, safe and kept small: 75–80% of available training time should go to climbing, the other 20–25% to training for climbing or other activities. Done right, it builds strength without bulk, teaches tension control, supports mental and physical health and prevents injuries such as shoulder injuries from rotation-strength imbalance. Train large muscle groups (deadlifts, squats, pull-ups), aim for strength rather than fatigue, and log and finish your program.

## Reasons against: weight training is often not specific to climbing
- Weight training can be effective, specific training for climbing, but only if done right. Most people don't have the training guidance or the hours of free time for research to do it right, so they don't know how to weight train in a way that specifically helps their climbing. Result: little to no gain in performance, or even worse climbing, because resources are allocated inappropriately.
- Specificity: the energy system targeted by weight training should reflect your style of climbing. Without this specificity you train the wrong energy systems and it won't help climbing performance.

| Style of climbing | Weight training |
|---|---|
| Boulderer working problems that last only a few moves | Higher-resistance, lower-repetition movements |
| Sport climber working a long route | More endurance-based weight training |

- Exercise choice: pick weight training exercises that specifically help climbing. Heavy bicep curls train in isolation a muscle that works together with many other muscle groups while climbing; an inverted row is better: it uses bodyweight and puts you in a position to train the correct muscle groups for climbing.
- **Warning:** Bicep curls in isolation may cause an overuse injury, or simply take away from climbing performance: with wrecked biceps you can't do your climbing training effectively.

## Reasons against: weight training can be risky
- **Warning:** Weight training is designed to progressively overload tissue and force it to create a new adaptation. Without knowing what you're doing you can end up with an overuse injury, an injury due to improper form, or a too-much-too-soon injury (increasing the weights far too quickly and damaging the tissue).
- **Warning:** The bicep curl shows two ways to cause an overuse injury: (1) it adds to the force that muscle group has to tolerate on top of climbing; (2) it reduces rest and recovery time for that muscle group.
- **Warning:** Weighted pull-ups are a common training tool for climbers, but if you don't adjust your climbing routine they put a high amount of strain on the shoulders and elbows and don't allow adequate recovery or rest time.
- Weight training is meant to supplement and add to climbing: if it injures you and you can't climb as a result, you're going in the wrong direction.

## Reasons against: time
- The most important reason against: people often spend too much time weight training and not enough time climbing. The best training for climbing is climbing: 75–80% of available training time should go to climbing; the other 20–25% can go to training for climbing or other activities.
- **Warning:** Too much weight training also takes up rest and recovery time: you may not be at optimum performance, can't push your limits or put max effort into climbing, and so likely won't advance.
- The body has only a certain capacity for adaptation: the more modes of training, the harder it is to get great at any one of them; the fewer, the better you become at each. Too much weight training and too many different styles of it rob the body of adaptation time for climbing.

## Reasons for: strength, body awareness and tension control
- Weight training improves strength, which helps you pull harder, build body awareness and learn to control tension.
- Done right, it won't bulk you out: strength training and hypertrophy training are different.

| Training | How | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Hypertrophy training | Moderate to sometimes high intensity with very high volume | Makes you big (e.g. cyclists' big legs) |
| Strength training | High intensity, lower volume, low repetitions | You can get stronger without adding weight |

- Deadlift example: done appropriately, it engages the scapular retractors, core, glutes and hamstrings in one movement, and teaches you to control the tension between those muscle groups. This makes you more efficient without getting heavier, and that tension control helps in climbing.

## Reasons for: mental and physical health
- Mental health: the distraction hypothesis says exercise gives a mental timeout from worrying, stressful or negative thoughts; the mastery hypothesis says completing an important or effortful task brings a feeling of mastery that can elevate mood (e.g. hitting a new PR in weight training).
- Climbing 100% of the time can be unhealthy in its own way: the hobby can start to feel like a job, doing only one thing can lead to burnout, and accomplishments can feel like completing a task rather than doing something fun. Complementing climbing with some weight training gives a reprieve and a mental boost.
- Physical benefits of exercise: lower blood pressure, weight loss, prevention of chronic diseases.
- Weight training, especially of large muscle groups, boosts anabolic hormones such as testosterone and growth hormone. Their increased bioavailability can trigger additional tissue building throughout the body or specifically where there is damage, which can mean faster healing and quicker recovery.

## Reasons for: stability and fewer injuries
- Weight training can cause injuries without good training and education, but done correctly it increases stability throughout the body and can prevent injuries. Avoiding injury lets you keep climbing, and continuing to climb makes you better, so climbing-specific weight training to prevent injuries can enhance climbing.
- **Warning:** Climbers often focus so much on hand and forearm strength that they forget strong shoulders; shoulder injuries are sometimes seen more often than pulley injuries. Research shows an imbalance between external and internal rotation strength of the shoulder can lead to shoulder injury; this is very common in overhead athletes, as it causes a biomechanical deficit that could be avoided with proper weight training.

## Recommendations
- Train large muscle groups to make the best use of time and get the hormonal benefits: e.g. deadlifts, squats, pull-ups.
- Focus on the outcome of strength training (strength) rather than sweat, fatigue or feeling gassed at the end.
- Do some research or find a climbing coach to work out what style of weight training to do for your style of climbing; this also helps avoid training mistakes that cause injury.
- There isn't one perfect way to train; each person's training may look different. Write down the results of your training so you can tell whether the program is working, and finish the program so you know whether its results were effective.
