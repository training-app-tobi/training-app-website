# Selecting Strength Exercises for a Climbing Move: Movement, Joints, Muscles, Exercise and Velocity

**Type:** Principles, Exercise/protocol · **Trains:** General strength, Pulling strength, Power · **For:** new to strength training, diminishing returns

**TL;DR:** An exercise is not necessarily specific; the adaptation you get from it is what is specific. To pick a specific exercise, identify the movement, the joints working, the prime movers around those joints, then choose a simple, stable exercise that allows progressive overload; one more step, the velocity of the lift, makes it even more specific. For most climbers, high load at slow velocity is great for strength gains and recruitment. If you have never done strength training, non-specific strength transfers quickly: pick an exercise you enjoy and stay consistent.

## Who should do what
| Situation / level | Do this |
|---|---|
| New to strength training | Non-specific strength transfers quite quickly to climbing; specificity is probably not that important for now. Pick an exercise you think you'll enjoy and stay consistent with it |
| Seeing diminishing returns from non-specific strength training | Start making your strength training more specific |

## Specificity: adaptations, not exercises
- An exercise is not necessarily specific; the adaptation you get from it is what is specific.
- An exercise doesn't need to look like or mimic a movement in the sport to be specific, as long as it produces an adaptation that benefits your climbing.

| Exercise | How it looks | What it gives |
|---|---|---|
| TRX chest fly | Looks very specific to climbing: you squeeze in using the chest, biceps and shoulders; it looks and feels like compression climbing (crushing something between you) and feels like a pulling exercise | The extra intensity and recruitment in the muscles that a bench press gives are not easy to achieve with it |
| Bench press | Looks non-specific: you push away from you, and you never have to push away from the wall | Strength and recruitment in the chest (pecs) and shoulders (deltoids), muscles still used in climbing, so its adaptations are specific to a certain style of climbing. May give more benefit than the TRX fly, because it is simple and allows progressive overload, for more intensity and recruitment in the muscles |

## The process
A four-step process to select an exercise that is specific and appropriate for any climbing training goal, plus one more step to make the exercise even more specific to the adaptation you want.

| Step | What to do |
|---|---|
| 1 | Identify the movement |
| 2 | Identify the joints working |
| 3 | Identify the muscles working around those joints |
| 4 | Choose exercises that target those muscles |
| One more step | Choose the velocity of the lift |

## Step 1: identify the movement
- Identify a movement in your climbing you want to get better at: either a move on a specific project (a crux move where you want to strengthen a certain area of the body) or a weakness you have identified and want to make a strength.
- Upper-body example, a hard undercling move: the difficulty is keeping tension through the lower hand while moving upward again with the hand that's already above. You pull in with the lower hand toward the hip so the hips stay close to the wall; this closeness of the hips to the wall helps you reach the other hand up for the bump. The upper arm stays bent as it pulls you in, and the elbow is tucked back toward the hip as the shoulder retracts to keep you close to the wall.
- Lower-body example, a heel hook: the leg drives the hips into the wall as you reach; the leg has to work really hard in this position so you don't sink away as you reach with one hand toward the next hold.

## Steps 2 to 4: worked examples
| Step | Undercling lock-off | Heel hook |
|---|---|---|
| 2. Main joints | Elbow and shoulder | Knee and hip, both working hard to maintain the position |
| 3. Prime movers | Biceps (keep the elbow flexed) and lats (at the shoulder, keep the elbow toward the hip) | Hamstrings and glutes |
| 4. Chosen exercises | Biceps curl (biceps strength) and low row (lat strength and scapular retraction) | Stiff-legged deadlift (hamstrings and glutes) |

## Step 2: identify the joints
- Climbing is always a full-body activity, with multiple (if not all) joints active and stabilizing the position. Pick the joints working the hardest, or the limiting factor.
- Example: in the heel hook the ankle also does quite a lot, but mostly just to create the profile of the heel; it isn't working as hard as the knee or hip to create or stabilize the movement.

## Step 3: identify the muscles
- Identify the muscles around these joints that create the action. Some anatomy helps; without it, a simple search usually works, e.g. "what bends the elbow" gives the muscles involved in elbow flexion.
- Many other muscles are involved: at the shoulder, the rhomboids and traps are big muscles fixating and stabilizing the movement. For simplicity, look only at the prime movers and focus training there. Keeping it this simple benefits most people.

## Step 4: choose the exercises
- Find exercises that target those muscles (biceps, lats, hamstrings, glutes). If you don't know any, search for exercises that train that muscle (e.g. "exercises that train the biceps / the hamstrings"). Not every exercise you find is a great one; use the criteria below.

| Criterion | Choose | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Progressive overload | An exercise that easily allows progressive overload: generally free weights or machines | Bands or straps: convenient, but harder to measure and progressively overload |
| 2. Simplicity | Something easy to do | Exercises that look complex and take ages to learn: difficult skill-based calisthenics moves, or big multi-compound lifts you probably need coaching for |
| 3. Stability | A stable exercise | Balance-based, rehab-style exercises built on proprioception: they mainly improve your ability to do the exercise and have limited transfer to climbing, because they are about learning a skill, not strength development and recruitment |
| 4. Equipment | An exercise convenient for the equipment you have | |
| 5. Muscle length | Go back to the movement analysis: if the move puts you really stretched out or really bunched up, consider whether the muscle works hard in a short position, a long position, or through a full range of motion, and choose the exercise and how you perform it accordingly | |

- You get strong in the range you train: research shows that training partials makes you stronger in the partial range, and training only at end range makes you stronger at end range.
- Hamstrings: one of the best exercises is the hamstring curl machine: an easy, effective way to get targeted intensity into the hamstrings, easy to progressively overload, and stable. Many gyms (at least in the UK) don't have one, so adapt to a more convenient exercise: the stiff-legged deadlift, mainly because it is very convenient and easily progressively overloaded.
- Enjoyment is probably one of the biggest factors in selecting an exercise for a training program: you stay more motivated and more consistent with it.

| Approach | Example | Pros |
|---|---|---|
| Compound (multiple muscles, multiple joints) | Stiff-legged deadlift: trains the hip and knee (hamstrings and glutes) at the same time | More convenient and time-efficient: one exercise |
| Isolation | Hamstring curl and hip thrust: more activation into just the hamstrings or just the glutes | Arguably better strength training for each targeted area |

- Both approaches have pros and cons.

## One more step: velocity
- The adaptations to an exercise depend on the velocity at which you perform it.
- In pretty much every case, try hard and work at a high level in all strength training. The velocity then depends on the weight: with a light weight velocity should be high; with a heavy weight it will naturally be low, because of how fast muscles can contract against a really heavy weight.

| Option | Load and reps | Velocity | Adaptation |
|---|---|---|---|
| a | Light weight, multiple reps to failure or close to it | The contraction slows down | A good strength training effect, with a high level of hypertrophy (muscle growth) |
| b | Heavy weight, fewer reps | Slow contraction | Better adaptation for strength gains and for the tendons |
| c | Light weight, low number of reps | Maximum velocity and maximum intent | Strength is expressed more through speed, which is how you express it on the wall when a move needs speed; good tendon adaptation, less muscle building and hypertrophy |

- For most climbers, working at a high load and slow velocity is great for strength gains and recruitment; in certain cases hypertrophy is really beneficial.
- High-velocity work can be done with most of the example exercises: move the bar as fast as possible, with a lower weight than when training for maximum strength. Use it, for example, if the undercling move actually needs you to drive a lot of power into the wall for a right-hand move such as a pogo.
- Gains in maximum strength transfer to gains in velocity. With periodization you can layer these:

| Phase | Focus | Result |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Maximum strength | Still seeing gains in power |
| 2 | Taper into a high-velocity focus | Further gains in power |

## When training doesn't need to be specific
- Making strength training specific may be overrated for some: people often do relatively unspecific training and still see big benefits in their climbing. There are a few circumstances where training doesn't need to be specific.
- Beginners or novices to strength training: not necessarily beginner climbers; advanced climbers who have never lifted weights or done any strength training count too. Non-specific strength transfers quite quickly to their climbing.
- Hypertrophy: bigger muscles can produce more force, and more force can benefit climbing, so this doesn't need to be very specific.
- **Warning:** hypertrophy may be unwanted in certain areas: it increases body weight and can hurt your strength-to-weight ratio in the fingers.
- Health and recovery: strength training to improve your health and ability to recover benefits climbing in general without a direct transfer to performance. Likewise metabolic conditioning or cardio is not very specific to climbing performance, but in the long run may improve it: less time off, better recovery, feeling better in sessions.
