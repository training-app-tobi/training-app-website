# Weight Training on Top of Climbing and Correcting an Arm Imbalance

**Type:** Principles, Planning · **Trains:** General strength, Pulling strength · **For:** arm strength imbalance

**TL;DR:** Supplemental strength training makes you progress faster when it supplies a stimulus climbing can't, such as progressive overload for the legs, or for muscles that are your weak point rather than your fingers; weigh the specificity of training on the wall against how easily a training form gives progressive overload. Correct an imbalance of 20% or more between the arms by holding the stronger arm's load and progressively overloading the weaker arm slightly more, raising total load by usually 2.5–5% a week instead of the normal 5–10%.

## Who should do what
| Situation / level | Do this |
|---|---|
| Arm strength imbalance of 20% or more | Correct it and bring it back into range; if you are already doing that, continue |

## Weight training on top of climbing
- Supplemental strength training can make you progress faster when it supplies a stimulus that climbing can't give you because of a limiting factor: it develops strength-based adaptations at a faster rate. The gains stay only if you keep doing it.
- Climbing can give progressive overload, but to hit every part of the body through climbing you would need to be very intentional, have great facilities, be extremely creative and have setting opportunities.
- Climbing does not progressively overload the legs, unless you do stand-up slabs, and even then you rarely find moves that force you to press to maximum. For upper-body muscles, the fingers and skin give out long before the muscles in general climbing, unless your fingers are not the weak point and your muscles are.
- This is why climbers campus (to overload the arms further) and do dynos (to get more powerful): to get these adaptations within climbing and keep the training specific, instead of using less specific weight training.
- If you can't fit all of that into your programming, less specific weight training will help you progress faster, because it makes progressive overload easy and efficient, targets exclusively one thing and mixes in with the rest of your training. Example: training legs and fingers through climbing overlaps and doesn't truly target either.
- Weigh the specificity of training it on the wall against how well the training form gives the progressive overload you want for the adaptation.

## Correcting a strength imbalance between the arms
- An imbalance of 20% or more between the arms (e.g. one-arm lock-off time or one-arm pull-up) is significant: correct it and bring it back into range. If you are already doing that, continue.
- Otherwise you don't need to do anything, but it would be smart if it was below 20%.
- How to correct it: keep the load on the stronger arm constant (maintain), or drop it to a level that gives a lesser adaptation, and progressively overload the weaker arm slightly more.
- For the one-arm pull-up: add unilateral training to your supplemental strength training for pulling exercises; uneven pull-ups are another option.

It takes time: a really slow build, because one side is overloaded and the other isn't.

| Training | Maximum increase in total load, week to week |
|---|---|
| Normal strength training | 5–10% |
| With unilateral intention | Usually 2.5–5% |
