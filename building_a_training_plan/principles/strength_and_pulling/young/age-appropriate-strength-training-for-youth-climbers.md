# Age-Appropriate Strength Training for Youth Climbers

**Type:** Planning, Injury & health · **Trains:** General strength, Finger strength, Technique · **For:** Youth

**TL;DR:** Youth climbers reach high levels mostly through consistent climbing and good instruction, and must not train exactly like adults. A limited amount of age-appropriate, well-monitored strength training is safe and useful: bodyweight exercises only at 6–9, a moderate amount (20–40 minutes, 3 days per week) at 10–15, and a gradual move to more intensive climbing-specific training at 16–18. Double-dyno campusing and hypergravity training wait until after 18, and antagonist training for the push muscles is perhaps the most important addition (see the note Antagonist Training for Youth Climbers).

## Who should do what
| Situation / level | Do this |
|---|---|
| Ages 6–9 | Movement-oriented training is the backbone of every workout. Climbing-specific strength comes simply from a consistent climbing schedule. A few supplemental climbing-like exercises are fine, as long as they don't take away from movement training and climbing-for-fun time. |
| Ages 10–15 (1+ year of climbing, solid technique) | Introduce a moderate amount of strength training: 20–40 minutes, 3 days per week. The main emphasis stays on developing outstanding climbing technique and mental fortitude. |
| Ages 16–18 (approaching adult height) | Begin a gradual transition into a more intensive climbing-specific training program. |

## How young climbers get strong
- Elite youth climbers (preteens sending V10 and 5.13) do not get there through a secret, arduous strength-training program. They reach elite levels mostly through consistent practice, high-quality instruction, a featherweight prepubescent physique and sometimes the genetic gift of uncommon flexibility, grip strength and power.
- Simply climbing 3 or 4 days per week for several years is enough for many who start young to pull through steep terrain on tiny holds with efficiency and ease.
- Believing in such secret programs has led many youngsters into unnecessary and potentially dangerous exercises.
- Youth climbers must not train exactly like adults.

## Is strength training safe for youth
- A limited amount of strength training does no harm and brings a good amount of benefit.
- "Strength training is not for kids" and "strength training stunts a youth's growth" are myths. Basic strength training for kids is supported by pediatric and sports-medicine bodies.
- Condition: the exercises must be age-appropriate, not overly dynamic or stressful, and well monitored for proper form.
- Guidelines are split into three age groups: 6–9, 10–15, 16–18.

## Ages 6–9: why strength training isn't needed yet
- Before the adolescent growth spurt, most apparent strength gains come from motor learning, not hypertrophy (muscle growth).
- Motor learning means adaptations of the nervous system, specifically better coordination and synchronization of motor units (groups of muscle fibers that fire together), which lead to a greater expression of strength and power.
- So any extensive strength training is unnecessary and largely a waste of time at this age.
- A consistent climbing schedule gives measurable strength gains with little or no change in muscle size.

## Exercises by age
| Age | Exercises | Hold off on |
|---|---|---|
| 6–9 | Bodyweight exercises only: pull-ups, push-ups, abdominal crunches and similar gymnastic movements | Any extensive strength training |
| 10–15 | Pull-ups, lock-offs, fingerboard pull-ups and hangs (in small doses), slow, controlled campus laddering (never double dynos), various core-strengthening routines. Do them on climbing days, not rest days. | Double-dyno campus training and high-resistance hypergravity isolation training (HIT) |
| 16–18 | Elite training techniques added incrementally over this multiyear ramp-up: one-arm pull-ups, campus laddering, weighted bouldering (e.g. with a 4.5 kg belt) | Fully dynamic double-dyno campus training and advanced hypergravity protocols until after the 18th birthday (or later for a late bloomer) |

- **Warning:** At 10–15, avoid the most advanced and stressful exercises used by older elite climbers: double-dyno campus training and high-resistance hypergravity isolation training (HIT). They can cause growth plate fractures in the fingers and force a long break from climbing.

## Ages 10–15: the growth spurt
- These are the years of the adolescent growth spurt. Peak height velocity: age 11.5 for girls, 13.5 for boys.
- Changing hormone levels lead to noticeable muscle growth as weight velocity peaks between 12 and 15.
- Some fast-growing youths notice a disturbing drop in strength-to-weight ratio, because weight gain outpaces strength gains. Quality coaching is critical so they understand why they seem to be getting weaker despite a consistent climbing schedule.
- **Warning:** Reacting to this drop with an extensive strength- or power-training program is unfortunate and unhealthy and often ends in injury or burnout.
- A good coach explains the changes all youth climbers go through in puberty, and how the process leads to a physique that is stronger, taller, more powerful and very capable of climbing at a high level.
- The coach gradually introduces both general and sport-specific strength exercises that train the new muscle to be efficient and effective on the vertical plane. This also reduces injury risk as growing youths try harder climbs.
- Antagonist training and stretching for growing climbers are in the notes Antagonist Training for Youth Climbers, and Stretching for Growing Youth Climbers.

## Ages 16–18: the transition to adult training
- **Warning:** Growth plates are still vulnerable and may not fully fuse until around age 20. Increases in training volume and intensity can lead to overuse and acute injuries of the fingers, elbows and shoulders.
- For these reasons, a fully engaged and informed coach or climbing parent is highly beneficial during this transition into self-directed adult climbing.
- Many late-teen climbers are already highly accomplished.
- **Warning:** Hold off on fully dynamic double-dyno campus training and advanced hypergravity protocols until after the 18th birthday (or later for a late bloomer).

## The complete climber
- The program must stay focused on the complete climber, including mental and technical training. A narrow, impulsive pursuit of more strength and power is not the only path to improvement.
- Remind youths going through puberty and maturation often that sharp jumps in ability often come from mental breakthroughs.
- Mastery comes only from vast experience on a wide range of climbing walls and rock types.
- A good coach constantly combines physical training with ongoing instruction on advanced climbing techniques, tactics and the mental game.
- For the details of an advanced youth training program, hire an experienced climbing coach.
