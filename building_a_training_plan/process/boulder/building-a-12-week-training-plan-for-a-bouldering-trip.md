# Building a 12-Week Training Plan for a Bouldering Trip

**Type:** Planning, Story/case study · **Trains:** Finger strength, Power endurance, Endurance · **For:** preparing a bouldering trip

**TL;DR:** Build the plan in five steps: basic structure, lifestyle constraints, strengths and weaknesses from testing, the specific goal, then the workouts. Over 12 weeks: two base mesocycles each ending with a deload week, a build mesocycle, a taper week, then the trip. Put the fixed outdoor sessions in first, then plan two structured climbing sessions a week (changed each mesocycle); the fingerboard sessions, weights-gym session, shoulder and core work and mobility are planned after the climbing.

## The method
| Order | Step |
|---|---|
| 1 | Build the basic structure: the foundation of everything |
| 2 | Work around lifestyle constraints: the key to consistency |
| 3 | Review strengths and weaknesses, to program the training properly |
| 4 | Use specific goals to your advantage |
| 5 | Choose the right workouts |

- There are many ways to train well and different methods work for different athletes; this is one method, not the only way.
- Worked example: a V9 boulderer going on a summer bouldering trip, 12 weeks available (start of April to mid-June), test results from a couple of weeks before planning.
- Parts of this plan have their own notes: the test review (Reviewing Test Results Before a Bouldering Trip: Edge Sizes, Fitness and Mobility), the fingerboard sessions (Two Fingerboard Sessions a Week Before a Bouldering Trip), the weights-gym session (A Weights-Gym Session Before a Bouldering Trip), the shoulder and core work (Shoulder and Core Conditioning Before a Bouldering Trip) and the mobility (Mobility Before a Bouldering Trip: Hide It in the Warm-Up).

## Lifestyle constraints
- Before any structure, look at lifestyle constraints such as work and family. Example: an inflexible work schedule of Monday, Tuesday, Thursday and Friday with Wednesdays off, working near a gym with good boards → the plan is built around working days and days off.
- Unstructured climbing (social sessions, outdoor climbing with friends at weekends): if it matters to you, put it into the plan. It is a priority of the plan, to maintain motivation, and strongly recommended.

## Basic structure
| Level | Length |
|---|---|
| Macrocycle | The biggest span of time available (here 12 weeks) |
| Mesocycle | Usually 3 to 4 weeks |
| Microcycle | About a week |

| Order | Phase |
|---|---|
| 1 | Base phase, ending with a deload week: builds fatigue, but also the foundations of later performance |
| 2 | Base phase, ending with a deload week |
| 3 | Build phase: works on all the specifics for the trip |
| 4 | Taper week: still training, but with reduced volume, so you leave recovered and refreshed |
| 5 | Performance (the trip) |

- Deload weeks are not always necessary. They are used mainly as a psychological break from structured training (physiological improvements can come too): no structure, free climbing, so you come back to the next phase more motivated than before.
- **Warning:** Don't finish a training phase and go straight into performance: you'll often carry fatigue. Put a taper week before the trip.

## Placing the fixed sessions first
- First put in the fixed elements: open outdoor climbing and the trip itself. Trip week: about four outdoor bouldering sessions, with a reminder to start slowly, build up and take care of the skin.
- Deload weeks: optional open sessions, completely unstructured, for fun, as the psychological break before the next training period.
- **Warning:** People often do too much climbing in one session during a deload week. Finish each session feeling fresh, don't push too hard or for too long, and go into the next cycle slightly more recovered.
- Outdoor limit bouldering at weekends (a priority): two sessions per week in each mesocycle (Saturday and Sunday). One is optional, because you may not want to climb both days or may have social engagements; the other has an alternative session for bad weather.

## Replacing an outdoor session
What replaces an outdoor or social session depends on the training phase.

| Phase | Replacement session |
|---|---|
| Mesocycle 1 | Boulder mileage, unstructured, changing wall angles and hold types for variety, to build fitness |
| Mesocycle 2 | Board climbing on a steep board on bigger holds (suits the climber's style, fun, unstructured) |
| Towards the final mesocycle | A longer board session on the same board, spending more time on smaller, crimpier holds |

## Using the goal
- Gather information on the goal problem: talk to people who have climbed it, watch videos of it, read comments on online guidebook sites.
- Example findings: a very crimpy problem, so it works the climber's weakness; needs good flexibility to keep the hips into the wall and make the most of small holds; needs good shoulder stability in an externally rotated position. All of these go into the plan.
- Simple way to find the fitness a goal needs: time how long climbers are on the wall in videos (timestamps). Example: about 24 seconds → at some point, train for efforts of roughly 24 seconds.

## Climbing sessions before conditioning
- Plan the climbing before the conditioning: climbing is a skill-based sport, so prioritise climbing movement above all else; also for enjoyment, when the climber enjoys climbing training more than conditioning.
- Two structured climbing sessions per week (one on the day off, one on another day), plus 1 to 2 weekend outdoor sessions that are unstructured but at the limit: count them in the overall training load.
- Change the workouts each mesocycle: a fresh training stimulus and a psychological change, so the climber stays motivated to try something new.

### Mesocycle 1
| Session | Part | Content |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 | Board climbing on specific board problems, some on smaller holds, at a success rate of about 50% or higher. Not projecting at your absolute limit: especially in a base phase, complete climbs and do lots of moves; that is when you build your strength base |
| 1 | 2 | 6-in-6: boulders on the minute for six minutes, to build power endurance (for performance across the trip, for longer problems and for the goal problem) |
| 2 | 1 | MoonBoard benchmarks within a recommended grade range below the limit (the MoonBoard can feel sandbagged; no need to climb 7C or 7C+ on it to be ready for the trip), for consistency and a high success rate: lots of successful climbs |
| 2 | 2 | One-on, one-off interval endurance: 1 minute climbing, 1 minute resting, repeated over and over. Builds the ability to recover between efforts and base endurance |

- One-on, one-off: because each effort is only 1 minute (a more anaerobic effort), there is less interference with the strength part of the same session, and recovery capacity is built during the rest off the wall. Works well for many boulderers.

### Mesocycle 2
| Session | Part | Content |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 | Project moves only, on a board or on boulders around the wall, at a level that is really hard for you: complete set moves at project level, without having to send the problems |
| 1 | 2 | Body-tension boulders: complete the boulders without cutting loose (the trip's climbs often need a lot of body tension) |
| 1 | 3 | Anaerobic (power endurance) work: a 12-move project, a longer boulder problem worked, not full project bouldering |
| 2 | 1 | Repeat harder climbs on the MoonBoard, interval style: 4 boulders 3 times each, with enough rest between, on problems the climber finds really challenging. Start the mesocycle with 4 problems and build to 5 by the end |
| 2 | 2 | A much easier endurance component: ARC (climbing around the wall nice and easily). It can be done at the start as a warm-up instead |

- Session 1 covers the full range: hard moves, specific boulders, a longer boulder.
- Repeats build a strength base and a movement base: repeating climbs slightly below your max level successfully makes you much stronger. Building from 4 to 5 problems gives less of a spike while the intensity builds. Use the hardest benchmarks learned in mesocycle 1, whose move patterns are already known.
- Because the repeats are high volume, keep the endurance part simple after so much hard climbing.

### Mesocycle 3 (build)
- Sessions become more and more specific. Replica training: many people don't need replica training or replica problems at all, and building general strength and fitness takes you a long way; but people buy into their training more with replica-style training before a trip, because it feels specific to the goal. It doesn't have to be perfect: it only has to replicate the style of movement and the type of hold.

| Session | Part | Content |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 | Replica hard moves that copy the type of climb for the trip |
| 1 | 2 | The 12-move anaerobic project: power endurance for the length of the trip's climbs, sustaining an effort for longer |
| 2 | 1 | Projecting much harder climbs on the MoonBoard |
| 2 | 2 | ARC (or as a warm-up) |

- MoonBoard progression across the plan: benchmarks → repeats → projects, building intensity.
- **Warning:** Keep ARC intensity low, to reduce the interference effect and help recovery.
- Throughout planning, keep checking a graph of general loading over the 12 weeks to see how the weeks stack up and where the attributes lie. In this plan: a lot of strength and power, a moderate amount of fingerboarding and power endurance, and a regular open session.
