# Steps for Building a Training Plan

Ten steps, in the order the process notes give them. Each step has a content folder with the material for that step. The principles apply throughout: [principles/whole_plan](../principles/whole_plan/) at every step, and the quality folders (finger strength, strength and pulling, power, power endurance, endurance, technique and skill, mobility, antagonists and core) once step 04 has chosen the focus.

The order comes from the process notes in this folder:

- [Principles of Training Program and Session Design](general/principles-of-training-program-and-session-design.md): goal, current status, then the time frame and the things available to you, then plan backwards; in each session, reps or time, sets, load, tempo and rest come before the exercise.
- [Writing Your Own Climbing Training Plan in 3 Steps](general/writing-your-own-climbing-training-plan-in-3-steps.md): before and after; then the timescale, with a goal window and life commitments; then structure and sessions, with the details last.
- [Planning the Journey: From Assessment to a Realistic Timeline](general/planning-the-journey.md): after the assessment, intermediate goals, a timeline worked backwards, and monitoring against it.
- [Building a 12-Week Training Plan for a Bouldering Trip](boulder/building-a-12-week-training-plan-for-a-bouldering-trip.md): the same method on a real case (structure, lifestyle constraints, strengths and weaknesses, the goal, then the workouts), with the full plan.

## 01 Climbing goal

- **Why first:** [Principles of Training Program and Session Design](general/principles-of-training-program-and-session-design.md) starts with the goal, and [Assessing Your Starting Point Before Writing a Training Plan](../content/02_assessment/general/assessing-your-starting-point-before-writing-a-training-plan.md) chooses the tests from the goal's demands, so the goal comes before the assessment.
- Check the goal against your values before making it SMART: [Goal Setting and Your Values](../content/01_climbing_goal/goal-setting-and-your-values.md).
- Get the general rules right first (inspiring, realistic, demanding, fun): [Making Successful Climbing Goals](../content/01_climbing_goal/making-successful-climbing-goals.md).
- Give the goal a date with at least two weeks either side: a window, not a slot (step 2 of the 3-steps note).
- Lay out the dream, 3-month checkpoints and a goal for each session: [Goal Setting on a Timeline: Long-, Medium- and Short-Term Climbing Goals](../content/01_climbing_goal/goal-setting-on-a-timeline.md), [SMART Goals for Climbing](../content/01_climbing_goal/smart-goals-for-climbing.md), [Effective Goal Setting for Climbing](../content/01_climbing_goal/effective-goal-setting-for-climbing.md).
- Name the goal's type, because the type decides how you find what it demands: a climb (a named boulder or a named route), a trip or a competition (a bouldering trip, a sport trip, preparing for a competition), a grade (the next boulder grade or the next sport grade), or a general goal (a terrain, a hold style, a movement style, your antistyle or all-round climbing, such as "stronger on steep terrain" or "all-round bouldering"). Every goal of each type, with how it is measured and the goals that go with it: [plan_template/goals](../../plan_template/goals/INDEX.md).
- List what the goal demands, for each of your 2–3 goals and in as much detail as you can, since step 02 chooses the tests from this list. Start with the biggest factors (wall angle, hold type and size, number of moves), then the length of the efforts, their intensity and rest, and the daily volume (Specificity in Climbing Training and Specific Training Rules, in the principles below). How you find them depends on the type:
  - a climb: talk to people who have climbed it, watch videos, read guidebook comments, and time how long climbers stay on the wall in the videos ("Using the goal" in the 12-week note); to rebuild its crux, measure the angle, the holds and the distances between them (the simulation section of [General, Specific and Simulation Training](../principles/whole_plan/general/general-specific-and-simulation-training.md));
  - a trip: the daily load, such as 6–8 hard classics every other day in Rocklands or about 400 m a day with V7 cruxes on El Cap (Specific Training Rules, below), and the grip the area needs ([Finger Strength Training: Methods, Grip Types and Progressive Overload](../principles/finger_strength/general/finger-strength-training.md));
  - a competition: one or two days of many blocks and moves, so long-session fatigue tolerance ([Structuring Climbing Training: Session Length, Split Days and Weekly Workload](../content/06_weekly_structure/structuring-climbing-training.md)); step 05 plans the taper for a competition or a trip ([Tapering Before a Competition or a Climbing Trip](../content/05_periodization/general/tapering-before-a-competition-or-a-climbing-trip.md));
  - a grade: a grade alone names no demand, so picture the skills and strengths of a climber who climbs it (step 1 of [Process Versus Outcome Goals](../content/01_climbing_goal/process-versus-outcome-goals.md)); for boulderers, step 02 compares your test scores with the grade predictors;
  - a general goal: first make it measurable by describing the climber you want to be with a grade on that terrain, a basic test and a movement goal, such as completing most steep V3s in the gym with controlled feet on the flash (step 1 of the 3-steps note); split a broad goal into its parts and give the weakest part the short-term goals (the all-round climber in [SMART Goals for Climbing](../content/01_climbing_goal/smart-goals-for-climbing.md)); then list the adaptations it needs, not the exercises ("adaptations, not exercises" in Specificity in Climbing Training, below), with the discipline notes below for bouldering and sport;
  - steep terrain, as an example of a general goal: [End-to-End Tension for Steep Bouldering](../principles/antagonists_and_core/boulder/end-to-end-tension-for-steep-bouldering.md) (the posterior chain working with the abs, pectorals, hip flexors and biceps), [Choosing Exercises That Transfer to Climbing: Underclings and Steep Lock-offs](../principles/strength_and_pulling/general/choosing-exercises-that-transfer-to-climbing.md) (rowing and rotational pulling more than pull-ups) and [Planning Bouldering Training: Beyond Finger Strength](../principles/whole_plan/boulder/planning-bouldering-training-beyond-finger-strength.md) (finger strength and its fatigability, end-to-end tension, mobility and flexibility).
- **Principles:** [Specific Training Rules for a Climbing Goal: Specificity, Volume and Intensity, and Your Super Power](../principles/whole_plan/general/specific-training-rules-for-a-climbing-goal.md) (match training volume and intensity to the goal's daily demand); [Specificity in Climbing Training](../principles/whole_plan/general/specificity-in-climbing-training.md) (angle, hold type and number of moves first; adaptations, not exercises); [Six Rules for an Effective Training Framework](../principles/whole_plan/general/six-rules-for-an-effective-training-framework.md) (rule 1, have a reason); the discipline notes [How Training for Bouldering Differs from Training for Routes](../principles/whole_plan/boulder/how-training-for-bouldering-differs-from-training-for-routes.md) and [Planning Sport Climbing Training: Pacing and Recruitment](../principles/whole_plan/sport/planning-sport-climbing-training.md).

## 02 Assessment

- **Why here:** step 2 of program design (current status), and the "before" in step 1 of the 3-steps note.
- For a first assessment, use 4–5 tests chosen from your weaknesses (ask three or more friends) and from the demands of 2–3 goals: [Assessing Your Starting Point Before Writing a Training Plan](../content/02_assessment/general/assessing-your-starting-point-before-writing-a-training-plan.md).
- Add a grade grid of the last three months, three repeatable training metrics and two moves you find hard (step 1 of the 3-steps note).
- Test numbers judge the training; route and boulder pyramids judge performance: [Are We Assessing the Right Things?](../content/02_assessment/general/are-we-assessing-the-right-things.md).
- Finger and endurance tests: [Max Hang Test to Set Finger Training Loads](../content/02_assessment/general/max-hang-test-to-set-finger-training-loads.md), [Three Hangboard Tests for Finger Strength and Endurance](../content/02_assessment/general/three-hangboard-tests-for-finger-strength-and-endurance.md), [Fatigue Curve Test for Aerobic Fitness](../content/02_assessment/general/fatigue-curve-test-for-aerobic-fitness.md), and [No-Hang Finger Strength Testing](../content/02_assessment/injured/no-hang-finger-strength-testing.md) when injured.
- Strength and mobility benchmarks: [Foundation Strength Standards: Six Movement Benchmarks](../content/02_assessment/general/foundation-strength-standards.md) (squat, hinge, press, pull, midsection, fingers); [Hip and Shoulder Mobility Tests: Frog Stretch and Fist Reach](../content/02_assessment/general/hip-and-shoulder-mobility-tests.md).
- Boulderers: [Which Tests Predict Bouldering Grade at Each Level](../content/02_assessment/boulder/which-tests-predict-bouldering-grade-at-each-level.md) (finger strength, then pulling strength, at each grade level). When gym grades vary, or the goal is a terrain or a style rather than a grade, log sessions by movement pattern and use benchmark problems by style instead of gym grades: [Training and Tracking Progress When Gym Grades Vary](../content/02_assessment/boulder/training-and-tracking-progress-when-gym-grades-vary.md).
- Female climbers: track the cycle for 3–6 months before using it in the plan: [Starting to Track Your Menstrual Cycle](../content/02_assessment/female/starting-to-track-your-menstrual-cycle.md).
- **Principles:** [Specificity in Climbing Training](../principles/whole_plan/general/specificity-in-climbing-training.md): test what the goal needs.

## 03 Time and resources

- **Why here:** program design step 3 is "the time frame and the things available to you", after the goal and the current status. The 3-steps note sets the timescale and lists life commitments (its step 2) before it sets priorities. The 12-week note looks at lifestyle constraints "before any structure", as the key to consistency.
- Count the weeks from now to the goal window from step 01, and check that each gain you want fits that time: power endurance can be built in 6–8 weeks, lasting strength takes much longer, newer trainees gain faster, and a big jump in grade takes years (the table in step 2 of the 3-steps note). The time frame also sets how detailed the plan needs to be (program design).
- List the life commitments over the plan and rate each one's impact on time, recovery, stress and nutrition; three big stresses in one month call for a reality check (3-steps note). Mark holidays, busy work periods and trips now, so step 05 can plan around them.
- Find the fixed weekly schedule (the days and hours you can train) and where added training minutes could go later: [Developmental, Maintenance and Recovery Sessions in the Week](../content/06_weekly_structure/developmental-maintenance-and-recovery-sessions-in-the-week.md). Unstructured or social climbing that matters to you goes in as a priority (12-week note).
- With children, travel or a demanding job: [Training Around Family, Travel and Busy Schedules](../content/03_time_and_resources/training-around-family-travel-and-busy-schedules.md).
- Facilities and equipment decide which sessions and exercises you can use. Boards: [Why Climb on a Training Board](../content/03_time_and_resources/why-climb-on-a-training-board.md), [Kilter Board vs Tension Board 2: Grade-by-Grade Comparison](../content/03_time_and_resources/kilter-board-vs-tension-board-2-grade-by-grade-comparison.md), [A 60° Board for Foot Tension in Volume Sessions](../content/03_time_and_resources/a-60-degree-board-for-foot-tension-in-volume-sessions.md). Sessions and exercises for limited set-ups carry a For tag in INDEX.md, such as "limited gym access", "only a board available" or "no weight room".
- Training age sets the pace of progress: build from your own history, not someone else's ([Progressive Overload From Your Own Training History: Volume First, Intensity Later](../content/08_load_and_recovery/progression/progressive-overload-from-your-own-training-history.md)). Hangboarding as a regular component suits strong climbers with 6+ years of experience ([Hangboarding: Risk vs. Reward](../principles/finger_strength/general/hangboarding-risk-vs-reward.md)).
- **Principles:** [Less Is More: Adding New Training Habits Slowly](../principles/whole_plan/general/less-is-more-adding-new-training-habits-slowly.md) (your schedule decides how much new training sticks); [Patterns Behind Very Strong and Very Weak Fingers](../principles/finger_strength/general/patterns-behind-very-strong-and-very-weak-fingers.md) (too much going on in work or other sports is a weak-finger pattern).

## 04 Training focus

- **Why here:** step 3 of the 3-steps note opens by setting priorities from the "after" picture and your strengths and weaknesses. [Planning the Journey: From Assessment to a Realistic Timeline](general/planning-the-journey.md) sets intermediate goals after the assessment and makes the weaknesses the main focus.
- A weakness that would stop you meeting the goal's demands comes first; a strength the goal hardly needs comes last. Train your weaknesses and perform in your strengths (the assessing note in step 02). Keep only the gains that step 03 found realistic in the time you have.
- Compare the skills and strengths the goal demands (step 01) with yours, and pick the drills, exercises and routines that close the gap (steps 2 and 3 of [Process Versus Outcome Goals](../content/01_climbing_goal/process-versus-outcome-goals.md)).
- Choose two focuses, up to four, or even one, and keep a first self-written plan simple: [Intermediate Training Goals and How Many Training Focuses to Choose](../content/04_training_focus/general/intermediate-training-goals-and-how-many-focuses-to-choose.md).
- Make each focus a measurable training goal with progressions on a timeline worked backwards. Move to the next progression after two sessions in a row at the current one: [Setting Training Goals](../content/04_training_focus/general/setting-training-goals.md).
- Worked example of reading tests to choose what to improve (10 mm against 20 mm edges, fitness, mobility): [Reviewing Test Results Before a Bouldering Trip: Edge Sizes, Fitness and Mobility](../content/04_training_focus/boulder/reviewing-test-results-before-a-bouldering-trip.md).
- Advanced climbers: put about 70% of projects in your antistyle: [Training Your Climbing Antistyle](../content/04_training_focus/general/training-your-climbing-antistyle.md).
- **Principles:** [Six Rules for an Effective Training Framework](../principles/whole_plan/general/six-rules-for-an-effective-training-framework.md) (rule 2, one or two qualities per program); [One Goal Per Training Cycle](../principles/whole_plan/general/one-goal-per-training-cycle.md); [Where Training Begins: Small Steps, One Focus](../principles/whole_plan/general/where-training-begins-small-steps-one-focus.md). Then the quality folder for each candidate focus, e.g. [Finger Strength vs Climbing Grade: Climbing Harder With Weak Fingers](../principles/finger_strength/general/finger-strength-vs-climbing-grade.md), [Should Climbers Weight Train? Reasons For and Against](../principles/strength_and_pulling/general/should-climbers-weight-train-reasons-for-and-against.md), [Power Endurance: What It Is and How Long to Train It](../principles/power_endurance/power-endurance-what-it-is-and-how-long-to-train-it.md), [Endurance Training for Boulderers Is Underrated](../principles/endurance/general/endurance-training-for-boulderers-is-underrated.md), [Stretching and Flexibility Are Underrated](../principles/mobility/general/stretching-and-flexibility-are-underrated.md).

## 05 Periodization

- **Why here:** program design steps 3–4 (time frame, then plan backwards), step 2 of the 3-steps note (timescale: macrocycle and mesocycles), and step 1 of the 12-week method (basic structure).
- Plan backwards from the goal date, with the life events from step 03 already on the calendar.
- A basic format: rest weeks 3:1 or 4:1, volume first and intensity later, more specific towards the end: [Periodizing a Self-Written Training Plan: Rest Weeks, Specificity and Volume to Intensity](../content/05_periodization/general/periodizing-a-self-written-training-plan.md).
- Plan the year roughly in 4-week blocks and only the next block in detail. Give each block a main focus, one technical skill and one mental skill, and expect to lose about 4 weeks a year: [Planning a Realistic Year of Training: Periodization and 4-Week Blocks](../content/05_periodization/general/planning-a-realistic-year-of-training.md). One shape for a 4-week block: [The 4-Week Cycle: Ease In, Hardest in Week 3, Coast Into the Deload](../content/05_periodization/general/the-4-week-cycle.md).
- General, then specific, then simulation: [A Year of General, Specific and Simulation Training](../content/05_periodization/general/a-year-of-general-specific-and-simulation-training.md).
- Strength in the base periods, power 2–6 weeks before the goal: [Strength in Base Periods, Power 2–6 Weeks Before a Goal](../content/05_periodization/general/strength-in-base-periods-power-before-a-goal.md). Strength across the year: [Off-the-Wall Strength Training Through the Year](../content/05_periodization/general/off-the-wall-strength-training-through-the-year.md), [The Strength Continuum: How Much and How Often to Lift](../content/05_periodization/general/the-strength-continuum-how-much-and-how-often-to-lift.md).
- Endurance over 12 weeks: [The 8-8-8 Plan: 12 Weeks for Long-Term Endurance](../content/05_periodization/general/the-8-8-8-plan.md). Power endurance blocks are short: see "How long to train power endurance" below.
- Taper before the goal: [Tapering Before a Competition or a Climbing Trip](../content/05_periodization/general/tapering-before-a-competition-or-a-climbing-trip.md), for a competition (an overreach week, then two weeks of reduced volume) or a trip (the week before: intensity at about 85% of normal max, volume halved). The 12-week note uses one taper week.
- The folder also covers the outdoor season, maintenance and breaks.
- Female climbers: periodise first, then fit the plan to the cycle: [Periodise Your Training First: Record, Rate and Schedule Deload Weeks](../content/05_periodization/general/periodise-your-training-first.md), [Before Syncing Training to Your Cycle: Contraception and Symptom Tracking](../content/05_periodization/female/before-syncing-training-to-your-cycle.md).
- Choice of model: see "Where the sources disagree" below.
- **Principles:** [General, Specific and Simulation Training](../principles/whole_plan/general/general-specific-and-simulation-training.md); [Six Rules for an Effective Training Framework](../principles/whole_plan/general/six-rules-for-an-effective-training-framework.md) (rule 3, begin with the end in mind; rule 4, what goes up must come down); [One Goal Per Training Cycle](../principles/whole_plan/general/one-goal-per-training-cycle.md).

## 06 Weekly structure

- **Why here:** program design plans weeks before sessions. The 3-steps note works from the schedule first and decides the number of sessions before choosing them. The 12-week note puts the fixed sessions in first.
- Put the fixed sessions in first: open or outdoor sessions, and the trip itself.
- Set the number of sessions as a maximum, e.g. at least one open session, three structured climbing sessions, two conditioning sessions (3-steps note).
- Label each session developmental, maintenance or recovery: [Developmental, Maintenance and Recovery Sessions in the Week](../content/06_weekly_structure/developmental-maintenance-and-recovery-sessions-in-the-week.md).
- How often: max hangs 1–2 times a week at most, 48–72 hours between projecting days, outdoor days counted as high-load sessions: [How Often to Climb and Train: Frequency, Repetition and Outdoor Days](../content/06_weekly_structure/how-often-to-climb-and-train.md). By grade: [Training Days per Week and Session Focus by Grade](../content/06_weekly_structure/training-days-per-week-and-session-focus-by-grade.md). Endurance: route climbers training three times a week need at least two sessions with endurance work, boulderers at least one: [Endurance Sessions per Week for Route Climbers and Boulderers](../content/06_weekly_structure/endurance-sessions-per-week-for-route-climbers-and-boulderers.md). Boards: a max-strength or power day, and off the board on other days: [How Often to Use a Training Board: Grip Overlap and the Best Uses](../content/06_weekly_structure/how-often-to-use-a-training-board.md).
- Across the week: hardest sessions at the start of the week, high intensity only when recovered, an outdoor day or competition counted as one of the high-intensity sessions: [Structuring Climbing Training: Session Length, Split Days and Weekly Workload](../content/06_weekly_structure/structuring-climbing-training.md). When strength is the focus, put strength and endurance on different days: [Session Length and Combining Training Modes: The Interference Effect](../content/06_weekly_structure/session-length-and-combining-training-modes.md). Supplemental strength after climbing or the next day, never where the next climbing session depends on fresh muscles: [Scheduling General Strength Alongside Climbing](../content/06_weekly_structure/scheduling-general-strength-alongside-climbing.md). Make one session longer and one shorter instead of identical sessions: [Vary Volume Between Sessions: One 20% Longer, One 20% Shorter](../content/06_weekly_structure/vary-volume-between-sessions.md).
- The 3-steps note lays out the week after the sessions are chosen: come back here at the end of step 07.
- **Principles:** [Only Green Days: Train Only When You Can Adapt](../principles/whole_plan/general/only-green-days-train-only-when-you-can-adapt.md); [Rest or Climb: When Tired, Back Off](../principles/whole_plan/general/rest-or-climb-when-tired-back-off.md); [Six Rules for an Effective Training Framework](../principles/whole_plan/general/six-rules-for-an-effective-training-framework.md) (rule 5, recovery is a real thing).

## 07 Sessions

- **Why here:** the 3-steps note fills in the big picture of each session before its details, and the 12-week note plans climbing sessions before conditioning. Program design calls the session the building block of the whole program.
- **What is a session here:** this folder holds what a whole session is for and how its parts fit: session types for open sessions (what the session is for and how to judge it), templates that arrange several parts, warm-ups, and the rules for building a session (`session_design/`). One exercise, drill or workout with a set dose is in step 09, however long it is and even when it fills a session on its own: the 12-week plan uses 6-in-6, 1 On 1 Off and ARC as parts of sessions.
- Plan the climbing sessions first, then the conditioning (12-week note).
- Name the adaptation each session is for, then its type: warm-up, finger strength, strength and pulling, power, endurance, technique and skill, mobility, or combined (the subfolders).
- Keep conditioning, strength and power, and skill in separate sessions: [Three Styles of Bouldering Session](../content/07_sessions/combined/general/three-styles-of-bouldering-session.md).
- Build each session from the rules in `session_design/`:
  - the top priority first, supplemental work after: [Session Priority Order: Top Priority First](../content/07_sessions/session_design/general/session-priority-order.md);
  - finger-intensive work first after a thorough warm-up, endurance in the middle, general strength and conditioning last: [Order Within a Session: Finger-Intensive Work First, Conditioning Last](../content/07_sessions/session_design/general/order-within-a-session.md); in bouldering, the most skill-dependent work first: [Bouldering Session Order: Most Skill-Dependent First](../content/07_sessions/session_design/boulder/bouldering-session-order.md);
  - one stimulus per session, and 3–4 fingerboard sets inside a session or 6–8 on a day of its own: [How to Structure Your Training](../content/07_sessions/session_design/general/how-to-structure-your-training.md);
  - power first, a progressive warm-up and long rests when strength and power share a session: [Designing a Session With Strength and Power](../content/07_sessions/session_design/general/designing-a-session-with-strength-and-power.md); power work after climbing when power is a weakness but not the priority: [Power Training When Power Is a Weakness](../content/07_sessions/session_design/general/power-training-when-power-is-a-weakness.md);
  - weights before or after climbing, by the session's goal: [Weights Before or After Climbing](../content/07_sessions/session_design/general/weights-before-or-after-climbing.md) (the sources disagree: see below).
- Do a session at least 10 and at most 20 times in a cycle before changing it: [Periodization Basics: The Training Pie, Variation, Specificity and Simplicity](../content/05_periodization/general/periodization-basics.md). The 12-week note changes the sessions each block, for a fresh stimulus and motivation.
- Then place the sessions in the week with the rules from step 06.
- **Principles:** [Specificity in Climbing Training](../principles/whole_plan/general/specificity-in-climbing-training.md) (adaptations, not exercises: name the adaptation first); [Energy Systems as Gears: Power, Endurance and Duration](../principles/whole_plan/general/energy-systems-as-gears.md) (effort length and intensity decide which system a session trains); the quality folder for each focus.

## 08 Load and recovery

- **Why here:** program design sets reps or time under load, sets, loading, tempo and rest, in that order, before choosing the exercise. The assessing note in step 02 names progressive overload and recovery as the second and third parts of writing a plan.
- The folder follows the step's three outputs: `intensity/` (how hard: RPE, reps in reserve, bar speed, session bounds, when to stop), `progression/` (how the load grows) and `recovery/` (rest days, deloads, sleep). Each has a `female/` subfolder for adjustments across the menstrual cycle where there are any.
- Effort length from the energy systems: up to about 12 seconds, 12–90 seconds, over 90 seconds: [Energy Systems as Gears: Power, Endurance and Duration](../principles/whole_plan/general/energy-systems-as-gears.md) (other sources give other durations: see below).
- Reps, sets and rest for one quality are in its principles folder. For example, maximum strength uses near-maximal efforts of about 10 seconds (3–5 reps) with at least 3 minutes of rest: [Maximum Strength Training for Fingers and Pulling](../principles/finger_strength/general/maximum-strength-training-for-fingers-and-pulling.md). Power endurance is built in short cycles: [Power Endurance Cycles: Where They Fit and How Much](../principles/power_endurance/power-endurance-cycles.md).
- Intensity: control it with RPE (most sessions at RPE 6–8), reps in reserve or bar speed: [Using RPE to Measure and Manage Climbing Training Intensity](../content/08_load_and_recovery/intensity/general/using-rpe-to-measure-and-manage-climbing-training-intensity.md), [Training with Reps in Reserve (RIR)](../content/08_load_and_recovery/intensity/general/training-with-reps-in-reserve-rir.md), [Velocity-Based Training: Stop the Set When Speed Drops](../content/08_load_and_recovery/intensity/general/velocity-based-training-stop-the-set-when-speed-drops.md). Give each session an upper and a lower bound: [Upper and Lower Bounds for Sessions, and Training Without Fatigue](../content/08_load_and_recovery/intensity/general/upper-and-lower-bounds-for-sessions-and-training-without-fatigue.md). Plan in advance when to stop: [Stopping a Session Before You Overtrain](../content/08_load_and_recovery/intensity/general/stopping-a-session-before-you-overtrain.md).
- Progression: start a couple of clicks easier than you can do and progress session by session: [From Exercising to Training](../principles/whole_plan/general/from-exercising-to-training.md). Progress at a rate that fits your own history, volume first and intensity later: [Progressive Overload From Your Own Training History: Volume First, Intensity Later](../content/08_load_and_recovery/progression/progressive-overload-from-your-own-training-history.md). Adding load is the last thing to try: [Advancing Training Load](../content/08_load_and_recovery/progression/advancing-training-load.md).
- Endurance progresses in four ways (harder climbing, longer intervals, shorter or more active rests, more repetitions), progressed one at a time. Three sources give them, each with details the others lack: [Four Ways to Progress Endurance Intervals](../content/08_load_and_recovery/progression/four-ways-to-progress-endurance-intervals.md), [Four Ways to Progress Endurance Training](../content/08_load_and_recovery/progression/four-ways-to-progress-endurance-training.md), and the "four ways to progress a boulder session" table in [Endurance Training: Capacity and Power](../principles/endurance/general/endurance-training-capacity-and-power.md).
- Recovery: what goes in a deload, and how much to cut when fatigue appears: [Deload Weeks: When and How to Take Them](../content/08_load_and_recovery/recovery/general/deload-weeks-when-and-how-to-take-them.md), [How to Take a Deload Week: Half Intensity, Half Volume](../content/08_load_and_recovery/recovery/general/how-to-take-a-deload-week.md). Grow weekly load by 5–10% at a submaximal RPE, with complete rest on rest days: [Rest Is Training: Rest Days, Building Capacity and Deload Weeks](../content/08_load_and_recovery/recovery/general/rest-is-training.md).
- **Principles:** [Progressive Overload Is Underrated](../principles/whole_plan/general/progressive-overload-is-underrated.md); [Only Green Days: Train Only When You Can Adapt](../principles/whole_plan/general/only-green-days-train-only-when-you-can-adapt.md); [Six Rules for an Effective Training Framework](../principles/whole_plan/general/six-rules-for-an-effective-training-framework.md) (rule 6, get one better).

## 09 Exercises

- **Why last:** program design decides the exercise last, and any exercise that gives the right intensity does the job. The 3-steps note chooses the details from the priorities.
- Pick from the folder for the quality, and the audience subfolder where there is one. Workouts that fill a whole climbing session are here too (for example ARC, 1 On 1 Off, 4x4s, intensive triples).
- Most exercise notes state their own sets, reps and rest: check they match the dose set in step 08.
- Choosing strength exercises: identify the movement, the joints and the prime movers, then pick a simple, stable exercise you can overload. Look at how the movement is used on rock: [Selecting Strength Exercises for a Climbing Move: Movement, Joints, Muscles, Exercise and Velocity](../principles/strength_and_pulling/general/selecting-strength-exercises-for-a-climbing-move.md), [Choosing Exercises That Transfer to Climbing: Underclings and Steep Lock-offs](../principles/strength_and_pulling/general/choosing-exercises-that-transfer-to-climbing.md).
- Choosing a finger grip: train the grip your goal demands: [Finger Strength Training: Methods, Grip Types and Progressive Overload](../principles/finger_strength/general/finger-strength-training.md).
- Prefer sessions and exercises you enjoy, so you stay consistent (3-steps note, motivation).
- **Principles:** [Specificity in Climbing Training](../principles/whole_plan/general/specificity-in-climbing-training.md).

## 10 Check and review

- **Why here:** [How to Judge Whether a Training Plan Works](../content/10_check_and_review/how-to-judge-whether-a-training-plan-works.md); [Planning the Journey: From Assessment to a Realistic Timeline](general/planning-the-journey.md) (monitor progress against the timeline and reconsider); [Planning a Realistic Year of Training: Periodization and 4-Week Blocks](../content/05_periodization/general/planning-a-realistic-year-of-training.md) (each block is a stopping point to review).
- Before starting, check the four dimensions (structural, physiological, goal alignment, behavioural) and the faults: unsafe progressions, unrealistic weeks, inconsistent load, duplicated or missing categories, illogical combinations, scheduling conflicts.
- For every piece of training, ask what stimulus it gives and whether that matches the goal. Don't stack maximal sessions back to back, and put the sessions you can drop at the end of the week: [Troubleshooting a Training Week: Three Worked Examples](../content/10_check_and_review/troubleshooting-a-training-week-three-worked-examples.md).
- While training, log the sessions and the RPE, and re-run the step-02 tests at the start and end of a block.
- At the end of each block, change the plan only when a pattern repeats (missed days, a steady RPE difference, building fatigue), not for one bad session. Change at least one variable each cycle, but not many at once, and add one or two new things at a time.
- At the end of a season, check the training goals and how they carried over to rock: [Reviewing Training Goals](../content/10_check_and_review/reviewing-training-goals.md).
- **Principles:** [Why Climbing Plateaus Happen and How to Break Out of One](../principles/whole_plan/general/why-climbing-plateaus-happen-and-how-to-break-out-of-one.md); [Less Is More: Adding New Training Habits Slowly](../principles/whole_plan/general/less-is-more-adding-new-training-habits-slowly.md).

## Where the sources disagree

Each view comes with the condition its source gives. Where a source gives no condition, none is added here.

### How many qualities to train at once

- One at a time: strength, power and endurance are built separately, one priority per 4-week phase, then maintained together: [One Goal Per Training Cycle](../principles/whole_plan/general/one-goal-per-training-cycle.md). Pick one thing and make small steps, especially when progress has stalled: [Where Training Begins: Small Steps, One Focus](../principles/whole_plan/general/where-training-begins-small-steps-one-focus.md).
- One or two per program: [Six Rules for an Effective Training Framework](../principles/whole_plan/general/six-rules-for-an-effective-training-framework.md).
- Two, up to four, or one; simple for a first self-written plan: [Intermediate Training Goals and How Many Training Focuses to Choose](../content/04_training_focus/general/intermediate-training-goals-and-how-many-focuses-to-choose.md).
- Several at once is fine for beginner to advanced climbers whose goals are focused and few; interference matters at a very advanced or elite level: [Training Blocks, Concurrent Training and Deloads](../content/05_periodization/general/training-blocks-concurrent-training-and-deloads.md).

### Which periodization model

- Switch qualities session by session (non-linear), and move to block periodization (two developmental sessions for the focus, one maintenance session) when that plateaus. Blocks with a single focus don't suit climbing: [Planning a Realistic Year of Training: Periodization and 4-Week Blocks](../content/05_periodization/general/planning-a-realistic-year-of-training.md).
- For performing year-round: 3 weeks of strength, 3 weeks of power, 1 week of recovery: [Alternating Linear Periodization](../content/05_periodization/general/alternating-linear-periodization.md). Or 3 weeks of strength and power, then 3 weeks of endurance and power endurance, twice, then a recovery week before performing: [Alternating Linear Periodization: Strength and Power vs Endurance](../content/05_periodization/general/alternating-linear-periodization-strength-and-power-vs-endurance.md).
- Build a strength base, then stack specific conditioning on low-volume strength work: [Stacking Phases: Specific Conditioning on a Strength Base](../content/05_periodization/general/stacking-phases.md).
- The detail of the models matters only for the best athletes; keep it simple and consistent: [Periodization Basics: The Training Pie, Variation, Specificity and Simplicity](../content/05_periodization/general/periodization-basics.md).

### How often to deload

- 3 weeks on and 1 off, or 4 on and 1 off: [Periodizing a Self-Written Training Plan: Rest Weeks, Specificity and Volume to Intensity](../content/05_periodization/general/periodizing-a-self-written-training-plan.md).
- 3:1 is the most common; 2:1 may suit some people better: [Periodise Your Training First: Record, Rate and Schedule Deload Weeks](../content/05_periodization/general/periodise-your-training-first.md).
- Every 3–4 weeks at about 50% of that period's peak load: [Training Blocks, Concurrent Training and Deloads](../content/05_periodization/general/training-blocks-concurrent-training-and-deloads.md). Every 4 weeks: [Rest Is Training: Rest Days, Building Capacity and Deload Weeks](../content/08_load_and_recovery/recovery/general/rest-is-training.md), and the deload ends each [4-week cycle](../content/05_periodization/general/the-4-week-cycle.md).
- Planned deloads every 6–12 weeks for most climbers, plus extra deloads when performance drops or fatigue rises. The same note warns that needing a deload every 3–4 weeks is a reason to rethink training or recovery habits: [Deload Weeks: When and How to Take Them](../content/08_load_and_recovery/recovery/general/deload-weeks-when-and-how-to-take-them.md). The 12-week note uses deload weeks mainly as a mental break from structured training.
- During the outdoor season, more often (e.g. 2 weeks on, 1 off): [Maintenance Training During the Outdoor Season](../content/05_periodization/general/maintenance-training-during-the-outdoor-season.md).

### How long to train power endurance

- Two to four weeks ahead of a period of performance climbing, at most two or three sessions a week; longer commonly leads to overtraining: [Power Endurance: What It Is and How Long to Train It](../principles/power_endurance/power-endurance-what-it-is-and-how-long-to-train-it.md).
- A cycle of 6–8 progressive sessions, no more than two a week, then a little every few weeks for the rest of the year: [Power Endurance Cycles: Where They Fit and How Much](../principles/power_endurance/power-endurance-cycles.md).
- The short end first, which takes at least 18 weeks to adapt, then the long end in 6–8 weeks, only after a base of aerobic and anaerobic capacity: [Power Endurance: Short End First, Then Long End](../principles/power_endurance/power-endurance-short-end-first-then-long-end.md).
- No power endurance program longer than a month; anaerobic training tops out after 4–6 hard sessions of a type: [The Fundamental Factors of Climbing Endurance](../principles/endurance/general/the-fundamental-factors-of-climbing-endurance.md).
- Aerobic power ("what most mean by power endurance") 2–3 times a week for 3 weeks: [Endurance Workouts by Quality, With Two 8-Session Progressions](../content/09_exercises/endurance/endurance-workouts-by-quality.md); focus on the powers for 3–4 weeks: [Endurance Training: Capacity and Power](../principles/endurance/general/endurance-training-capacity-and-power.md).
- Power endurance fitness can be built in 6 to 8 weeks: [Writing Your Own Climbing Training Plan in 3 Steps](general/writing-your-own-climbing-training-plan-in-3-steps.md).

### How long each energy system lasts

- Alactic up to about 12 seconds, lactic from 12 to about 90 seconds, aerobic beyond 90 seconds; intensity decides which one you train: [Energy Systems as Gears: Power, Endurance and Duration](../principles/whole_plan/general/energy-systems-as-gears.md).
- System 2 (lactic) supplies most of the energy for about a minute: [The Three Energy Systems as the Gears of a Car](../principles/whole_plan/general/the-three-energy-systems-as-the-gears-of-a-car.md). This note and the one above come from the same author and use the same picture; this one adds that your easy grade is aerobic territory.
- Alactic 8–15 seconds, glycolytic 30–90 seconds and 1–2 minutes at most: [Training the Aerobic Energy System for Climbing](../principles/endurance/general/training-the-aerobic-energy-system-for-climbing.md).
- The systems work together, with no switch at set times: [Energy Systems in Climbing](../principles/whole_plan/general/energy-systems-in-climbing.md).
- Endurance sessions by work time: under 10 seconds, 10–40 seconds, up to about 10 minutes: [Fundamentals of Endurance](../principles/endurance/general/fundamentals-of-endurance.md).

### Weights before or after climbing

- Exercises first when the session's goal is building strength in muscles and tendons; climbing first for climbing and technique: [Weights Before or After Climbing](../content/07_sessions/session_design/general/weights-before-or-after-climbing.md).
- Weight training goes towards the end even when you want max strength, because the climbing-specific work is the priority: [Order Within a Session: Finger-Intensive Work First, Conditioning Last](../content/07_sessions/session_design/general/order-within-a-session.md).
- The top priority first and supplemental training after climbing, with a small activation dose at the start; true low-rep power work before the session, high-rep work after: [Session Priority Order: Top Priority First](../content/07_sessions/session_design/general/session-priority-order.md).
- Supplemental strength after the climbing session or the next day: [Scheduling General Strength Alongside Climbing](../content/06_weekly_structure/scheduling-general-strength-alongside-climbing.md).

### When beginners start hangboarding

- Beginners shouldn't fingerboard train; experienced climbers with no recent finger, elbow or shoulder injury can use one maximum-strength protocol twice a week: [Four Fingerboard Protocols for Maximum Strength and Strength-Endurance](../content/09_exercises/finger_strength/general/four-fingerboard-protocols-for-maximum-strength-and-strength-endurance.md).
- Six months into climbing, don't hangboard at all; with weak fingers keep at least 75% climbing to 25% finger training: [Finger Training Questions: Grip Specificity, Light Hangs for Recovery, Weak Fingers and When to Start Hangboarding](../principles/finger_strength/general/finger-training-questions.md).
- Beginners use the hangboard as 2–5% of the warm-up: [Hangboarding vs Climbing for Finger Strength](../principles/finger_strength/general/hangboarding-vs-climbing-for-finger-strength.md).
- Hangboarding can be safe and effective at any level, including for new climbers: start on a 20 mm edge, 1–2 times a week: [Hangboarding Protocols for Beginners](../content/09_exercises/finger_strength/beginner/hangboarding-protocols-for-beginners.md).
- A gentle fingerboard warm-up from the very beginning, not as training: [Warming Up the Fingers on a Fingerboard](../content/07_sessions/warm_up/beginner/warming-up-the-fingers-on-a-fingerboard.md).
- Climb first, then vary grips, then add light hangboarding; after more than 6 months, if you can hang bodyweight on a 20 mm edge for 7 seconds, consider a max hang test: [Building Finger Strength in Your First Year of Climbing](../principles/finger_strength/beginner/building-finger-strength-in-your-first-year-of-climbing.md).
- Novices should approach hangboarding cautiously: [Hangboarding: Risk vs. Reward](../principles/finger_strength/general/hangboarding-risk-vs-reward.md).

### How many high-intensity finger sessions a week

- Max hangs at 90% or more effort 1–2 times a week at most: [How Often to Climb and Train: Frequency, Repetition and Outdoor Days](../content/06_weekly_structure/how-often-to-climb-and-train.md).
- High-intensity elements (fingerboarding, campusing, board climbing) up to about five a week at most; a couple of fingerboard sessions and one or two board sessions tend to be enough: [Structuring Climbing Training: Session Length, Split Days and Weekly Workload](../content/06_weekly_structure/structuring-climbing-training.md).
- One maximum-strength fingerboard protocol twice a week, with no more than four rigorous climbing and finger days a week: [Four Fingerboard Protocols for Maximum Strength and Strength-Endurance](../content/09_exercises/finger_strength/general/four-fingerboard-protocols-for-maximum-strength-and-strength-endurance.md).
- Two fingerboard sessions a week; more might overload the fingers or create monotony: [Two Fingerboard Sessions a Week Before a Bouldering Trip](../content/07_sessions/finger_strength/boulder/two-fingerboard-sessions-a-week-before-a-bouldering-trip.md).
- Three fingerboard sessions a week from week 3 of an 8-week ramp-up: [Tendon Strength Program: Open-Hand Max Hangs and Weighted Pull-ups](../content/09_exercises/finger_strength/general/tendon-strength-program.md).
- A board for a max-strength or a power day only: [How Often to Use a Training Board: Grip Overlap and the Best Uses](../content/06_weekly_structure/how-often-to-use-a-training-board.md).

## What a finished plan contains

Taken from "what a training plan must cover" in [How to Judge Whether a Training Plan Works](../content/10_check_and_review/how-to-judge-whether-a-training-plan-works.md), the 3-steps note and the 12-week example:

- The goal, its type, its date window, why it matters, and what it demands.
- Before-and-after measures: test scores, a grade grid or pyramid, and movement goals.
- The time available: weeks to the goal, days and hours each week, the facilities and equipment, and the life commitments and other constraints during the plan.
- The focuses, each with a measurable training goal and a timeline.
- The block calendar: each block's focus, the deload weeks and the taper.
- A weekly template for each block, including open or outdoor sessions and rest days.
- The sessions, each with its purpose, its parts in order, its dose (reps or time, sets, load, tempo, rest) and its exercises.
- Progression rules and safety limits, such as an upper limit on fingerboard and board volume.
- Test and review dates, and what would trigger a change to the plan.
