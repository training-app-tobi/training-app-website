# Principles of Training Program and Session Design

**Type:** Planning, Principles · **Trains:** General strength, Power, Endurance

**TL;DR:** Design any program in four steps: determine the goal, determine current training status, determine the time frame and what is available, then plan backward and execute forward. Design each session by six criteria, in order: repetitions or time under load, number of sets, loading, tempo / muscle action, rest, and only last the exercises. The session is the ultimate building block of performance: structure it properly and check for progress in its specific exercises.

## Four steps of program design
| Step | What to do |
|---|---|
| 1 | Determine the goal |
| 2 | Determine current training status |
| 3 | Determine the time frame and the things available to you |
| 4 | Plan backward and execute forward |

- Planning backward: look at the goal and the status needed to achieve it, then take the steps back from there to today.
- The time frame sets how detailed the plan must be: with 2.5 years to get ready for something you don't need to be as obsessive about the details as with 8–10 weeks.

## Designing a session: six criteria, in order
Within that framework, look at what the actual sessions need to look like to get the most out of gym time.

| Order | Criterion |
|---|---|
| 1 | Repetitions or time under load |
| 2 | Number of sets |
| 3 | Loading |
| 4 | Tempo / muscle action |
| 5 | Rest between sets and exercises |
| 6 | Exercises |

- **Warning:** A common mistake is using the exercise as the primary criterion when designing a session; exercises are the last thing to decide.

## 1. Repetitions or time under load
- Whatever the exercise, the most important part of the workout is how intensively the exercises are done.
- Example: "doing pull-ups" could train endurance or maximum strength, so start with load. If you load yourself to almost 100% of max and do pulling, you can use pull-ups, rows, landmine rows or Moon Board exercises: as long as the intensity is correct, you get the desired training outcome.
- If intensity is all over the place, you stimulate many different systems and don't get the expected outcome.

## 2. Number of sets
- In the weight room, e.g. 5 sets of 5 pull-ups; in a bouldering session, the number of problems done before taking a long break.
- The number of sets determines how much capacity is asked of the athlete and the biological load the athlete carries out of the session.
- **Warning:** Long sets and long sessions take a long time to recover from; have a really good reason for them. Otherwise be conservative: keep sets short and targeted.

## 3. Loading
How heavy to go for the repetitions chosen in criterion 1. Decide whether you want long sessions that improve endurance and the ability to sustain high levels of work, or to push up max numbers.

| Loading choice | What it does |
|---|---|
| At limit on every set (heavy) | Pushes up max numbers |
| Slightly lower levels (lighter) | Allows more sets and works capacity better |

| Session | How to do it | What it builds |
|---|---|---|
| Long bouldering session (e.g. 3 hours) | You can't go anywhere close to maximum bouldering ability | Capacity |
| Absolute maximum limit | Warm up thoroughly, target limit level for only 30–45 minutes, then move on to other training or to lower intensity | Maximum level |

## 4. Tempo of movement / muscle action
| Tempo | Develops |
|---|---|
| Slow | Control |
| Explosive | The ability to execute more powerfully |

- To develop holding lock-offs, holding positions, keeping the hips in and similar, design the session so many exercises are done more slowly. Otherwise people fall into a comfortable tempo, usually the one where they already develop most and are already strongest.

## 5. Rest between sets and between exercises
- Rest changes a workout profoundly; very long rests between exercises vs. very little rest makes it much harder.
- Getting an athlete to slow down and take a bit more time between each thing often gives much higher performance throughout the workout.

| Goal | Rest between sets and exercises |
|---|---|
| More muscular endurance | Shorter rests |
| More strength | Longer rests (general rule) |

## 6. Exercises
- Choose the exercise that fits each part of the workout (e.g. pull-up here, squat here, core work at this part).
- This gives the whole picture of what you want from the training, and you can easily substitute different exercises and still get the proper benefit.

## Planning from career to session
| Scale | What to plan |
|---|---|
| Career | What you want from your career |
| Next 3 years | What you want in the next 3 years |
| Next 1 year | What you want in the next year; programming drops in the specific stimuli the athlete should experience within this period |
| Months | Single months of training |
| Weeks | Single weeks of training |
| Sessions | Single sessions of training |

- The session is the ultimate building block of performance: failing to structure sessions properly means failing to structure the whole program.
- Take the time to put structure in place, then check whether you see progress in the specific exercises within the session.
