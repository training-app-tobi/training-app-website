# Writing Your Own Climbing Training Plan in 3 Steps

**Type:** Planning · **Trains:** Finger strength, General strength, Endurance, Technique · **For:** Self-coached climbers

**TL;DR:** Write a plan in three steps, in this order: (1) measure where you are now and where you want to be (grade, basic tests, movement); (2) set the timescale, with a goal window, priorities and life commitments; (3) only then choose and order sessions to fit your schedule, facilities, recovery and motivation. Build finger load slowly, keep training varied, plan less rather than too much, and lay out the week around the open session, with rest before the key sessions.

## The method
- For climbers who write their own plan without a coach (for budget reasons or because they prefer to self-coach). It applies to sport climbers, trad climbers, boulderers or a mix, and is used to decide the strategy for the year or season. The aim is more efficient improvement in strength, fitness or any other aspect of climbing.

| Step | What it covers |
|---|---|
| 1. Before and after | Your performance metrics now and at the end of the plan |
| 2. Timescale | The length of the plan, including priorities |
| 3. Structure and session choice | Structure of the training and choice of individual sessions that fit your schedule and the facilities you have |

## Before you start
- **Warning:** Fingers are the most injured area of the body across all disciplines, so pay them the most attention. Most problems come from stepping up the loading of the plan too much, too quickly. Too much board climbing and fingerboarding harms finger health, so set an upper limit on them; finding it takes some experimentation. Always play it safe and build up slowly ("train like a tortoise, not a hare").
- **Warning:** Too much of the same thing: don't keep hammering one preferred niche style of climbing or training, e.g. climbing on a Moonboard for 9 out of 10 sessions. Too much of the same movement makes you very good at that movement but doesn't carry over to all climbing styles: too much time on a 45° board won't help on vertical limestone; lots of pull-ups won't necessarily give you better body tension. Keep training broad and always consider variety of movement; performance depends on it.
- Plan realism: you can only follow a plan that is completely realistic in the long term and won't be sabotaged in the first week. Many plans fail because they are unrealistic on several fronts. Be realistic about time: better to plan less and have extra open, unstructured climbing than to over-structure, assume you have more time, and fail. Open climbing is equally important for building a skill base and for enjoying climbing. If you have extra time, add open climbing.
- Motivation: training is often hard, so plan sessions that are motivating to follow; otherwise you are likely to lose your psych within the first month or so. Choose sessions you enjoy; it makes a big difference to performance. For core and flexibility, pick the sessions you most enjoy so you stay consistent. If you hate an endurance session, even one you think will help a lot, cycle it in and out with something more enjoyable or find a better replacement, so you go into it with motivation and focus.

## Step 1: Before and after
- A plan rests on knowing where you are now and what kind of climber you want to be at the end of the plan, with a way to track progress. This decides what to do next.

| Area | What to measure | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Grade | The most specific performance indicator for your goals. Use benchmark grades, or climb at the same crag or the same wall, to see if you have moved up | From V5 to V7 |
| Basic tests | Basic measures of strength, endurance and flexibility at the start and end of the plan: a fingerboard finger-strength test if you already have fingerboarding experience, otherwise pull-ups or flexibility. If you improved, the plan worked; if not, adapt it differently next time | Finger strength from hanging with +20 kg to +28 kg |
| Movement | Movement styles and proficiency in specific areas; improvement that may not show in grades | "Couldn't do a two-handed dyno in January, now I've done over 10 from V2 to V4"; from not being able to dyno to being more dynamic; if you climb a steep wall by jumping and cutting loose and want better body tension, repeat problems you have already done keeping your feet on the whole way and using different techniques |

| Task | How |
|---|---|
| Grade grid | Write down six grade boundaries from your current maximum down the scale (e.g. V6, V5, V4, V3, V2, V1). Make a grid of what you ticked in each grade over the last three months. Then track what you complete in three-month blocks for the next year |
| Three training metrics | Choose three basic training metrics to track. Keep them sport-specific and repeatable: a test you can only do once a year because the equipment is hard to get is no use |
| Two hard moves | List two types of climbing moves you find hard now and describe what success would look like, e.g. "right now I can't climb steep terrain without my feet cutting loose; in six months I want to complete most steep V3s in the gym with controlled feet on my flash goes" |

## Step 2: Timescale
- Define the edges of the plan (start and end); without them it is very hard to fill in the steps between. Many climbers treat training as open-ended and skip this.
- The timescale sets the big picture (macrocycle). In sport a macrocycle usually lasts 1 to 4 years depending on the sport; here it runs from the before to the after, usually several months. A month is usually a mesocycle.
- Check whether the improvement is realistic in the time you have:

| Quality or factor | Shorter time | Longer time |
|---|---|---|
| Type of gain | Power endurance fitness can be built in 6 to 8 weeks | Lasting strength gains (structural rather than neural) take much longer |
| Experience | Newer to training: expect gains much quicker | The more experienced and well trained you are and the longer you have climbed, the longer improvements take |
| Size of the gain | Improving finger strength a small amount to get ready for the outdoor season | Going from 7a to 9a takes several years |

- Timescale also depends on priority: how the training fits into your overall climbing and your life, how much time you have, and which before-and-after elements you must prioritise for the goal.
- Plan from the start around holidays, family commitments, work and travel, since they will affect training. Mark obstacles, good or bad, from the start and plan around them rather than ploughing through: e.g. a very busy work period in mid-summer; good conditions and weather for sea-cliff climbing at the end of summer, when that is what you want to be doing.

| Task | How |
|---|---|
| Goal window | Set a date for your goal and allow at least two weeks either side of it. Performance rarely comes exactly when planned, so plan a "window", not a narrow "slot" |
| Progress per metric | For each before-and-after metric, describe how progress towards it will look. This is often where you realise some of them are unrealistic |
| Life commitments | List all other life commitments during the training period and categorise each by its impact on your time, recovery, stress and nutrition. If three things have a big impact on stress in a single month, it is time for a reality check |

### Example timescale
| Element | Example |
|---|---|
| Time available | The goal route or problem comes into condition in autumn and it is now spring, so there are six months to work with |
| Gain to make | The route is very crimpy and small holds are a weakness, so finger strength is the gain to make |
| How to measure it | Finger-strength testing, or slowly increasing the load in training and recording it week on week, so you can see the training is working and adapt it if gains don't come |
| Priority now | Lots of time to train: focus on base training |
| Priority at the end | Peak performance on the goal route |

- **Warning:** Take it slowly and look for small increments of gain. With six months available there is no point trying to make the gains in three: that is too quick and more likely to upset the plan.

## Step 3: Plan writing and session choice
- Filling in the day-by-day, week-by-week details is the last step, not the first. Most people want to start with the details, then lose the big picture, which can seriously derail their efforts.
- Work from your schedule first and choose sessions afterwards, so you know what you can realistically fit in. Picking sessions first because they look cool online is fine for experimenting but not for planning.
- This step takes practice and patience: don't expect a perfect plan in an afternoon; keep adjusting your plans over the years as you learn more about yourself. Planning, like training, is a process you get better at over time.

### Set priorities from the "after" picture
- Use the "after" picture (goal route or type of climber) to set training priorities. Example goal: steep, a short crux sequence, very crimpy, lots of body tension.
- Judge strengths and weaknesses from the metrics you measured, from friends' views of what you are good and bad at, or from problems and routes you have already climbed. Example: good at body tension and steep climbing; powers out on short hard sequences; struggles on small holds. It doesn't need to be accurate or detailed at this stage.

### Decide how many sessions you have
- Decide the number of sessions before making the plan: the maximum time available and what is realistic for your motivation. Doing structured climbing at every opportunity leads to burnout and lost motivation after several weeks or months.

| Session type | Example per week |
|---|---|
| Open session (outdoors, or relaxed projecting indoors with friends) | At least 1 |
| Structured climbing sessions | 3 |
| Extra conditioning sessions (can be combined with the climbing sessions) | 2 |
| Total | This is the maximum |

### Fit sessions to facilities, energy and priorities
- Plan sessions around your facilities.
- Adjust how much training goes into each session to your recovery time and life demands, not just the number of sessions.
- Fill in the big picture of each session before the exact details; then choose the details from the priorities (short sequences, finger strength, crimpy climbing): fingerboarding for finger strength, and board climbing.

| Session | Facilities | Time and energy | Big picture | Details |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Open: outdoors (if the weather allows) or fun | A priority and big motivator: rest day before it for good skin | Stays open | Stays open |
| 2 | Climbing wall: climbing and conditioning together | The big day with lots of focus and energy: rest day before it; train with a partner (work around their schedule) | Long day; priority is crimp strength and short sequences. Finger strength (fingery climbing or fingerboarding, chosen later), then strength-based climbing with more volume on slightly more finger-friendly holds that work the body hard, then full-body conditioning with weights, with a partner for motivation | Fingery climbing on a board, then mileage bouldering on bigger holds for full-body movement and body tension (still needed for the goal), then full-body weights |
| 3, morning | Climbing wall, before work | Plenty of energy, but not too much volume or duration because a big work day follows | Very easy aerobic capacity (endurance) work; little interference with the strength priority because it is on a separate day (though the two can be combined); it helps through a desk-based work day | Easy "one on, one off" endurance work for base endurance and active recovery |
| 3, evening | Conditioning at home (less equipment) | Low in intensity or volume because you will be tired | Home conditioning: a pulling exercise and flexibility; no big core sessions at home if you find them demotivating | Pull-ups and stretching at home: simple, unlikely to affect the weekend open session, and a useful stimulus because the goal route requires it |
| 4 | Home board and fingerboard: climbing and conditioning there | Short, high energy and intense, but not much volume because of the board angle and facilities | Finger strength and strength, climbing-based or a mix of fingerboarding and climbing | Fingerboarding for finger strength and board climbing |

### Lay out the week
- Place the open session first (probably at the weekend), then build the 7 days around it:

| Order | Day |
|---|---|
| 1 | Open session (session 1) |
| 2 | Rest (tiredness and sore skin) |
| 3 | Intense session at home in the evening (session 4); alternatively the big partner session could go here (personal preference) |
| 4 | Rest |
| 5 | Big training day mid-week (session 2), rested and recovered, with the partner |
| 6 | Split session (session 3): easy, active recovery, very low intensity in the morning; low-volume conditioning in the evening |
| 7 | Rest before the open session (a prerequisite) |
